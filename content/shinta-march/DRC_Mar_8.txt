*Seth Kikuni Rejects Dialogue Without Preconditions
KINSHASA February 26, 2026 — In exile since mid-January, opposition leader Seth Kikuni stressed that any national dialogue in the Democratic Republic of Congo cannot be credible in a context of repression. Responding to Martin Fayulu, who had proposed topics for discussion, Kikuni called for clear prerequisites, including a ceasefire and the cessation of all forms of political repression.
“The opposition will not engage in dialogue unless it is based on clear, credible, and verifiable preconditions,” wrote Kikuni on X, emphasizing that dialogue in a context of injustice, intimidation, and deprivation of fundamental rights risks legitimizing the status quo.
Among Kikuni’s preconditions for an inclusive dialogue are:
Release of political prisoners and prisoners of conscience
Lifting of politically motivated convictions
Restoration of civil and political rights, including the return of passports and freedom of movement for political actors
Annulment of politically motivated legal proceedings and lifting of arrest warrants
Reparations for damages suffered
Martin Fayulu, meanwhile, has called for a dialogue giving voice to all segments of society, including armed groups, outlining seven essential discussion points:
Preservation of DRC sovereignty and army/security reforms
Promotion of ethical governance, public finance reform, and anti-corruption measures
Strengthening justice, human rights, and equality before the law
Electoral reforms to ensure transparency for the 2028 elections
Peaceful coexistence among Congolese ethnic groups
Regional stability in the Great Lakes region
Review and analysis of past national dialogue resolutions since 1958 to develop a coherent governance doctrine
Kikuni and other opposition leaders insist that participation criteria must ensure tangible benefits for the Congolese people, rejecting any dialogue reduced to a mere political exercise.
*DRC Nominates Juliana Lumumba for Secretary General of La Francophonie
KINSHASA February 27, 2026 — The Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) has officially nominated Juliana Amato Lumumba, daughter of independence hero Patrice Emery Lumumba, as its candidate for Secretary General of the International Organisation of La Francophonie (OIF).
A political science graduate from Paris and a prominent figure in Congolese public life, Lumumba brings over 30 years of experience in governance, cultural diplomacy, and international cooperation. The selection was reinforced by recent diplomatic engagement with France, including the February 2 visit of French Minister Delegate for Francophonie Éléonore Caroit to Kinshasa and the February 25 meeting in Paris between Presidents Emmanuel Macron and Félix Tshisekedi, emphasizing the DRC’s strategic role in the future of the Francophone space in Africa.

*Paul Kagame Denounces Selective Pressure, Calls for Equal Accountability of All Parties
KIGALI, Rwanda March 6, 2026 — Rwandan President Paul Kagame has rejected what he describes as selective international pressure on Rwanda over its alleged support for the AFC/M23 rebellion in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), calling instead for equal scrutiny of all parties to the conflict.
Speaking at a dinner with ambassadors, diplomats, and representatives of international organizations accredited to Kigali, Kagame emphasized that the crisis in eastern DRC is long-standing and rooted in structural issues, including the persistent threat posed by the Forces Démocratiques de Libération du Rwanda (FDLR). He criticized Kinshasa’s view that the FDLR is a residual force, arguing the group continues to pose a significant threat to Rwanda and spreads its ideology to other armed factions in the region.
Kagame highlighted what he sees as a double standard in international expectations: while the Washington Agreements call for a ceasefire and security measures by all parties, he asserts that only Rwanda faces punitive measures and sanctions. He cited military operations and troop buildups by DRC and allied forces in eastern and western DRC, arguing that these actions undermine the ceasefire and shift the burden unfairly onto Rwanda.
“As expected, responsibility has been placed exclusively on Rwanda. This selective pressure has only encouraged the Congolese government and affiliated elements to resume military operations. We are asking for the same rigor to be demanded from all parties,” Kagame said.
The Rwandan President’s remarks come amid U.S. sanctions and visa restrictions targeting Rwandan officials accused of supporting the M23 rebellion. The U.S. State Department insists that all parties, including the DRC, must honor the Washington Agreements, including the neutralization of the FDLR and withdrawal of Rwandan forces from Congolese territory.
Kagame stressed that Rwanda’s actions are driven by legitimate security concerns, defending the country’s right to protect its borders from the continued presence of armed groups and their genocidal ideology. “The choice is clear: tolerate the FDLR and its growing network of militias advancing toward our border, or defend ourselves and bear the consequences. Any other country in our position would do the same,” he concluded.
This statement underscores ongoing tensions between Kinshasa and Kigali, as diplomatic pressure intensifies amid continued clashes in North Kivu and South Kivu provinces, prolonging instability in the Great Lakes region.

*Government Acknowledges Delays in Civil Servants’ Salaries, Promises Normalization Within a Month
KINSHASA March 6, 2026 — The Democratic Republic of Congo government has acknowledged delays in salary payments for civil servants, citing strain on public finances, and pledged a return to regular payments within the month.
At a meeting on March 5, the government, led by Prime Minister Judith Suminwa, and the Economic Situation Committee (CCE) reviewed public finances, monetary dynamics, market prices, and the potential effects of the Middle East crisis on the Congolese economy. The government, however, rejects claims of liquidity problems, maintaining that sufficient resources are available.
The International Monetary Fund (IMF) has also flagged the growing public sector wage bill as a fiscal risk, particularly in education, health, and justice, where recent salary adjustments, recruitment, and administrative regularizations have strained the budget.
President Tshisekedi previously instructed a comprehensive payroll audit, with results expected within 30 days, aimed at ensuring sustainable public finances and regular, reliable salary payments for civil servants.
This move comes as the DRC faces increased expenditures related to security operations, including responses to the AFC/M23 rebellion in North and South Kivu, highlighting the delicate balance between fiscal management and social stability.
*Kinshasa Holds 65.2% of Bank Deposits, Central Bank Reports
KINSHASA February 28, 2026 — Bank deposits in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) remain heavily concentrated in the capital, Kinshasa, accounting for 65.2% of total deposits, according to the latest economic outlook from the Central Bank of Congo (BCC).
The report, covering economic developments from February 13 to 20, 2026, shows that after Kinshasa, deposits are primarily held in Haut-Katanga (22.9%), Lualaba (4.0%), and North Kivu (2.7%). Total bank deposits reached $16.24 billion at the end of December 2025, marking a 3.4% increase from the previous month and a 10.2% year-on-year rise, largely driven by dollar-denominated deposits from households and private enterprises.
Households represent 35.8% of total deposits, while private businesses account for 31.8%. Sectorally, deposits come mostly from sectors outside public administration (39.2%), trade (21.7%), and extractive industries (14.5%).
Meanwhile, gross loans totaled $10.27 billion, up 2.5% month-on-month and 20.2% year-on-year, primarily due to increased lending to SMEs and the central government. Loans in foreign currency rose 2.5%, while local currency loans fell 1.7%.
The BCC notes that the formal financial system comprises 287 institutions, including 108 credit institutions, 137 financial companies, 39 insurance companies, two social security funds, and one guarantee fund. The banking sector includes 14 active banks with 445 service points (371 branches and 74 ATMs), representing 97% of financial sector assets.
The microfinance sector has 101 institutions, including 78 savings and credit cooperatives, 15 microfinance companies, and 8 microcredit enterprises, with 186 operating points representing roughly 3% of sector assets. Nearly 70% of microfinance institutions are concentrated in Kinshasa, North Kivu, and South Kivu.
The report underscores the continued dominance of Kinshasa in the country’s financial landscape, highlighting challenges for financial inclusion in other provinces.

*UN Proposes Initiatives to Strengthen Governance and Combat Illicit Flows
Critical Minerals March 7, 2026 — At the UN Security Council meeting on Energy, Critical Minerals, and Security, the growing importance of critical minerals in the global economy was highlighted. Minerals such as lithium, cobalt, and nickel are now essential for digital technologies and the energy transition, with trade in raw and semi-processed minerals reaching $2.5 trillion in 2023. Demand is projected to triple by 2030 and quadruple by 2040, creating opportunities for job creation, economic diversification, and sustainable development.
The UN outlined three key priorities to ensure conflict-affected countries benefit responsibly from their natural resources. First, exploitation must lead to fair and equitable outcomes, strengthening national institutions, regulatory frameworks, and processing capacities, while mitigating negative impacts. Second, multilateral and regional action is needed to improve traceability, combat illicit flows, and build resilient supply chains. Third, mediation tools must be deployed to address conflicts related to natural resources, fostering cooperative relationships and enabling parties to share benefits equitably.
The session comes amid ongoing conflict in eastern DRC, where the AFC/M23 rebellion occupies areas in North and South Kivu. The Congolese government frames these attacks as part of an economic war aimed at plundering critical minerals, while providing military support to rebels. Rwanda denies involvement, citing the threat of the FDLR, a group with a genocidal ideology, and framing the situation as linked to Tutsi community security concerns.
Efforts to resolve tensions include the Washington Process, addressing relations between Kinshasa and Kigali, and the Doha Process, focused on the AFC/M23 crisis. Despite these initiatives, hostilities persist, endangering civilian populations and worsening humanitarian conditions amid declining international aid.
The UN emphasized that equitable and responsible management of critical minerals could lift millions out of poverty, promote peace, and support sustainable development, but only if governance, traceability, and diplomacy are effectively enforced.

The ceremony followed a workshop organized on March 6 by the Ministry of Gender, in collaboration with the Friedrich Ebert Foundation (FES), to validate the implementing decree of Law No. 15/013 of 2015 on women's rights and gender parity.
The initiative seeks to strengthen inclusive governance, promote networking among women, and enhance female leadership. The signing was attended by the Ambassador of Germany to the DRC, Ingo Herbert, and representatives of Congolese women’s associations.
Minister of Gender, Chantal Ombae, highlighted the significance of the agreement: “This paradigm shift means moving from symbolic representation to women’s decision-making participation; from political discourse to the structural implementation of parity. Together with civil society, we are building concrete mechanisms to strengthen governance and accelerate the achievement of SDG 5.”
She added that the partnership complements government efforts to build a more just and egalitarian society, amplifying the voices of women and children, “not as an option, but as a necessity for progress.”
During the ceremony, Minister Ombae also inaugurated the headquarters of the Friedrich Ebert Foundation, emphasizing its role as a space for dialogue, cooperation, and empowerment.
The Foundation’s representative, Constantin Grund, said: “By inaugurating this building on the eve of International Women's Day, we are sending a symbolic message: spaces for dialogue must be open to everyone, particularly women. We hope this will be a place for debates, inspiration, and partnerships that promote justice, equality, and democracy.”

*DRC Inducts 2,500 New Magistrates to Strengthen Rule of Law
Kinshasa, February 23, 2026 – The Democratic Republic of Congo formally inducted 2,500 new magistrates into the national judiciary on Saturday, February 21, 2026, in a ceremony held at the Congress Hall of the People's Palace in Kinshasa. The event marked the conclusion of an intensive three-month training program aimed at enhancing the efficiency, ethics, competence, and professional conduct of magistrates before their deployment across the country.
The Minister of Justice and Keeper of the Seals, Guillaume Ngefa, emphasized that this initiative represents a crucial step in strengthening the rule of law. “A magistrate is neither a tool of the powerful, nor a hostage to emotion, nor a prisoner of personal convictions. They are an impartial servant of the law, bound by strict neutrality and an equal distance from all parties,” he declared.
This graduating class completes the initial cohort of magistrates, already in office since 2023, all graduates of the national competitive examination held in October 2022 and dubbed the “Rule of Law” class.
During his address, Minister Ngefa reminded the new magistrates of the weight of their responsibilities: “In the exercise of your duties, you will be called upon to rule on matters of liberty, rights, property, and the honor of those seeking justice. These powers are delegated to you by the nation, within the limits set by the Constitution and the laws of the Republic.”
He stressed that integrity and probity are not merely personal virtues, but fundamental prerequisites for public trust in the justice system. Independence, he added, is not a privilege but an institutional guarantee that protects citizens against arbitrary power.
“You will face pressure, attempts at influence, and even threats, but these challenges cannot justify abandoning the principles upon which your oath is based. Fighting corruption, influence peddling, and unjustified delays is now both a professional obligation and a requirement for institutional performance,” Minister Ngefa stated.
The Minister also highlighted the need for digital transformation, streamlined procedures, expedited case processing, and transparent decision-making as essential elements of modern judicial governance. “Magistrates must be not only legal practitioners but also agents of transformation, demonstrating methodological rigor, quality decisions, and strict adherence to legal deadlines,” he said.
The deployment of these 2,500 magistrates is expected to address pressing challenges in multiple jurisdictions, including in field operations. Minister Ngefa reaffirmed the government’s commitment to supporting the judiciary with the necessary material and organizational resources to ensure effective performance.
The DRC Constitution of February 18, 2006, guarantees the independence of the judiciary from the legislative and executive branches, reinforcing the separation of powers as a cornerstone of democracy. The High Council of the Judiciary, responsible for managing appointments, promotions, disciplinary measures, and advisory functions, ensures the proper implementation of these principles.
“The mandate is clear: build a justice system worthy of the nation, protective of fundamental rights, firm against violations, and responsive to the legitimate expectations of the people,” concluded Minister Ngefa.

The reopening sparked relief and renewed hope among residents and traders who depend on the crossing for their livelihoods and daily commerce.
For many small-scale traders, the closure had severely disrupted cross-border economic activities, particularly trade between Uvira and the Burundian capital, Bujumbura.
With the border now open again, traders and travelers began resuming their movements, signaling a gradual return to normal economic life in the region.
Strategic Border for Regional Trade
The Gatumba–Kavimvira crossing is one of the most important border points between the DRC and Burundi, facilitating the movement of goods, traders, and travelers every day.
Its reopening is expected to revive cross-border trade, which plays a crucial role in supporting local economies on both sides of the frontier.
Authorities hope that the reopening will contribute to easing tensions and restoring economic stability, although the security situation in eastern DRC remains fragile amid ongoing armed conflicts in the region.

*MONUSCO and ICGLR Deploy Joint Assessment Mission to Uvira to Support Ceasefire Monitoring
UVIRA, South Kivu February 24, 2026 — The United Nations Organization Stabilization Mission in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (MONUSCO) and the International Conference on the Great Lakes Region (ICGLR) have launched a joint exploratory and preliminary assessment mission in Uvira, South Kivu, as part of efforts to support the implementation and monitoring of the ceasefire in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo.
The mission, scheduled to run from February 23 to 27, 2026, follows consultations with President Félix Tshisekedi, who currently serves as Chair of the ICGLR. According to a joint statement released Tuesday, the deployment represents a crucial preparatory step toward the future establishment of a joint ceasefire monitoring mechanism within the framework of the broader regional ceasefire architecture.
The primary objective of the mission is operational and technical, focusing on ensuring that future monitoring and verification mechanisms are credible, secure, and operationally feasible. Teams on the ground will assess accessibility, security conditions, logistical requirements, and operational needs necessary for the effective functioning of the monitoring system.
During the mission, the joint delegation will conduct a comprehensive evaluation of the political, security, logistical, and socio-environmental environment in and around Uvira, with the goal of facilitating coherent planning for the future deployment of ceasefire monitoring structures.
Officials emphasized that effective monitoring of the ceasefire is essential to reducing violence and creating the conditions necessary for a lasting political solution in the region.
The acting head of MONUSCO, Vivian van de Perre, noted that the mission’s mandate in South Kivu differs from its operations in North Kivu and Ituri, explaining that MONUSCO’s role in the province is focused primarily on monitoring the ceasefire rather than conducting Protection of Civilians operations, in accordance with United Nations Security Council Resolution 2808 (2025).
She stressed that the current deployment represents an exploratory phase, and that the scope and configuration of any future operations will depend on agreed parameters, security guarantees, and operational requirements.
For his part, ICGLR Executive Secretary Dr. Mubita Luwabelwa highlighted the importance of regional cooperation, stating that the Great Lakes region possesses significant potential for peace and prosperity if states work together to address shared security challenges.
Both organizations underscored that credible ceasefire monitoring requires favorable operational conditions, including freedom of movement for monitoring teams and reliable security guarantees, which remain key aspects of the ongoing assessment.
Under UN Security Council Resolution 2808 (2025), MONUSCO is authorized to support the implementation of a permanent ceasefire in the Democratic Republic of Congo. This support includes assistance to the Ceasefire Monitoring and Verification Mechanism as well as the Expanded Joint Verification Mechanism Plus (EJVM+), established following the signing of the Doha Global Peace Framework on November 15, 2025.
The deployment comes amid renewed diplomatic and peacekeeping efforts aimed at stabilizing eastern DRC. It also follows a recent field visit to the region by MONUSCO leadership, during which discussions were held with AFC/M23 representatives, as well as a visit to Kinshasa by United Nations Under-Secretary-General for Peace Operations Jean-Pierre Lacroix, who reaffirmed the UN’s commitment to implementing MONUSCO’s updated mandate and supporting peace efforts in the region.

Conclusion
The period from February 22 to March 8, 2026, in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) was marked by a complex interplay of persistent conflict, diplomatic tension, and internal governance reforms. While the country demonstrated significant international initiative—notably through the nomination of Juliana Lumumba for the OIF leadership and active participation in global mining forums—the security situation in the East remains its most pressing challenge.
The escalating conflict with the Rwandan-backed AFC/M23 rebellion continues to destabilize North and South Kivu, resulting in mass displacements, humanitarian crises, and the discovery of mass graves in recently liberated areas such as Uvira. Despite international efforts under the Washington and Doha frameworks, diplomatic friction between Kinshasa and Kigali remains high, with both sides exchanging accusations regarding support for armed groups and the legitimacy of security concerns.
Domestically, the government is navigating a delicate political landscape, evidenced by internal divisions within the ruling coalition over constitutional reforms and growing opposition demands for a more inclusive and transparent national dialogue. At the same time, the presidency’s emphasis on government performance reviews and addressing fiscal pressures, including civil service salary delays, highlights a commitment to administrative efficiency and accountability.
In parallel, the DRC is making progress in areas such as agricultural transformation, industrial partnerships, and gender equality institutionalization. However, these gains remain fragile, and achieving lasting stability will require not only the effective implementation of regional peace agreements, but also a sustained effort to strengthen governance, ensure mineral traceability, and address the urgent health and humanitarian needs of the population.
In conclusion, while the DRC demonstrates resilience and strategic initiative, its future stability hinges on balancing security, diplomacy, and internal reforms to create a more peaceful, inclusive, and prosperous nation.
