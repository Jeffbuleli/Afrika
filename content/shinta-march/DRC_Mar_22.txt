*EAC Calls on AU to Strengthen Mediation Efforts in Eastern DRC
Arusha, March 9, 2026 — The East African Community (EAC) has urged the African Union (AU) to mobilize adequate financial and logistical resources to support ongoing mediation efforts aimed at resolving the escalating crisis in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo.
The appeal follows the 25th Summit of Heads of State held on March 7 in Arusha, where regional leaders reviewed the deteriorating security and humanitarian situation marked by rising tensions between Democratic Republic of Congo and Rwanda, as well as continued clashes involving the AFC/M23 rebellion.
In its final communiqué, the Summit emphasized the urgency of reinforcing the AU-led mediation framework. “The Summit urged the African Union to provide the necessary financial and logistical resources to the African Union-led mediation process in eastern DRC to ensure a swift and sustained commitment to its conclusion,” the statement read.
The mediation process, aligned with parallel diplomatic tracks in Washington and Doha, is led by Faure Essozimna Gnassingbé, who serves as the AU’s designated mediator for the eastern DRC crisis. The current structure stems from a high-level meeting convened in January 2026 in Lomé, aimed at harmonizing peace initiatives.
Under this framework, Gnassingbé is supported by a Togolese mediation team drawn from the presidency and foreign affairs ministry. Five co-facilitators—former African heads of state—have been assigned key thematic roles: Olusegun Obasanjo oversees military and security issues, Sahle-Work Zewde handles humanitarian concerns, Uhuru Kenyatta leads dialogue with armed groups, Mokgweetsi Masisi manages regional economic cooperation, and Catherine Samba-Panza is responsible for civil society, reconciliation, and women’s issues.
A joint secretariat—bringing together the AU, EAC, Southern African Development Community (SADC), and International Conference on the Great Lakes Region (ICGLR)—has also been established to coordinate efforts, alongside international partners including the United Nations, the European Union, and Qatar.
Despite intensified diplomatic engagement, progress on the ground remains limited. The Washington track, backed by Donald Trump and endorsed by Félix Tshisekedi and Paul Kagame, has yet to translate into tangible security improvements. Mutual accusations between Kinshasa and Kigali over alleged violations continue to undermine trust and stability.
Meanwhile, the Doha negotiations—facilitated by Qatar between the Congolese government and the AFC/M23 rebellion—have slowed significantly. Key provisions, including the ceasefire mechanism, declaration of principles, and framework agreement, remain unimplemented, stalling efforts to address root causes of the conflict such as state authority and armed group reintegration.
This diplomatic impasse has contributed to renewed violent clashes in eastern DRC, further complicating an already fragile humanitarian situation. Calls for a national dialogue to complement international mediation efforts have also gone unanswered. President Tshisekedi maintains that such an initiative must be led exclusively by his office, despite mounting pressure from socio-political actors and recent proposals from religious groups.
As mediation efforts struggle to gain momentum, regional leaders warn that without stronger institutional backing and coordinated implementation, prospects for lasting peace in eastern DRC remain uncertain.
*Oakland Institute Criticizes Washington Agreement, Calls for Stronger Action Against Rwanda
DRC March 13, 2026 — The Oakland Institute has sharply criticized the Washington Agreement, arguing that it contains “inherent contradictions” by allegedly rewarding Rwanda despite its continued involvement in the conflict in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo.
In a statement released on March 12, the organization called on the United States administration to take stronger measures to end what it describes as Rwandan aggression through the AFC/M23 rebellion, which it says fuels instability and illicit exploitation of natural resources at the expense of Congolese civilians.
The think tank also deemed U.S. sanctions imposed by the Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) on the Rwandan Defence Forces (RDF) and several senior officials as insufficient, arguing they fail to counter the financial gains derived from the conflict.
According to the report, the RDF has played a decisive role in supporting the M23 rebellion, including in the capture of key areas such as Goma and Bukavu, as well as strategic mining sites. The organization highlights that control over resources—particularly coltan and gold—has enabled the rebels to generate significant revenues, including an estimated $800,000 per month from coltan taxation and up to $2 billion in gold exports in 2025.
The Oakland Institute further notes that Rwanda continues to receive substantial international financial support, including U.S. bilateral aid and major funding from the World Bank, raising concerns about policy contradictions. It points to infrastructure contracts allegedly linked to entities associated with the RDF, suggesting a nexus between military influence and economic interests.
Frédéric Mousseau, policy director at the institute, argued that Rwanda’s actions undermine the credibility of the Washington Agreement. “Rwanda’s violations and continued presence in the DRC highlight the contradictions of a so-called peace agreement that effectively grants access to Congolese resources while failing to ensure accountability,” he said, warning that such an approach cannot lead to lasting peace.
The report echoes earlier findings published in the institute’s study “Shafted: The Race for Critical Minerals in the DRC,” which cautioned that diplomatic initiatives may be used to advance resource interests under the guise of peacebuilding.
Diverging Positions
In response to the sanctions, Rwanda rejected the measures as “unilateral” and “distorting the facts”, arguing they unfairly target one side of the conflict. Kigali accused Kinshasa of violating ceasefire agreements through drone strikes and military offensives, and reiterated concerns over the presence of armed groups, including the FDLR, alongside Congolese forces.
On its part, the Congolese government welcomed the sanctions as a “clear signal” in support of sovereignty and territorial integrity, stressing the need for consistency between diplomatic commitments and realities on the ground. Authorities in Kinshasa maintain that ending support for armed groups and respecting international law are essential to stabilizing eastern DRC.
Fragile Diplomatic Progress
Despite ongoing diplomatic efforts, including the Washington and Doha processes supported by the African Union, progress remains limited on the ground. Persistent tensions between Kinshasa and Kigali, combined with renewed clashes in North and South Kivu, continue to undermine hopes for a sustainable resolution to the conflict.
The Oakland Institute’s critique adds to growing international debate over whether current diplomatic frameworks can effectively address the root causes of the conflict or risk entrenching existing power dynamics in the region.

*DRC Launches “Country Window” Project to Boost Geoscience and Mining Sector Modernization
Kinshasa, March 20, 2026 – The Democratic Republic of Congo officially launched the “Country Window DRC” project on Thursday, March 19, a major initiative under the PanAfGeo+ program, aimed at modernizing the mining sector and strengthening geoscience cooperation.
The launch, presided over by Minister of Mines Louis Watum Kabamba, took place in Kinshasa and formalized collaboration with the European Union. The project is coordinated by the French Geological Survey (BRGM) and supports the National Geological Survey of Congo (SGN-C) in enhancing its technical and scientific capacities.
“The Country Window DRC project represents a significant opportunity to improve our understanding of the subsoil, strengthen the capacities of our institutions, and reduce the risks associated with mining investment. Geoscientific data is a strategic decision-making tool,” said Minister Kabamba.
The initiative aims to modernize the acquisition, management, and use of geological data, providing tools to guide investment decisions, improve mining governance, and promote responsible and transparent exploitation of natural resources. Kabamba emphasized that the project is part of the DRC’s broader strategy to transition from a raw materials export model to one focused on local value creation, sustainability, and transparency.
EU Support and Strategic Cooperation
Fabrice Basile, acting head of the EU delegation in the DRC, hailed the project as a concrete milestone in the 2023 strategic partnership roadmap between the EU and the DRC. He outlined the project’s four main areas of focus:
Modernizing geoscientific data management and consolidating the national database.
Mapping and assessing resources in priority areas, including artisanal mining sites.
Supporting artisanal mining with innovative technologies for decarbonization and waste recycling.
Strengthening human and technical capacities in public institutions, including ESG training.
“Our ambition is clear: to have a reliable national geoscience database, integrate artisanal mining communities into sustainable value chains, and train a new generation of Congolese geoscientists,” Basile said.
Reducing Investment Risks
The project is also designed to reduce risks for investors. Jean-Claude Guillaneau, PanAfGeo+ coordinator, explained that the program builds on the previous AfricaMaVal phases, which trained 1,773 experts across Africa. PanAfGeo+ Invest aims to expand the scope, focusing on critical minerals, water resources, and fostering a pan-African network of geoscience experts.
Project Scope and Objectives
Running from 2026 to 2029, the PanAfGeo+ Country Window DRC targets:
Geological and mining reconnaissance in Tanganyika, Kasai Central, and Kongo Central.
Feasibility studies for artisanal mining in Lualaba and Haut-Katanga.
Digitization of geological archives in the DRC and abroad.
Mapping artisanal mining nationwide and promoting sustainable, low-carbon practices.
Strengthening human capital through training programs and technical equipment provision.
The project involves multiple partners, including CTCPM, SAEMAPE, CAMI, and CRGM, and represents a significant step toward enhancing state sovereignty over geoscientific data while promoting sustainable investment and governance in the mining sector.
“Improving our knowledge of the geological potential is essential to ensure responsible, transparent, and sustainable exploitation of mineral resources, contributing to national economic and social development,” concluded Minister Kabamba.

*Middle East Conflict Paralyzes Gold Trade in North Kivu
Butembo, North Kivu – March 12, 2026 – The war between the US-Israeli coalition and Iran is disrupting North Kivu’s gold market, leaving traders and miners struggling. Butembo, the main trading hub for artisanal gold from North Kivu and neighboring provinces, has seen a sharp slowdown in operations as Dubai—the key destination for the region’s gold—reduces its purchases.
The crisis has left local gold buying offices short on cash and dependent on credit. Merchants who usually travel to Dubai to purchase goods have suspended their trips, further tightening the flow of funds in the region.

The disruption highlights the vulnerability of North Kivu’s gold trade, which depends heavily on international markets, and shows how geopolitical crises abroad can have immediate, devastating effects on local economies.
*Landslide at Safi Mine in Lualaba Kills at Least 10 Artisanal Miners
Kakanda, Lualaba March 13, 2026 – At least ten artisanal miners have died and one person injured following a landslide at the Safi mining site near Kakanda in Lualaba’s Lubudi territory. Rescue teams also recovered the body of a child, approximately 13 years old, from the debris.
Authorities have recommended the immediate suspension of mining activities in the affected area and a technical assessment of the unstable quarry sections. The landslide occurred in a pit where miners were working early in the morning, exposing unsafe mining conditions.
A technical review identified several factors contributing to the disaster, including excessively high slabs of up to 37 meters (compared to the recommended five meters), fragile sandy-clay soil, dangerously steep slopes reaching 70–80 degrees, and the absence of proper drainage. These conditions indicate a lack of proper oversight in the artisanal operations.
The Safi site, part of the Boss Mining company’s permit, allows artisanal miners to operate in specific areas under strict agreements with local cooperatives. However, the cooperative involved had not received formal approval for technical operations, raising questions about the legality of the work conducted.
Officials have also called for the withdrawal of military personnel overseeing miners, suggesting that the Mines and Hydrocarbons Police take over supervision. The affected area remains closed while safety assessments and security measures are implemented.
The tragedy highlights ongoing safety challenges in unregulated artisanal mining and the urgent need for stronger oversight and compliance with mining standards.

*Two Years After Amendment 5: Progress on Infrastructure Amid Persistent Criticism
Kinshasa March 18, 2026 – Two years after the renegotiation of the Sicomines agreement between the Democratic Republic of Congo and a consortium of Chinese companies, progress on infrastructure is visible, but criticism from civil society and experts remains. The Congolese government recently announced a technical and financial audit of the project, renewing debate over whether the nation is truly benefiting from its mineral wealth.
A Renegotiation Presented as a Turning Point
Signed in March 2024, Amendment 5 to the original 2008 Sicomines agreement aimed to correct long-standing imbalances. The agreement included increased infrastructure investment, greater involvement of Gécamines in marketing mining production, and a more structured institutional framework for energy projects such as the Busanga Dam.
Visible Infrastructure Gains
Some improvements are evident on the ground. Along National Route 1, between Nguba and Lubudi, formerly muddy roads have been paved, easing travel and trade. Four bridges are under construction on this 142-kilometer section, part of an 850-kilometer road network planned to connect Nguba to Mbuji-Mayi by 2027. Drivers and local traders note positive effects on transportation and commerce.
Persistent Concerns and Discrepancies
Despite these visible gains, many projects remain incomplete, and the cost of some works appears inflated. Reports highlight discrepancies such as Boulevard du 30 Juin in Kinshasa, billed at $142 million despite estimates of $28 million, and Triomphal Boulevard, billed at $61 million versus an actual $5 million.
Financial Mechanisms Under Scrutiny
Analysts also question the financial structure of Amendment 5. The fixed annual contribution formula limits the DRC’s benefits if copper prices rise while exposing the state to losses if prices fall. Critics argue this mechanism diminishes the potential gains from the country’s mineral wealth.
Electricity and Tax Exemptions
The Busanga Dam, a key component of the partnership, primarily powers mining facilities, with minimal electricity directed to households. Tax exemptions granted to Sicomines are estimated to have cost the state hundreds of millions of dollars annually, with projections suggesting losses could reach $7 billion over the remaining life of the project.
Audit Could Clarify Accountability
The government’s announced audit aims to clarify resource allocation, project costs, and financial mechanisms. Experts hope the process will identify irregularities and ensure the partnership delivers tangible benefits to Congolese citizens.
Public Debate Continues
Two years after Amendment 5, the question remains: who truly benefits from the Sicomines partnership? While some infrastructure improvements are apparent, civil society, analysts, and local stakeholders continue to call for renegotiation and greater accountability to ensure the DRC maximizes the value of its mineral resources.

*Former Energy Minister and Renowned Jurist Jeannot Matadi Nenga Ngamanda Dies at 76
Kinshasa March 22, 2026 – The Democratic Republic of Congo mourns the loss of Jeannot Matadi Nenga Ngamanda, former Minister of Energy and Water Resources, who passed away on March 22 at the age of 76.
Born on September 14, 1949, in Kifumu, Ngamanda was a towering figure in both the Congolese legal and political spheres. He earned a doctorate in law from the University of Paris X-Nanterre, a law degree from the National University of Zaire, and a diploma in human rights from Columbia University, New York. He also held a bachelor’s degree in philosophy and literature from Lovanium University in Kinshasa.
A practicing lawyer since 1975, Ngamanda appeared before the Court of Cassation and the Council of State throughout his career. He was a member of the Standing Commission for the Reform of Congolese Law and held leadership positions within the legal profession, including President of the Kinshasa Bar Association, President of the Young Lawyers Association of Zaire, and Vice-President of the International Association of Young Lawyers.
In politics, he served as Minister of Energy and Water Resources under the Matata II government, was a member of parliament, and held the position of Second Vice-President of the National Assembly during the transitional period.
Ngamanda was also a prolific author, publishing several influential works on law and governance, including Private Judicial Law (2006), The Right to a Fair Trial (2002), The Question of Judicial Power in the Democratic Republic of Congo (2001), and Zaire: An Improbable Democracy (1997).
His passing marks the end of a distinguished career dedicated to law, justice, and public service, leaving a lasting legacy in Congolese legal and political life.

*ICRC Ready to Support Transfer of Over 5,000 Released FARDC Soldiers to Kinshasa
Eastern DRC March 9, 2026 — The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) has announced its readiness to facilitate the transfer of more than 5,000 Congolese soldiers released by the AFC/M23 rebellion in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo.
The soldiers, members of the FARDC (Armed Forces of the DRC), were handed over on March 2 at the Rumangabo military camp in Rutshuru Territory, according to a statement issued by the AFC/M23. The group indicated that the troops, captured on various frontlines, would be transferred to Kinshasa under ICRC supervision.
“The AFC/M23 informs the public that it has handed over more than 5,000 FARDC members to the ICRC. They will be repatriated to Kinshasa under its supervision,” the statement said.
Responding to the announcement, François Moreillon, head of the ICRC delegation in Kinshasa, confirmed the organization’s position. “As a neutral intermediary, the ICRC stands ready to assist with such an operation, with the agreement of all parties,” he stated.
This large-scale prisoner transfer forms part of confidence-building measures agreed upon within the Doha mediation process, aimed at easing tensions between Kinshasa and the rebellion. The AFC/M23 says the release reflects its commitment to international humanitarian law and efforts to de-escalate the conflict.
The ICRC has previously played a similar role. In May 2025, it facilitated the transfer of more than 1,300 disarmed soldiers from Goma to Kinshasa.
However, despite this development, tensions remain high. The AFC/M23, often described as Rwandan-backed rebels, accuses the government of Kinshasa of refusing to release detained fighters, including individuals allegedly arrested “simply because of their appearance.”
The group further alleges ongoing ceasefire violations, claiming that government forces continue military operations targeting civilian populations and property, accusations that risk undermining fragile peace efforts in eastern DRC.
While the planned transfer could mark a significant step toward de-escalation, its success will depend on cooperation between all parties and sustained commitment to the broader peace process.

*Drone Strikes Hit Goma, International Reactions Pour In
Goma DRC March 11, 2026 — The city of Goma, capital of North Kivu Province and currently under AFC/M23 rebel control, was struck by drone attacks early Wednesday morning. The strikes, attributed by the AFC/M23 rebellion to the Armed Forces of the Democratic Republic of Congo (FARDC), caused material damage and at least three deaths, including a French UNICEF aid worker, as well as two other civilians.
The Himbi neighborhood, near Lake Kivu, was particularly affected, with homes and civilian infrastructure damaged. The city has been controlled by the AFC/M23 since January 2025 and serves as the rebellion’s operational headquarters in North and South Kivu provinces.
Rebel Response
Bertrand Bisimwa, deputy coordinator of AFC/M23, condemned the strikes, accusing the Kinshasa government of violating the ceasefire and deploring the silence of international partners. He described the attacks as part of a campaign to “decapitate the revolution” and force an escalation of regional conflict.
Corneille Nangaa, political coordinator of AFC/M23, specified that the strikes were allegedly targeting the leadership of the rebellion and key political figures, including General Sultani Makenga, Bertrand Bisimwa, and Freddy Kaniki. He affirmed the rebellion’s commitment to protecting civilians in areas under its control and warned that any escalation would be the responsibility of Kinshasa.
International Reactions
Belgium :Maxime Patrick Robert Albert Prévot, Belgian Deputy Prime Minister and Foreign Minister, condemned the attacks as a violation of the ceasefire, emphasizing that such strikes against humanitarian workers are rising. “The drone attack in Goma claimed the lives of a humanitarian worker and civilians. The guns must fall silent. Let’s negotiate,” he said on X, offering condolences to the victims’ families.
European Union :  Commissioner Hadja Lahbib, responsible for Crisis Preparedness and Equality, called for respect for international humanitarian law and protection of humanitarian personnel. “All parties must respect their commitments. Humanitarian workers must never be targeted,” she warned.
France :President Emmanuel Macron confirmed the death of the French UNICEF aid worker and urged all parties to respect international humanitarian law. “These aid workers are committed to saving lives in a precarious and dangerous environment,” he said, offering condolences to the family, colleagues, and loved ones of the deceased.
United Nations : The UN mission in the DRC (MONUSCO) strongly condemned the escalation, describing the attacks as deeply concerning and reiterating that targeting UN personnel could constitute war crimes under the Rome Statute. Bruno Lemarquis, Acting Head of MONUSCO, called for a swift, independent investigation into the drone strikes and urged both parties to prioritize political dialogue to protect civilians.
International Contact Group (ICG) :  Earlier, on March 5, members of the ICG, including Belgium, Denmark, the EU, France, Germany, the Netherlands, Sweden, Switzerland, the UK, and the USA, had expressed concern over ceasefire violations in eastern DRC, highlighting the use of drones in military operations that endanger civilians. They stressed that there is no military solution to the conflict and called for urgent negotiations.
Context
The strikes come amid ongoing diplomatic efforts, including the Washington and Doha processes, supported by the African Union. Despite these initiatives, tensions remain high between Kinshasa and Kigali, as well as between Kinshasa and the AFC/M23, limiting progress on the ground.
The attacks also reignite concern over humanitarian security, as the UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) had recorded a decrease in incidents affecting aid workers in January 2026, but the Goma attack demonstrates that risks remain severe.

*Islamic State-Linked ADF Fighters Launch Deadly Attack on Muchacha Mining Site
Mambasa, Ituri March 15, 2026 – A deadly attack struck the Muchacha and Mavuvu mining sites on the night of March 11–12, carried out by Allied Democratic Forces (ADF) fighters linked to the Islamic State. Conclusion
The period from March 9 to 22, 2026, revealed the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) at a pivotal crossroads, navigating ambitious national development projects amid persistent security and political challenges.
On the security front, eastern provinces remain volatile. Despite international mediation efforts through the Washington and Doha tracks, which enabled gestures such as the release of over 5,000 FARDC soldiers, tangible improvements for local populations remain limited. Clashes involving the AFC/M23, Wazalendo militias, and the ADF continue, with civilian communities bearing the brunt of the violence. Humanitarian crises persist, exemplified by the destruction of judicial infrastructure in Goma and Bukavu and continued displacement.
Politically, the landscape is marked by polarization and high-stakes maneuvering. Resignations of senior parliamentary figures, such as Modeste Bahati Lukwebo, and rising opposition debates over constitutional amendments underscore a tense road toward 2028 electoral cycles. Meanwhile, legal reforms and institutional strengthening, including the establishment of the Economic and Financial Criminal Court, signal efforts to improve governance and combat corruption.
Economically, the DRC is pursuing strategic modernization, from infrastructure projects under Amendment 5 of the Sicomines agreement to the launch of a national gold refinery in Kalemie. Investments in energy, notably the Inga III development program, reflect a drive to transform the country into a continental energy hub. Yet these advances coexist with setbacks: the precarious state of Congo Airways, deadly landslides in Lualaba and Masisi, and the ongoing risks posed by irregular migration routes highlight continued vulnerabilities.
Overall, March 2026 underscores a nation striving for progress but constrained by conflict and institutional fragility. While the DRC lays foundations for long-term economic growth, energy expansion, and administrative modernization, persistent security threats and political tensions threaten to undermine these gains. Sustained stability will demand robust governance, inclusive dialogue, transparent resource management, and effective enforcement of the rule of law to ensure that economic and social progress reaches all Congolese citizens.
