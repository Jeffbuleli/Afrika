Nduhungirehe rejected that narrative, arguing that Kinshasa’s commemoration ignores violence committed against Tutsis in the DRC, particularly during the outbreak of the Second Congo War in 1998.

He also cited statements attributed to then-Foreign Affairs Minister Abdoulaye Yerodia Ndombasi, whom he accused of using dehumanizing language against Tutsis. Nduhungirehe said Belgium subsequently issued an arrest warrant against Ndombasi over allegations linked to his statements, but that he was never prosecuted by Congolese authorities.
Ndombasi died on February 19, 2019, and Nduhungirehe criticized Tshisekedi for allowing him to receive an official funeral and state honors. The Rwandan minister argued that Kinshasa cannot present itself solely as a victim of mass atrocities while, in his view, failing to acknowledge violence against Tutsis and the role of Congolese political actors in fueling ethnic persecution.

*Rwanda-DRC Partnership: From Regional Cooperation to Renewed Tensions
Kigali, 10 August 2026 - The relationship between Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of Congo has shifted sharply in recent years, moving from efforts to strengthen security and economic cooperation to renewed accusations over armed groups and the exploitation of eastern Congo’s mineral resources.
After Félix Tshisekedi became president of the DRC, Kigali and Kinshasa pursued closer ties, culminating in a series of agreements in 2021 aimed at improving security cooperation, cross-border trade and investment. The initiatives were designed to allow the two neighboring countries to address shared challenges directly and create opportunities for economic development.
In February 2021, Rwandan and Congolese security and intelligence officials agreed to strengthen intelligence sharing, particularly in the fight against armed groups such as the FDLR and ADF. The approach marked an attempt to replace confrontation with greater coordination on regional security.
The partnership expanded during a meeting between Presidents Paul Kagame and Félix Tshisekedi in Rubavu and Goma on 26 and 27 June 2021. The two countries signed agreements covering investment, taxation and mineral trade. Among them was a deal involving Congolese state mining company Sakima SA and Rwandan company Dither Ltd, under which gold produced in Kivu and Maniema would be processed in Rwanda, including at the Gasabo Gold Refinery in Kigali.
The initiative was presented as a way to formalize mineral trade, increase state revenues and reduce smuggling networks that have long been linked to armed groups operating in eastern DRC.
However, relations deteriorated toward the end of 2021 and into 2022, as Kinshasa increasingly accused Rwanda of supporting the M23 armed group and seeking to benefit from Congo’s mineral wealth. Kigali has repeatedly rejected the accusations.
The debate has also revived questions about the historical role of foreign interests in Congo’s mineral sector. Critics of the existing system argue that decades of external involvement in the exploitation and export of gold and other valuable minerals have allowed foreign intermediaries to capture significant profits while local communities and the Congolese state have received less benefit.
Supporters of closer Rwanda-DRC cooperation argue that the 2021 agreements offered a potential framework for addressing two interconnected problems: armed-group activity and illegal mineral trafficking. The collapse of that cooperation, they contend, has contributed to renewed tensions and competing narratives over the roots of instability in eastern Congo.
In April 2025, President Kagame argued that international attention on minerals should not overshadow the security and humanitarian problems affecting communities in eastern DRC. He questioned the logic of focusing on mineral wealth while civilians continue to face violence, displacement and insecurity.
The deterioration of Rwanda-DRC relations has since become one of the central challenges shaping security and diplomacy in the Great Lakes region, with the future of economic cooperation increasingly tied to efforts to resolve the broader conflict in eastern Congo.

Kigali, August 3, 2026 - Rwanda has allocated land in its industrial zones to about 405 investors, with 193 projects already completed, according to Trade and Industry Minister Antoine Marie Kajangwe. The government is now tightening oversight of projects that have failed to meet construction deadlines agreed upon with investors.
Kajangwe provided the update during a Senate plenary session on August 3, where he outlined measures being taken to address infrastructure and implementation challenges affecting the country’s industrial zones. Of the 405 projects allocated land, about 99 are currently under construction, while approximately 122 have not yet started. He said investors sign contractual agreements specifying when construction must begin, allowing the government to enforce the terms when deadlines are repeatedly missed.
Infrastructure remains another challenge. While electricity has been supplied to all industrial zones and water networks have been installed, the Senate noted that water shortages continue to affect some areas. Under Rwanda’s second National Strategy for Transformation (NST2), the government plans to prioritize infrastructure development in four industrial zones-Muhanga, Bugesera, Rwamagana and Musanze-while work in other zones will depend on the availability of funds.
Rwanda currently has 10 industrial zones, with investors assigned according to the types of industries intended for each location. Nyagatare and Nyabihu focus on agro-processing industries, while Musanze is designated in part for the production of construction materials. The government says improving infrastructure, enforcing investment agreements and reallocating unused industrial land are intended to accelerate industrial development and increase economic returns from the zones.

A solar-powered system draws water from Lake Muhazi, supported by irrigation equipment worth about Rwf72 million.
The farm currently has 1,282 avocado trees, with production expected to increase as the trees mature. A recent harvest yielded 260 kilograms, while another harvest is expected to reach about one tonne as the trees develop. The farm is also targeting markets outside Rwanda.
At the Catholic University of Rwanda’s Save campus in Gisagara District, SAIP II has supported irrigation for a 10-hectare orchard, using water from the Rwasave Swamp. The project, also valued at around Rwf72 million, has created employment for about 80 people, while the first harvests are already underway. The university plans to integrate the farm into practical training for students studying food processing.
Another example comes from Busasamana Sector in Nyanza District, where investor Kuyihura Claver grows avocados on 7.5 hectares. His farm received more than Rwf49 million through SAIP II to establish an irrigation system after some of the trees began showing signs of drought stress.
The farm’s approximately 800 mature avocado trees, planted in 2006, now produce around four tonnes annually, with markets in Kigali and abroad. The growing results from these farms suggest that reliable irrigation could help Rwanda expand fruit production, improve farmers’ incomes and gradually reduce reliance on imported fruit.
