*Burhan Dialogue Plan Faces Opposition Rejection

Khartoum, August 15, 2026 - Sudan's military leader Abdel Fattah al-Burhan backed legal, political and security guarantees for an internal national dialogue, but key opposition figures rejected the initiative as an attempt to legitimise continued military rule. Burhan said the state would provide logistical support and protections requested by an independent mechanism, while not setting the agenda or outcomes of the talks.

The proposed measures include suspending state legal proceedings against participants, shielding attendees from arrest linked to private legal claims during the process, and granting immunity for statements and proposals made in connection with the dialogue. Opposition leaders in the Somoud coalition said the plan did not address the priorities they see as essential to ending the war, including a ceasefire, civilian protection and humanitarian access.

Babikir Faisal said the initiative would not restore a democratic civilian transition, while Khaled Omer Youssef warned that continued military dominance would empower Islamist networks linked to the former Omar al-Bashir regime. Somoud spokesman Bakry Eljak said talks held only in army-controlled areas would lack inclusivity and appeared designed to sideline international mediation, including efforts by the Quad grouping of the United States, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates and Egypt.

Umma Party leader Mubarak al-Fadil al-Mahdi said any viable process should be held outside Sudan, require the military to cede political and economic power, and include acceptance of international peace proposals. The Democratic Bloc, a coalition of armed groups and political factions aligned with the military against the RSF, welcomed the initiative and argued that national ownership was essential.

The split response underscores Sudan's fragmented political landscape and the risk that competing peace tracks will prolong uncertainty over governance, sanctions exposure, aid flows and eventual reconstruction planning.

*Central Bank Restores National Electronic Payment Switch

Khartoum, August 6, 2026 - The Bank of Sudan reactivated the national electronic payment switch after more than three years of war-related disruption, marking a significant step toward restoring the country's damaged financial infrastructure. The conflict between the army and the Rapid Support Forces has severely damaged the banking system, with branches looted and electronic payment networks, ATMs and interbank transfers disrupted across much of the country.

First Deputy Governor Al-Muatasim Abdullah Ahmed launched the restored service with officials from Electronic Banking Services, the central bank's technical arm. Omdurman National Bank became the first institution reconnected to the switch, completing a successful ATM cash withdrawal test.

Central bank officials plan to reconnect remaining operational banks in stages. The platform is intended to support interbank transfers, retail payments such as electricity purchases and the reactivation of point-of-sale terminals. Restoring digital banking capacity is important for commerce, humanitarian payments and financial inclusion after years of economic paralysis, though reliability will depend on security conditions, power supply and the pace of bank reconnections.

*Central Bank Tightens Foreign Exchange And Customer Compliance Rules

Khartoum, August 12, 2026 - The Central Bank of Sudan restricted commercial banks' ability to buy export proceeds at their own declared exchange rates, tightening control over foreign currency operations as authorities seek to stabilize the national currency. Under the new rules, banks may use their own declared rates only for export operations they directly financed.

For import transactions and export proceeds not financed by the individual bank, commercial lenders must use the central bank's daily foreign exchange injection rate. The measure is part of a wider effort to streamline foreign exchange management in a wartime economy marked by hard-currency shortages, parallel-market pressure and high import costs.

In a separate circular, the central bank gave commercial banks until September 30 to update all customer account details, with the two-month window running from July 30. Accounts that are not updated by the deadline will be suspended and frozen. The regulator said the update is tied to anti-money laundering, counter-terrorism financing and proliferation-financing compliance.

Banks were also instructed to deploy monitoring systems capable of tracking account movements and detecting unusual activity at an early stage.

*Fuel And Cooking Gas Prices Cut As Central Bank Backs Imports

Khartoum, August 11, 2026 - Several Sudanese states reduced prices for petroleum products and cooking gas after the Central Bank of Sudan provided foreign currency for fuel imports, easing pressure on consumers and businesses in a market strained by war damage to energy infrastructure. Khartoum State cut the price of a 12.5-kilogram cooking gas cylinder to 85,000 Sudanese pounds from 106,400 pounds.

Bakery gas was lowered to 3,742 pounds per litre, while diesel fell slightly to 8,518 pounds per litre. River Nile state lowered the cylinder price to 80,000 pounds from 104,000 pounds and cut diesel prices by 750 pounds.

The reductions followed recent import measures under which the central bank supplied foreign exchange directly to public and private importers. The policy is intended to reduce reliance on the parallel currency market, lower import costs and curb pressure on the Sudanese pound.

Sudan's energy supply remains vulnerable after the war disrupted oil infrastructure, including the shutdown of the al-Jaili refinery, which had previously covered about 70 percent of domestic consumption. The government moved in June to directly manage petroleum imports and imposed tighter requirements on fuel importers, including gold deposits for import permits, as part of broader efforts to stabilize trade and foreign exchange reserves.

*Smuggled Sudanese Gold Is Laundered Through South Sudan

Khartoum, August 6, 2026 - Sudanese gold continues to be smuggled across land borders into South Sudan, where traders say it is recast, documented as locally mined and exported onward through Juba to international markets. The trade reflects a shift in Sudan's conflict economy.

Gold from Darfur and South Kordofan is moved through unmonitored corridors by truck and motorcycle, then bought in cash with US dollars at prices above the official rate offered by the Central Bank of Sudan. Traders then resmelt and consolidate the metal before securing paperwork that identifies it as South Sudanese production.

The process allows gold to move by air from Juba International Airport to the United Arab Emirates and international refineries with a changed legal origin. Economists describe the practice as origin laundering, a method used to bypass sanctions exposure and obscure links to armed groups.

The trade has direct implications for Sudan's war financing. The Rapid Support Forces have long been linked to gold revenues, particularly after taking control of the Jebel Amer mine in 2017. Gold-producing areas such as Songo and Um Dafuq in South Darfur, now under RSF control, are estimated to yield several tonnes annually and provide revenue for checkpoints, supply lines and logistics.

Darfur is estimated by conflict-economy researchers and UN expert panels to produce between 10 and 20 tonnes of gold a year. For investors and sanctions monitors, the laundering of Sudanese gold through neighboring transit states complicates due diligence across bullion, refining, logistics and banking networks tied to the Gulf.

*Sudan And Egypt Agree 16-Month Plan To Rehabilitate Jebel Aulia Dam

The bridge was destroyed in November 2023 during shelling by the Rapid Support Forces, while the main dam structure remained largely intact.

Sudanese and Egyptian irrigation officials reviewed field assessments, technical specifications and urgent maintenance needs before broader restoration work begins. The dam, located about 44 km south of Khartoum, was built between 1933 and 1937 and has storage capacity of 3.5 billion cubic meters.

It supports irrigation, local fishing and power generation, making its restoration important for water management and economic recovery around the capital. Sudanese irrigation officials said the facility had deteriorated after RSF forces used it to move heavy armor and operated its mechanisms without technical oversight.

Egyptian Water Resources and Irrigation Minister Hani Sewilam directed Egyptian teams to maintain close coordination with Sudan and expedite required supplies under bilateral Nile water arrangements.

Kurmuk's location on the Ethiopian border gives the allegations wider significance for Sudan's war trajectory, cross-border security and accountability efforts. *Sudan Convenes National Forum On Violence Against Women And Children

The agenda includes a unified national strategy, practical guidelines for state-level operations, and improved systems for monitoring, documentation, case management, psychosocial support, and logistics in displacement camps and shelters. The initiative comes as the United Nations Population Fund estimates that 12.3 million women and girls in Sudan face the risk of gender-based violence in 2026, an 80% increase from 2024.

Economic hardship, armed group activity, displacement, and deteriorating access to water, sanitation, markets, schools, and aid distribution points have increased exposure to forced and early marriage, sexual exploitation, harassment, trafficking, and hazardous informal labour.

Women and girls account for roughly half of Sudan's displaced population, with overcrowded shelters and limited privacy compounding protection risks. The crisis is also intersecting with food insecurity, as an estimated 4.2 million children, pregnant women, and breastfeeding women face acute malnutrition in 2026, while 13.6 million women and girls require nutritional assistance.

The Mohafiz, produced by Pakistan Ordnance Factories, is designed for mobility and field maintenance and offers ballistic protection against light and medium arms fire and shrapnel. The vehicles are expected to be deployed in terrain such as Kordofan and Darfur, where both sides rely heavily on mobile ground operations.

Military experts said the vehicles could help the army counter the RSF's use of fast, open-top four-wheel-drive pick-ups in urban fighting and on state highways. The fighting underscores the strategic value of North Kordofan's transport corridors, which connect Darfur, El Obeid and the Khartoum region. Continued RSF pressure on these routes raises risks for logistics, humanitarian access and any commercial activity dependent on cross-country movement.

*Army Says It Repelled RSF Drone Attack On Diling

*Cholera And RSF Blockade Deepen Crisis In North Kordofan

Conclusion

The situation in Sudan from August 1 to 15, 2026, illustrates a nation deeply entangled in a two-front struggle: a volatile military contest and a fractured political landscape.

Political Stalemate: Burhan's domestic dialogue initiative-offering temporary legal immunity-has laid bare the political rift in the country. While military-aligned factions like the Democratic Bloc support the domestic track, major civilian alliances such as Somoud and TASIS continue to reject army-backed frameworks, demanding an immediate ceasefire, civilian protection, and an end to military dominance. Meanwhile, international mediation via the Quintet mechanism remains stalled due to widespread boycotts and disputes over participant selection.

Military & Humanitarian Escalation: Despite the Sudanese Armed Forces securing strategic trade corridors like the Export Road to El Obeid, the Rapid Support Forces continue to mount counter-offensives, execute heavy drone attacks across major urban hubs, and expand direct tribal mobilization in Darfur. This ongoing warfare has exacerbated acute humanitarian emergencies, driving new waves of displacement, disrupting the agricultural season, and fueling severe public health outbreaks, including cholera and dengue.

Economic & Infrastructure Resilience: Amid severe inflation and currency depreciation, central state institutions are attempting to reassert control through foreign exchange restrictions, anti-smuggling crackdowns on gold, and the reactivation of core infrastructure like the national electronic payment switch. However, these stabilization efforts remain highly vulnerable to chronic power shortages, war damage, and border security risks.

Ultimately, Sudan’s short-term outlook hinges on overcoming political fragmentation. Without an inclusive, unified transition framework that prioritizes a durable ceasefire and humanitarian access, competing peace tracks risk hardening territorial divisions, prolonging economic paralysis, and deepening the crisis for millions of displaced civilians.
