*EACOP hits 91% completion

Kampala, August 8, 2026 - The EACOP has reached 91 percent completion. The construction of the 1,443-kilometre East African Crude Oil Pipeline (EACOP) has reached 91 per cent completion, bringing Uganda closer to achieving first oil production, which is expected by the end of this year.

The USD 5 billion pipeline is designed to transport crude oil from Uganda’s Albertine Graben to the Chongoleani Peninsula near the Port of Tanga in Tanzania. The project is being implemented through a joint venture between China Petroleum Pipeline Engineering Co.

The pipeline begins at Nyamasoga Village in Kabaale Parish, Hoima District, Uganda and passes through the districts of Hoima, Kikuube, Kakumiro, Kyankwanzi, Mubende, Gomba, Sembabule, Lwengo, Kyotera, and Rakai before crossing into Tanzania near the Uganda-Tanzania border. It then continues through Bukoba, Kahama, Singida, Kondoa, and terminates at Tanga on the Indian Ocean.

Owned by TotalEnergies E&P Uganda (62 per cent), Uganda National Oil Company (15 per cent), Tanzania Petroleum Development Corporation (15 per cent), and CNOOC Uganda (8 per cent), the pipeline features a 24-inch insulated and electrically heated pipeline, six pump stations, two of them in Uganda, two pressure reduction stations, and a marine export terminal in Tanzania. EACOP Deputy Managing Director John Bosco Habomugisha said that progress on the works, which was expected to have reached 94 per cent completion by now, was slowed by several unforeseen challenges. He attributed part of the delay to the conflict in the Middle East, which disrupted the transportation of critical project cargo.

*Civil society debates how to regulate methane

The government expects first oil before the end of September this year.

But as the country moves closer to production, questions are growing over how Uganda should regulate methane emissions from the oil and gas sector while also addressing emissions from agriculture, waste management and other activities. At the centre of the debate is whether Uganda should develop specific methane regulations for petroleum operations or adopt a wider framework covering short-lived climate pollutants, including methane, black carbon and hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs).

Scientists estimate that methane has more than 80 times the warming potential of carbon dioxide over its first 20 years in the atmosphere. The debate comes as the Ministry of Energy and Mineral Development develops a draft Methane Emissions Abatement Roadmap and a National Action Plan for Short-Lived Climate Pollutants.

Conclusion

Between August 1 and 15, 2026, Uganda operated at a critical intersection of monumental economic transitions, persistent governance friction, and expanding regional-global diplomacy. As the nation stands on the precipice of commercial oil production, its macro-economic outlook is defined by both significant structural momentum and foundational institutional challenges.

Infrastructure & Energy Milestones: Construction progress on the East African Crude Oil Pipeline (EACOP) reached 91%, setting the stage for first oil production before the end of the year. Key supporting assets, including the Kabalega International Airport handover (scheduled for late October), the Tanga Regional Energy Hub agreement with Tanzania, and the expansion of manufacturing to 16.5% of GDP, signal a structural shift toward industrialization and resource-backed growth.

Institutional & Regulatory Friction: Economic gains remain vulnerable to administrative inefficiencies and governance hurdles. Cabinet formation delays driven by appointee eligibility verifications, ongoing public-sector payroll/absenteeism disputes, land registration bottlenecks, high commercial lending costs, and anti-corruption probes within Parliament highlight execution risks that directly affect market confidence and project delivery.

Political Environment & Rule of Law: Grassroots power dynamics following the LC1 elections, ongoing opposition friction-exemplified by the high-profile legal and medical developments surrounding Dr. Kizza Besigye-and legal challenges regarding civic space and electoral administration emphasize the delicate balance between state authority and democratic accountability.

Expanded Geopolitical and Security Footprint: Uganda continues to leverage its diplomatic and military capacity as a central pillar of state strategy. This is evidenced by the national consensus supporting Olara Otunnu’s UN Secretary-General candidacy, deep-seated economic ties via Stanbic Bank's direct CIPS Yuan integration, UPDF leadership transitions, and parliamentary authorization for troop deployments to the Gaza peace mission.

The narrative of August 2026 underscores a country transitioning into a capital-intensive, resource-producing economy. However, sustaining long-term growth and translating "black gold" into broader public prosperity will depend heavily on resolving internal governance bottlenecks, enforcing rule of law, protecting civic stability, and managing declining international humanitarian funding alongside growing regional commitments. Investors and policy observers must monitor how effectively high-level accountability mandates are converted into institutional compliance across all levels of state administration.
