REPORT ON THE SITUATION IN SUDAN FROM August 16 to 31, 2026

	1.	Politics and Diplomacy
*AU Keeps Sudan Suspension On Table After Khartoum Mission
Khartoum, August 24, 2026 - An African Union Peace and Security Council delegation did not discuss lifting Sudan's suspension during talks with military leader Abdel Fattah al-Burhan, keeping the country's regional isolation tied to democratic transition benchmarks.

The delegation, led by Algerian Ambassador Mohamed Khaled, visited Khartoum on August 16 and 17 and met Burhan, Transitional Prime Minister Kamil El-Tayeb Idris, and other senior officials. The discussions focused on implementing recent AU summit resolutions and addressing the war between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces.

Sudan has been suspended from the AU since October 2021, when the military seized power and removed the civilian-led government of Prime Minister Abdalla Hamdok. The suspension was imposed under the African Charter on Democracy, Elections and Governance, which mandates sanctions after unconstitutional changes of government.

Khartoum presented a roadmap beginning with a ceasefire, followed by an inclusive Sudanese dialogue to prepare for a civilian government and general elections. Sudanese officials also made the end of the RSF rebellion and the withdrawal of RSF fighters from civilian infrastructure and public institutions a primary condition for resuming the political process.

The AU delegation emphasized that ending Sudan's suspension depends on concrete steps toward restoring democratic rule and building national consensus. For investors and lenders, the outcome confirms that normalization with key African institutions remains contingent on both security de-escalation and a credible political transition.

*African Union Backs Sudan Dialogue Plan As Civilian Blocs Object
Khartoum, August 31, 2026 - The African Union endorsed a new Sudanese dialogue initiative aimed at ending the civil war, but major civilian and political groups rejected the move as premature and potentially favorable to the army-led authorities.

A Khartoum-based preparatory committee of public figures began consultations to coordinate talks with political and civil society actors, with security guarantees from the army-led government. Army chief Abdel Fattah al-Burhan had previously offered support for the initiative, including legal and political protections for participating factions.

The African Union Commission welcomed the effort as an initiative led by an independent committee of Sudanese elders and said it could complement mediation under the AU-led Quintet Mechanism, which includes the United Nations, European Union, IGAD, and Arab League. The AU said any process should be inclusive and aimed at a unified, sovereign Sudan governed by legitimate civilian institutions.

Civilian coalitions and parties, including figures from the National Umma Party, the Sudanese Congress Party, and the Declaration of Principles forces, warned that talks held under military oversight and without an active ceasefire could convert battlefield gains into political leverage. They argued the process lacks transparent participation criteria and risks entrenching Sudan's de facto fragmentation.

For investors and policymakers, the dispute underscores the difficulty of turning diplomatic initiatives into a credible transition framework while the army and Rapid Support Forces remain locked in conflict and civilian actors distrust processes shaped inside areas controlled by one side.

*Arman Faction Rejects Burhan Dialogue Plan Without Ceasefire
Khartoum, August 16, 2026 - The Sudan People's Liberation Movement-Revolutionary Democratic Current rejected army chief Abdel Fattah al-Burhan's call for national dialogue, warning that talks held during active hostilities would legitimize the war rather than create a credible path to peace.

The faction led by Yasir Arman said any political process launched without a ceasefire, humanitarian measures, and agreed mediation would lack practical value. It also objected to a preparatory committee shaped by one belligerent party and its allies.

The SPLM-RDC called for inclusive participation by anti-war forces and communities directly affected by the conflict. It argued that a durable settlement must address the roots of Sudan's crisis, including militia proliferation, state capture, national unity, the legacy of the former National Congress Party, and the absence of a single professional national army.

The movement also warned against the inclusion of Islamist forces in the proposed Khartoum talks, comparing the initiative to failed dialogue efforts under former President Omer al-Bashir. It called instead for direct negotiations between the military and anti-war forces at a mutually agreed neutral venue.

For investors and diplomats, the statement underscores the depth of Sudan's political fragmentation and the difficulty of converting elite dialogue proposals into a ceasefire or governance framework capable of stabilizing state institutions.

*Civil Figures Launch Domestic Dialogue Initiative To End Sudan War
Khartoum, August 30, 2026 - A coalition of prominent Sudanese civil figures launched an initiative to prepare an internal national dialogue aimed at ending the war, testing whether domestic mediation can gain traction amid deep political distrust.

The move follows General Abdel Fattah al-Burhan's August 14 approval of a domestic framework for talks and his offer to provide legal and security guarantees. Organizers said the new group would operate independently to rebuild confidence among political, civil and community leaders before formal intra-Sudanese negotiations are held inside Sudan.

The panel includes constitutional lawyer Nabil Adib, Ahfad University President Gasim Badri, writer Mohamed Jalal Hashim and editor Osman Mirghani. It called for preliminary talks to define the framework, representation rules and core security safeguards for a credible process.

Several key actors remain opposed. The Somoud Coalition, the Sudan Liberation Movement led by Abdel Wahid al-Nur and Mubarak al-Fadil's Umma Party faction have rejected Burhan's initiative, warning that talks under current conditions could entrench military control. They want the Islamic Movement and its affiliates excluded, while the Democratic Bloc supports broader participation and would exclude only forces allied with the RSF unless they sever ties and condemn violations.

The initiative demands written, verifiable guarantees, including temporary procedural immunity from arrest or prosecution for participants entering Sudan for talks. For investors and diplomats, the effort is significant because any credible political process would be central to restoring institutional stability, reducing sanctions and legal risks, and reopening a path to reconstruction financing.

*Hamdok Urges Unified Civilian Front To End Sudan War
Addis Ababa, August 23, 2026 - Former Sudanese prime minister Abdalla Hamdok said Sudan's civil war has no military solution and called for civilian unity and sustained regional diplomacy to stop the conflict.

Hamdok, who heads the Somoud coalition and the Declaration of Principles group, spoke after two days of consultations in Addis Ababa among political parties, civil society organizations, and youth and women's networks. The meetings were aimed at building a unified anti-war civilian front.

Participants outlined a peace vision built around three integrated and simultaneous tracks, including a humanitarian pathway to protect civilians, open aid routes, and support the unconditional return of displaced people.

The groups also rejected military leader Abdel Fattah al-Burhan's dialogue initiative, saying it was shaped unilaterally by one side, controlled in timing and venue by a warring party, and conducted under that side's security apparatus. They argued that such a process would legitimize the war rather than end it.

The consultations matter for Sudan's political outlook because a more cohesive civilian bloc could influence future mediation efforts, transition planning, and international engagement if the battlefield stalemate persists.

*South Sudan Offers To Mediate Sudan Peace Process
Juba, August 17, 2026 - South Sudanese President Salva Kiir said Juba is ready to mediate a settlement to Sudan's war, positioning his government as a potential regional channel for talks between the Sudanese Armed Forces, the Rapid Support Forces and allied factions.

Kiir made the offer after meeting Darfur regional governor Minni Arko Minawi in Juba. He said stability in Sudan and South Sudan is closely linked and that his government is prepared to engage all warring parties through diplomatic channels.

Juba previously brokered the October 2020 peace agreement between Khartoum and several rebel groups, including Minawi's Sudan Liberation Movement faction. Implementation has largely stalled, with signatory groups split between the SAF and RSF since the war began in April 2023.

Minawi said a Sudanese delegation would visit Juba soon to review the 2020 deal and assess unresolved issues, including regional stability, land disputes, displaced populations and alleged RSF abuses. Any revived mediation track could affect conflict dynamics in Darfur and the wider investment climate by shaping prospects for security and reconstruction.

*Sudan Deepens Saudi Ties As Army Pursues Turkish Military Support

The pact, signed in Riyadh, focuses officially on economic and trade cooperation across 10 sectors. Its broader significance lies in Sudan's effort to secure political backing, supply lines, and regional alignment along the Red Sea corridor.

The Saudi track coincided with a visit to Turkey by Lieutenant General Yasir al-Atta, assistant commander-in-chief of the Sudanese Armed Forces. The talks in Ankara come as Sudan seeks advanced military capabilities, including drones, while fighting continues in Kordofan and Darfur.

For investors, the agreement points to a more structured Sudanese outreach to Gulf partners at a time when financial support, Red Sea security, and the balance of military power are central to the country's political trajectory.

*Sudan Rejects Claims Its Forces Struck Inside Chad
Khartoum, August 27, 2026 - Sudan's foreign ministry rejected allegations that its military carried out airstrikes in Chad, warning that premature blame could further strain relations across already volatile border zones.

The ministry responded after a senior U.S. Africa adviser condemned air attacks in Chad that he attributed to the Sudanese military. Chad's armed forces said an air raid had struck a vehicle convoy in Ennedi-Est province, more than 100 kilometres from the Sudanese border, and said available information pointed to Sudanese aircraft or allied fighters.

Khartoum said responsibility should not be assigned before a professional and independent inquiry. It said Sudan respects Chad's territorial integrity and that its army operates within a mandate to protect national security, not to target neighbouring territory.

Sudan also accused the international community of ignoring what it described as daily drone incursions from Ethiopia into Blue Nile state. The army said it intercepted a drone over the southeastern region on Thursday, the third such incident it reported this week.

The dispute highlights the regionalisation of Sudan's war, with Khartoum accusing both Chad and Ethiopia of enabling support to the RSF, charges both governments deny. Sudan said it remains willing to address border security through direct dialogue, a critical issue for regional trade routes, refugee flows and any future diplomatic settlement.

*Sudan Seeks China-Led Backing Against UN Rights Inquiry Renewal
Khartoum, August 26, 2026 - Sudan asked a 29-member coalition led by China to help block efforts to renew the mandate of the UN Human Rights Council’s independent fact-finding mission, escalating a diplomatic fight over accountability during the war.

Khartoum has refused to cooperate with the investigators or allow them into Sudan. Hassan argued that three other UN human rights mechanisms already operate with the government’s cooperation and said an additional investigative body unfairly equates the Sudanese army with the Rapid Support Forces.

The fact-finding mission is tasked with collecting and analyzing evidence, identifying responsible individuals and entities, and recommending accountability measures. The dispute is likely to remain a marker of Sudan’s alignment with non-Western diplomatic blocs as the government seeks to limit external scrutiny while pursuing support against the RSF.

*Sudanese Anti-War Blocs Reject Burhan Dialogue Plan
Addis Ababa, August 23, 2026 - Sudanese anti-war political and civil groups rejected army chief Abdel Fattah al-Burhan's proposed domestic dialogue, deepening the divide over how to end the war and restore civilian rule.

The decision followed two days of consultations in Addis Ababa involving signatories to the Sudanese Declaration of Principles, the Popular Congress Party, the National Umma Party, civil society groups, women and youth networks, academics, and independent figures not aligned with the warring parties.

Participants included former Prime Minister Abdalla Hamdok, Sudan Liberation Movement leader Abdel Wahid al-Nur, Umma Party figure Mubarak al-Fadil, and other civilian leaders. The groups said they would neither join the proposed Sudanese-Sudanese dialogue nor recognize its outcomes, arguing that a process held in areas under one side's control would entrench military rule and risk legitimizing Sudan's de facto fragmentation.

The coalition instead proposed a peace process under an agreed independent international umbrella, built around three simultaneous tracks: urgent humanitarian access, a permanent ceasefire, and an exclusively civilian political process. It said the armed parties should be confined to ceasefire and humanitarian negotiations, while former ruling party networks and affiliated entities should be excluded from the political track.

For investors and donors, the position underscores that Sudan's political settlement remains contested, with civilian groups pressing for international mediation and a ceasefire before any governance transition can gain broad legitimacy.

*US Pushes UN To Expand Sudan Arms Embargo To Drones
New York, August 24, 2026 - The United States introduced a draft UN Security Council resolution to extend Sudan's arms embargo nationwide and explicitly include unmanned aerial vehicles, a move aimed at curbing external military support that is intensifying the war.

The proposal was presented during a Security Council briefing on Sudan attended by senior UN officials, Massad Boulos, Senior Adviser to the US President for Africa, Arab, and Middle Eastern Affairs, and Sudan's Permanent Representative to the UN, Al-Harith Idriss. The Sudanese government, China, and Russia opposed the move.

The draft resolution would expand the existing Darfur-focused embargo to all of Sudan, clarify that drone systems and related technologies are covered, increase staffing for the UN's 1591 Panel of Experts, and direct closer coordination with other regional sanctions panels to track violations of asset freezes, travel bans, and arms restrictions.

Washington said armed drones have become a central driver of civilian harm, killing more than 1,120 Sudanese civilians in the first half of 2026, or roughly 80 percent of recorded civilian deaths this year. The United States also cited attacks on markets, health facilities, humanitarian convoys, water stations, electricity grids, and other critical infrastructure by the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces.

For investors, the initiative signals rising international pressure on Sudan's warring parties and their foreign supply networks, but opposition inside the Security Council could limit enforcement. Any tighter sanctions regime would affect political risk, logistics, and due diligence for companies exposed to Sudan or regional supply chains.

*Burhan Orders Faster Repairs And Security At Merowe Dam
Merowe, August 16, 2026 - Sudan's army chief and Transitional Sovereign Council head Abdel Fattah al-Burhan ordered faster repairs and tighter security at the Merowe Dam to stabilize electricity supplies after outages hit several key states.

Burhan inspected the dam's power station and reviewed rehabilitation work following a fire that damaged main transmission lines. The disruption caused widespread blackouts in Khartoum, River Nile, Northern and Red Sea states.

Authorities deployed thermal generation as an emergency measure while crews replaced damaged cables and worked to reconnect the dam to the national grid. Technical teams also repaired damaged facilities, including a key transformer substation.

The directive underscores the national importance of Merowe Dam as power infrastructure during wartime. Reliable electricity is critical for banking, telecoms, water systems, ports and any private-sector restart in areas under government control.

*Central Bank Launches Platform To Track Foreign Capital Flows
Khartoum, August 26, 2026 - The Central Bank of Sudan launched an electronic system to monitor foreign capital and investment flows, a step aimed at tightening digital oversight and supporting monetary stability during a period of severe economic stress.

The Electronic Capital Flows System will be operated by the central bank's foreign exchange administration and is designed to record and regulate foreign capital moving into and out of the country.

The platform automates administrative procedures for foreign investors and allows online tracking of transactions through a central portal. It will also build a unified database covering investor profiles, economic sectors, source countries, capital volumes, and transaction flows.

Authorities said the system will track the full investment cycle, including capital registration, profit remittances, loan servicing, and capital repatriation. Commercial bank employees have completed operational training ahead of the nationwide rollout.

For investors, the system could improve regulatory transparency and balance-of-payments data, but its effectiveness will depend on implementation capacity, banking-sector connectivity, and the broader security environment.

Khartoum, August 19, 2026 - The Central Bank of Sudan is reviewing recent foreign exchange interventions aimed at boosting market liquidity and stabilizing the exchange rate, as pressure on the Sudanese pound continues to complicate imports and inflation control.

For investors, the policy signals continued administrative management of scarce hard currency and heightened compliance scrutiny across trade finance.

Sudan is seeking foreign investment and technical support to rebuild critical utilities damaged since the 2023 outbreak of fighting between the army and the Rapid Support Forces. For investors, Chinese interest in power generation underscores both the scale of reconstruction needs and the continued security and execution risks around infrastructure delivery.

*Darfur Gold Smuggling Network Fuels Sudan War Economy
El Fasher, August 31, 2026 - Darfur's gold fields have become a major pillar of Sudan's wartime shadow economy, with smuggling routes and militarized mining sites helping finance armed actors and bypass official state controls.

The Rapid Support Forces control large parts of Darfur, including mining areas that have been turned into closed-access, militarized extraction zones. The Jebel Amer mine in North Darfur remains the region's most prominent artisanal site, while high-output areas such as Radom and Songo in South Darfur provide access toward the Central African Republic border.

Other strategic hubs include Kutum and Kabkabiya in North Darfur, which act as collection points for alluvial gold, and Um Dukhun and Hufrat al-Nahas near the Sudan-Chad-Central African Republic tri-border area. Convoys guarded by armed fighters move gold through routes including Um Dafuq to Birao in northern Central African Republic and desert tracks into Chad.

The network has included links between the RSF and Russia's Wagner mercenary group, with arrangements built around securing extraction sites, operating closed processing facilities using cyanide and mercury, and exchanging logistics and technical support for arms and military hardware. Remote airstrips in Darfur and Wagner-linked positions around Birao have been used in the movement of supplies and consignments.

For investors, the expansion of illicit gold flows underlines the erosion of sovereign control over Sudan's mining sector, the sanctions and compliance risks attached to conflict minerals, and the difficulty of rebuilding formal export revenues while armed groups dominate production corridors.

*Sudan Central Bank Loosens Currency Rules As Pound Slides
Khartoum, August 25, 2026 - Sudan's central bank gave commercial banks wider authority to set exchange rates and buy export proceeds, seeking to stabilize a sharply weakening pound and bring foreign-exchange trading closer to market conditions.

The regulator said banks may adjust their published exchange rates at any time during the day, provided updated prices are displayed before transactions are executed. Commercial lenders are also allowed to purchase export earnings at posted rates based on supply and demand.

The directive revokes earlier restrictions, including a January 14 circular amending 2022 currency regulations and two circulars issued earlier in August on foreign-exchange market stability. If a bank declines to buy offered export proceeds, it must coordinate with the central bank's financial markets directorate to complete the transaction and ensure timely settlement.

The measures take immediate effect across bank branches. The Gold Exporters Chamber welcomed the shift, saying business groups had pressed authorities to remove the previous curbs.

For investors, the move points to growing pressure on Sudan's macroeconomic framework as war, hard-currency shortages, and parallel-market volatility strain trade and banking. A more flexible exchange-rate regime could improve formal market liquidity, but it also underscores continuing currency and convertibility risks.

	3.	Social and Health
*Sudan Health Ministry Flags HIV, Hepatitis And Cholera Pressures Amid War Damage
Khartoum, August 19, 2026 - Sudan's Health Ministry recorded 13,401 HIV cases and tens of thousands of hepatitis infections, underscoring the scale of public health stress after more than three years of war damaged hospitals, surveillance systems and basic services.

The ministry's Communicable Diseases Department presented the figures at a weekly emergency operations meeting in Khartoum. Officials said surveillance data through 2026 showed 13,401 HIV cases, while records through 2025 included about 32,000 hepatitis B cases and 23,000 hepatitis C cases.

Health authorities also cited localized hepatitis E outbreaks in Blue Nile state, ongoing cholera infections in West Kordofan and North Kordofan, and continued dengue transmission in Blue Nile and South Kordofan despite a national decline in case rates. Malaria remained steady in most regions, while Red Sea state continued treating heatstroke cases.

The federal health ministry said specialized medical teams were preparing to deploy to Al Jazirah and Sennar to investigate a reported rise in appendicitis cases. Authorities and rapid response units are also coordinating with UNICEF on missions across seven states after seasonal rains from August 11 to 17 damaged infrastructure in four states and eight localities.

The widening disease burden adds pressure to Sudan's already weakened state capacity and raises risks for humanitarian logistics, workforce availability and any future reconstruction of the health system.

Conclusion

Sudan closed the second half of August 2026 with the conflict becoming more institutionalized rather than closer to settlement. Political initiatives multiplied, but nearly all remained conditional, fragmented, or externally mediated, while battlefield activity intensified across Kordofan, Darfur, Blue Nile, and the Nuba Mountains. For investors and counterparties, the operating environment remains defined by contested authority, acute security volatility, weakening monetary conditions, damaged infrastructure, and rising compliance exposure linked to sanctions, arms flows, human rights scrutiny, and cross-border escalation.

Political Stalemate: Army chief Abdel Fattah al-Burhan's call for domestic dialogue failed to generate broad civilian buy-in. The SPLM-RDC, anti-war blocs, and key Umma Party figures rejected or conditioned participation, insisting on a ceasefire, a code of conduct, neutral venues, and credible guarantees before talks can be meaningful. Abdalla Hamdok's appeal for a unified civilian front underscored the same structural problem: Sudan has multiple anti-war and civilian platforms, but no single mechanism with sufficient legitimacy to compel the belligerents toward a transition. The AU's continued linkage of Sudan's suspension to democratic benchmarks and the US decision to maintain diplomatic channels below ambassadorial level reinforce that external legitimacy remains constrained.

Military & Humanitarian: The war's center of gravity continued to shift across corridors and logistics nodes rather than fixed front lines alone. North and West Kordofan saw repeated RSF attacks, drone incidents, displacement around Bara and El Obeid, and pressure on power and water systems. Nyala remained central to RSF military and administrative consolidation, while drone strikes and cargo-linked activity highlighted its strategic value. The SAF's processing of RSF defectors and JEM's stated readiness to integrate into the army may strengthen pro-army manpower, but they also raise accountability and command-and-control risks. In South Kordofan, SPLM-N control over Kauda was restored after internal violence, though local truces remain fragile. Blue Nile displacement near the Ethiopian border and Chadian accusations of a cross-border drone strike show that spillover risks are no longer theoretical.

Economy & Infrastructure: Macroeconomic pressure deepened as the pound weakened and the Central Bank shifted toward more flexible bank-level exchange-rate setting while reviewing FX interventions. The new electronic capital-flow monitoring platform signals an attempt to restore visibility over external transactions, but enforcement capacity remains limited by war fragmentation and damaged banking channels. The fintech license dispute illustrates regulatory unpredictability in emerging digital finance, while efforts to rehabilitate Khartoum industrial zones, secure Merowe Dam operations, and attract Chinese hybrid-power investment point to selective reconstruction planning despite continuing insecurity. Saudi and Kuwaiti engagement offers potential financing and trade-channel relief, but meaningful execution will depend on security guarantees, payment mechanisms, and sanctions risk management.

External Alignment & Compliance: Sudan's diplomacy became more transactional and security-focused. Khartoum deepened coordination with Saudi Arabia, sought Kuwaiti financing, pursued Turkish military cooperation, and asked a China-led bloc to oppose renewal of the UN rights inquiry. At the same time, the US push to expand the arms embargo to drones reflects growing international concern over unmanned systems and external supply chains. Any foreign investor, lender, logistics provider, or technology vendor should expect heightened due diligence requirements around counterparties, end-use controls, dual-use equipment, beneficial ownership, and exposure to military-linked entities.

Social & Health: Public-health and displacement indicators deteriorated further. HIV, hepatitis, and cholera pressures are unfolding in a health system weakened by destroyed facilities, impaired surveillance, and service disruption. More than 1.24 million returnees have attempted to resettle since 2024, but many remain unable to reach final destinations, while displaced families outside formal camps are increasingly invisible to aid pipelines. These dynamics will weigh on labor availability, consumer demand, local stability, and the feasibility of restoring normal commercial operations in affected states.

Looking ahead, Sudan is likely to remain in a high-risk equilibrium: no decisive political track, no decisive military outcome, and only partial economic stabilization measures. The most important indicators to monitor are whether a credible ceasefire framework emerges before any dialogue process, whether drone warfare triggers wider sanctions or cross-border retaliation, whether Kordofan supply routes and Nyala remain contested, and whether Gulf or Chinese-backed infrastructure initiatives can move beyond announcements. Until those variables improve, investor exposure should be limited, highly structured, and concentrated in essential services, humanitarian-adjacent supply chains, secured infrastructure repair, and carefully vetted financial channels.
