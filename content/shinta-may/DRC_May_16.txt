*WHO Declares Ebola Outbreak in Eastern DRC a Public Health Emergency of International Concern
GENEVA / KINSHASA, May 17, 2026 - The World Health Organization (World Health Organization) has declared the Ebola outbreak detected in Ituri a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC), citing heightened risks of regional spread, limited medical countermeasures, and ongoing insecurity in affected areas.
The decision was announced by WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, one day after the outbreak was confirmed as the 17th Ebola epidemic recorded in the Democratic Republic of Congo and linked to the Bundibugyo strain.
According to the WHO, the classification was based on several critical factors, including a high positivity rate among initial samples, documented cross-border transmission, and the absence of an approved vaccine or specific treatment for this strain.
The organization emphasized that while the situation is severe, it does not currently meet the criteria for a global pandemic emergency.
Health authorities reported that the outbreak has already shown signs of regional spread, with confirmed cases detected in neighboring Uganda, as well as in Kinshasa among individuals returning from Ituri.
As of May 16, preliminary figures indicate 8 confirmed cases, 246 suspected cases, and at least 80 deaths, with the most affected areas including Bunia, Rwampara, and Mongwalu health zones.
The WHO highlighted that insecurity in eastern DRC, population displacement, mining-related mobility, and dense informal healthcare networks are significantly increasing transmission risks. These conditions, it noted, mirror those seen during the major 2018-2019 Ebola outbreak in North Kivu and Ituri.
Unlike previous outbreaks caused by the Zaire strain, the Bundibugyo variant currently circulating has no approved vaccine or treatment, complicating response efforts.
The WHO has recommended activation of national emergency systems, intensified contact tracing, strict screening at points of entry, and safe burial practices. However, it cautioned against border closures or trade restrictions, stating that such measures lack scientific justification and may worsen uncontrolled cross-border movement.
The outbreak comes less than six months after the DRC declared the end of its previous Ebola epidemic in Kasai, which ended after 42 days without new confirmed cases.

Conclusion
The period from May 4 to 16, 2026, highlights the complex interplay of security, diplomatic, macroeconomic, and humanitarian developments within the Democratic Republic of Congo.
Geopolitics and Security Operations: Regional stability remains fragile despite active diplomatic and military efforts. Bilateral engagements with Uganda have progressed through joint economic projects and the extension of Operation Shujaa to combat the Allied Democratic Forces (ADF). However, persistent rebel activity, escalating civilian casualties, and growing local dissatisfaction in North Kivu and Ituri demonstrate the ongoing difficulty of fully securing the eastern provinces. Simultaneously, the Washington-mediated peace talks face diplomatic friction, with the AFC/M23 coalition accusing mediators of bias and questioning international neutrality.
Domestic Political and Legal Tensions: Domestically, the DRC is experiencing significant friction regarding institutional governance. President Félix Tshisekedi’s push for a constitutional revision has sparked widespread domestic and international debate. Opposition leaders and high-profile figures, such as Dr. Denis Mukwege, strongly contend that moving forward with a constitutional referendum or altering electoral timelines is democratically illegitimate while major portions of the eastern national territory remain highly insecure.
Macroeconomic Resilience amidst Governance Audits: On the economic front, the DRC shows signs of macroeconomic stability and strong structural growth. The successful staff-level agreement reached with the International Monetary Fund (IMF) for the third review of the Extended Credit Facility (ECF) points to a resilient national economy, with real GDP growth remaining above 5.5% and inflation heavily contained. Concurrently, the newly announced government audit into mining export revenues and state-owned entities marks a pivotal step toward structural accountability, answering long-standing calls from institutions like the Carter Center to correct systemic tax gaps and governance weaknesses in the extractive sector.
Public Health and Environmental Vulnerabilities: The period closes with heightened emergency conditions, primarily driven by the declaration of the 17th Ebola epidemic in Ituri as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC). The lack of a standard treatment or vaccine for the circulating Bundibugyo strain, paired with regional mobility and active conflict zones, significantly heightens the risk of cross-border transmission-triggering aggressive border-screening measures from neighbors like Rwanda. Furthermore, localized natural disasters like the landslide in Walikale underscore the compounding infrastructure vulnerabilities facing communities in rain-prone, conflict-affected regions.
Ultimately, while the DRC continues to achieve notable economic milestones and forge strategic regional partnerships, its immediate path forward depends on its capacity to manage a severe public health crisis, implement transparent fiscal reforms, and establish an inclusive solution to the compounding security challenges in the east.
