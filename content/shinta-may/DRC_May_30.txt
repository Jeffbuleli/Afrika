*Uganda Postpones Martyrs’ Day Pilgrimage Over Ebola Concerns
Kampala, May 18, 2026 - Ugandan President Yoweri Museveni has announced the postponement of the country’s annual Martyrs’ Day celebrations amid growing concerns over the Ebola outbreak affecting the region.
In a statement released on Sunday, the Ugandan leader said the decision followed consultations with the national epidemic response team and religious authorities.
According to the presidency, the move was taken due to the large number of pilgrims who travel to Uganda each year from neighboring countries, particularly eastern Democratic Republic of Congo, where Ebola cases have been reported.
Martyrs’ Day, observed every year in early June at Namugongo, near Kampala, is one of the largest religious gatherings in East Africa. The event traditionally attracts tens of thousands of faithful from Uganda as well as from the Democratic Republic of Congo, Kenya, South Sudan, Rwanda, and Tanzania.
President Museveni urged pilgrims who had already begun traveling to return home and strictly follow health precaution measures aimed at preventing the spread of the virus.
Uganda has already recorded two confirmed cases of Ebola linked to travelers arriving from the DRC, including one fatality in Kampala involving the Bundibugyo strain of the virus.
Health authorities in Uganda have since intensified surveillance at border crossings and in high-risk districts, deployed mobile laboratories, and activated the country’s National Health Emergency Operations Center.
The World Health Organization (WHO) on Sunday declared the Ebola outbreak affecting both Uganda and the Democratic Republic of Congo a Public Health Emergency of International Concern, the highest level of global health alert.

Mukwege argued that discussions surrounding a possible constitutional amendment threaten the foundations of democracy in the DRC.
“The nation seems to be at the mercy of one man, the President of the Republic, and a coalition of political parties seeking to cling to power,” he stated, criticizing what he described as a political system centered on a “cult of personality” rather than democratic alternation.

Mukwege urged authorities to prioritize national security and social stability, particularly efforts aimed at ending the conflict in eastern DRC and restoring state authority across the national territory.
As political tensions continue to rise, he concluded his statement by calling on Congolese citizens to reject authoritarian tendencies and preserve national unity.
“Let us reaffirm our shared commitment to building, in the heart of Africa, a state governed by the rule of law and a prosperous nation,” he concluded, reiterating the slogan: “Hands off my Constitution!”

Conclusion
Based on the comprehensive monitoring of the Democratic Republic of Congo from May 15 to 30, 2026, the country remains caught in a complex web of intense political polarization, escalating security and humanitarian crises, and tentative economic steps toward structural reform.
Here is the conclusion structured around the core dynamics defining this period:
1. Political Polarization and Institutional Strain
The political landscape is increasingly fractured by the intensifying debate over constitutional reform. The ruling UDPS and the CNC have firmly pushed for a new constitution or extensive revisions, framing the current 2006 framework as an outdated wartime compromise. This push has met fierce resistance from opposition forces, civil society, and prominent figures like Nobel laureate Denis Mukwege, who warn of an "authoritarian drift" and a threat to democratic term limits. The deep divide was sharply illustrated by the opposition walkout in the National Assembly over the referendum law and the mobilization of the new C64 opposition alliance. Furthermore, localized governance challenges-evidenced by the resignation of Haut-Katanga Governor Jacques Kyabula and the legislative crisis in Lomami Province-highlight a broader systemic volatility.
2. Volatile Security and Regional Humanitarian Crises
The security situation in eastern DRC continues to be the nation's most pressing crisis. Despite territorial gains by the FARDC-Wazalendo coalition in Masisi, a fluid battlefield characterized by rapid cycles of advance and retreat-particularly around the strategic mining hub of Rubaya and across Walikale-has left local populations highly vulnerable. The entrenchment of parallel rebel administrations, drone strikes targeting critical infrastructure, and deadly ADF insurgencies in Ituri and North Kivu have driven mass civilian displacement and exacerbated food insecurity. This ongoing conflict continues to strain regional relations and underscores the limitations of purely military solutions, keeping international focus on stalled diplomatic tracks like the Washington and Doha agreements
3. Public Health Emergencies and Regional Containment
Compounding the security crisis is a severe public health emergency following the mid-May declaration of the Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern. Epicentered in Ituri, the virus has triggered cross-border transmission into Uganda, prompting urgent, coordinated containment efforts by the East African Community (EAC) and the World Health Organization (WHO). While logistical bottlenecks and temporary airport restrictions have complicated the delivery of humanitarian aid, international medical assistance and ongoing talks regarding experimental monoclonal treatments offer a critical path forward.
4. Economic Transformation and Governance Reforms
On a more resilient note, the DRC is aggressively pursuing structural economic reforms aimed at retaining national wealth and boosting investor confidence. The introduction of pioneering draft laws on local content and private sector subcontracting signals a deliberate pivot away from raw resource extraction toward local mineral processing and greater citizen participation in corporate value chains. Simultaneously, the announcement of a 100% completion rate on the FATF roadmap positions the DRC on the precipice of removal from the financial grey list, which would dramatically enhance its standing with international financial institutions and global markets.
Summary Statement: Ultimately, the latter half of May 2026 underscores that the DRC’s path to long-term stability requires an intertwined approach. Structural economic progress and public-private modernization-such as the new population identification program-will remain heavily compromised unless the state can forge an inclusive political consensus on governance, effectively manage its cross-border health crises, and decisively quell the violence uprooting its eastern provinces.
