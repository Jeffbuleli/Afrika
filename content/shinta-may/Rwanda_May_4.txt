*EAC Chief Pledges to Recover Outstanding Member Contributions
April 27, 2026 - The new Secretary General of the East African Community (EAC), Stephen Mbundi, has pledged to ensure timely payment of financial contributions by member states, as the bloc faces persistent budget shortfalls.
Mbundi, who assumed office on April 25, said recovering unpaid dues would be a top priority, noting that delayed or missing contributions have disrupted several of the organization’s activities in recent years.
According to EAC figures, only four of its eight member states have fully met their financial obligations for the 2025/2026 fiscal year. Outstanding arrears include $27 million owed by the Democratic Republic of the Congo, $22.7 million by Burundi, $21.8 million by South Sudan, and $10.5 million by Somalia.
In response to ongoing funding challenges, EAC heads of state agreed on March 7, 2026, to revise the bloc’s contribution model. Under the new framework, 50 percent of the budget will be shared equally among member states, while the remaining 50 percent will be calculated based on each country’s gross domestic product, meaning wealthier economies will contribute more.
Mbundi emphasized that consistent financial contributions are critical to the functioning of the regional body.
“My absolute priority is to ensure that member states meet their financial obligations and pay their contributions on time,” he said.
Beyond budget issues, the Secretary General highlighted the need to boost intra-regional trade by removing barriers such as high tariffs. He also called for increased investment in infrastructure, stressing that future projects must be designed to withstand the impacts of climate change.
The commitments signal a renewed push to strengthen the effectiveness and sustainability of the EAC amid growing regional integration efforts.

*DRC parliamentary report highlights weaknesses in national security forces
Kinshasa, 29 April 2026 - A report by the National Assembly of the Democratic Republic of Congo has raised serious concerns over the capacity of the country’s armed forces and allied groups to effectively secure national territory, citing structural and operational shortcomings.
The findings follow consultations held in December 2025 between the parliamentary committee on defence and security and several key government officials, including ministers responsible for security, defence, finance, public service and mining. The discussions aimed to assess the state of national security amid ongoing conflicts in the eastern regions.
According to the report, the Armed Forces of the Democratic Republic of Congo (FARDC) face multiple challenges, including indiscipline, outdated equipment and infiltration by hostile elements. These weaknesses have reportedly undermined the military’s ability to respond effectively to armed groups, particularly in North and South Kivu.
Officials also highlighted broader security concerns across the country, including the presence of foreign forces in parts of Tanganyika province and local conflicts in northern regions. Authorities warned that without swift and coordinated action, the situation could deteriorate further, with implications for national stability, governance and economic activity.
The defence minister acknowledged recent investments in military technology, including drones and combat aircraft, but noted that opposing forces have adapted with countermeasures, limiting their effectiveness. He emphasized the need for a comprehensive strategy combining military, diplomatic, intelligence and economic approaches, alongside broader reforms within the security sector.
The report further pointed to governance and financial management challenges. Although significant funds were allocated to the military in 2025, not all resources were fully utilized, and some planned expenditures were not executed. Concerns were also raised over delays in the payment of allowances to deployed soldiers.
The role of the Wazalendo militia alliance was also examined. While these groups are seen by some officials as important partners in territorial defence, the report highlighted issues related to their organization, conduct and oversight. Tensions between the militia and regular forces have reportedly increased in recent months.
Authorities indicated that future plans include maintaining cooperation with local armed groups during ongoing operations, while considering long-term measures such as disarmament, reintegration or potential integration into formal security structures.
The report concludes that strengthening coordination, accountability and institutional reform will be critical to improving the country’s overall security posture.

*Rwanda Introduces New Malaria Treatment Strategy to Prevent Drug Resistance
April 28, 2026 - Rwanda’s health authorities have launched a new national strategy aimed at improving malaria treatment and preventing the disease from developing resistance to commonly used drugs.
The Rwanda National Health Commission has introduced the approach to reduce overreliance on a single treatment, particularly Coartem, which has long been the most widely used anti-malaria drug in the country.
Health officials warn that prolonged use of the same medication can lead to resistance by the malaria parasite, reducing the drug’s effectiveness over time.
A doctor at Shyorongi Health Center, Habimana Pierre Celestin, said the new strategy is expected to improve patient recovery rates and strengthen treatment outcomes.
“Before, Coartem did not work like this… patients would return still sick after a week, but now it treats them well and they recover properly,” he said.
The Malaria Prevention and Control Officer at the Rwanda Biomedical Centre, Rwanda Biomedical Centre, Habanabakize Epaphrodite, explained that the country is now implementing a Multiple First-Line Therapies (MFT) approach. Under this system, Rwanda is divided into zones where different malaria drugs are alternated to slow down resistance development.
He said the rotation strategy ensures that the parasite does not adapt to a single treatment, helping maintain long-term effectiveness of available medicines.
According to national health data, Rwanda recorded about 1.2 million malaria cases in 2025, highlighting the continued burden of the disease.
In addition to Coartem, newer treatments such as Dihydroartemisinin-Piperaquine (DHAP) and Artesunate-Pyronaridine (ASPY) were introduced in 2025 to strengthen the country’s malaria response.
Health authorities say the new strategy represents a significant step toward improving malaria control and protecting the effectiveness of treatments in the long term.
