*IMF Reports DRC Structural Reforms Met but Remain Fragile
Kinshasa, February 9, 2026 – The International Monetary Fund (IMF) has presented a generally positive assessment of the Democratic Republic of Congo’s structural reforms, following the second review of the program supported under the Extended Credit Facility (ECF).
The report notes that the majority of structural benchmarks were met on schedule, with seven out of eight benchmarks achieved on time, and the remaining one completed with a slight delay. Additionally, three ongoing benchmarks were also fulfilled.
Key advances highlighted by the IMF include:
Strengthening the expenditure chain
Progressive decentralization of budget allocation
Implementation of instruments to improve monetary policy transmission
Enhanced revenue forecasting from the extractive sector
However, the IMF notes persistent institutional weaknesses and legislative delays, particularly regarding the standardized VAT invoicing system, which was not fully adopted by the planned date despite significant progress.
The report warns that while administrative and executive decisions have accelerated reform, this reliance creates institutional fragility in the absence of a stronger legislative framework. It emphasizes the need to:
Sustainably strengthen public financial management
Limit emergency procedures
Improve control and traceability of public expenditures
The IMF concludes that progress on structural reforms is real and significant, supporting the credibility of the program, but stresses that reforms must be consolidated institutionally to ensure long-term sustainability.
*DRC-US Strategic Partnership Enters Implementation Phase
Kinshasa, February 9, 2026 – The first meeting of the Joint Steering Committee between the Democratic Republic of Congo and the United States, held on February 5, marks a major step in the operationalization of the strategic partnership between the two countries.
Key Developments
The Committee reviewed the initial Strategic Asset Reserve submitted by the Congolese side, which will guide American private sector investments in the DRC. Projects supported by the U.S. International Development Finance Corporation (DFC) are prioritized, and American technical teams will be deployed on the ground to identify areas for specific financial and technical support.
The meeting formally activates the Joint Steering Committee as the central body for implementing the agreement, with a defined timetable and regular sessions.
Political Implications
The partnership is now moving from declarative commitments to a structured, institutional mechanism. The Committee serves as a permanent coordination forum, involving several Congolese ministries and U.S. agencies, reducing dependence on short-term political dynamics and creating a stable framework for collaboration.
Economic Implications
Economically, the focus has shifted from general promotion to presenting specific, bankable assets and projects. The inclusion of technical assistance on the ground and project review mechanisms ensures a move from a logic of intention to a logic of execution. Projects that fail to generate interest are re-evaluated after 18 months, reflecting a pragmatic, market-oriented approach.
Governance of Strategic Sectors
The partnership introduces regular dialogue mechanisms on sensitive economic issues, including cobalt export quotas. Congolese authorities are required to provide periodic briefings to the U.S. Embassy, strengthening transparency, accountability, and internal coordination.
A joint committee for the Grand Inga hydroelectric project highlights the strategic importance of this long-term infrastructure initiative and sets a framework for financial and institutional structuring.
Next Steps
The real impact of the partnership will depend on the ability of Congolese institutions to:
Speak with one voice
Propose bankable projects
Translate joint recommendations into actionable regulatory and fiscal decisions
While this initial meeting does not immediately transform the Congolese economic landscape, it establishes a technical, implementation-focused framework that underpins the future strategic collaboration between the DRC and the United States.
*DRC Remains Among Most Corrupt Countries in Sub-Saharan Africa, Transparency International Reports
Kinshasa, February 10, 2026 – The Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) continues to rank among the worst-performing countries globally in terms of perceived corruption, according to the 2025 Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI) published by Transparency International.
With a score of 20 out of 100, the DRC remains at the bottom of the global ranking, reflecting persistent challenges in governance and public accountability. Sub-Saharan Africa, as a region, also performs poorly, with a regional average of 32 points. Only four of the 49 countries assessed scored above 50.
Transparency International notes that, since 2012, ten countries in the region have seen significant deterioration in corruption perception, while only seven improved, illustrating the ineffectiveness of anti-corruption efforts. The DRC remains stagnant, with no notable improvement in recent years.
The NGO highlights the human impact of corruption, stating that it hinders access to essential services and affects the most vulnerable populations the hardest. It calls on governments to implement strong measures to fight corruption and strengthen democratic governance.
Regional comparisons include:
Angola, score 32, improved 17 points since 2015 but still ranks low
Mozambique, lost 10 points over the past decade
Seychelles, top regional performer with 68 points, facing judicial delays in a money laundering case
Lowest scores: Sudan (14), Eritrea (13), Somalia (9), South Sudan (9)
“Corruption in the public sector always hits the most vulnerable people the hardest,” said Paul Banoba, Regional Advisor for Africa at Transparency International. He urged African governments to translate commitments into decisive action, including strengthening oversight institutions, enhancing transparency, and protecting civic space.

*EGC and Trafigura Launch First Copper and Cobalt Shipment via Lobito Atlantic Railway
Kinshasa, February 13, 2026 – Entreprise Générale du Cobalt (EGC) and Trafigura announced their first shipment of copper and cobalt to international markets through the Lobito Atlantic Railway (LAR), marking a major step in creating a fast, efficient, and traceable mining supply chain from the DRC.
The rail corridor, spanning 1,300 kilometers from the deep-water port of Lobito in Angola to the Congolese border at Luau, with an additional 450 kilometers to Kolwezi in the Copperbelt, is now the shortest route from Kolwezi to an African port, reducing inland transit time to approximately seven days.
Eric Kalala, CEO of EGC, said the shipment reflects progress toward an ethical, traceable, and transparent supply of large-scale artisanal cobalt and copper, facilitated by ARECOMS and the Congolese Ministry of Mines. “The copper shipment is initially destined for the United States, reflecting the strategic partnership between the DRC and the U.S., supported by Gécamines and FOMIN,” he added.
Franck Rogozin, Trafigura’s Head of Metals and Minerals in Africa, emphasized that the partnership strengthens the resilience of the global supply of critical metals. “This collaboration facilitates the responsible transport of copper and cobalt to global markets via the most efficient route, highlighting the importance of strong producer-trader partnerships,” he stated.
Nicholas Fournier, CEO of LAR, described the railway as a regional asset and a catalyst for positioning Angola and the DRC as key suppliers of metals and minerals vital for decarbonization, digitalization, and industrialization.
Owned by Trafigura, Mota-Engil, and Vecturis, LAR recently secured $753 million in financing from the International Development Finance Corporation (DFC) and the Development Bank of Southern Africa (DBSA) for rehabilitation and expansion of the network.
Under existing agreements, Trafigura handles the marketing of cobalt supplied by EGC, which purchases minerals from artisanal producers across the DRC.
About the partners:
Trafigura: A leading employee-owned commodities group operating in over 150 countries, with 14,500 employees. Its portfolio spans metals, minerals, oil, gas, electricity, and renewable energy projects.
EGC: A subsidiary of Gécamines, established in 2019, with an exclusive mandate to purchase, process, and market strategic minerals like cobalt, coltan, and germanium from artisanal mining.
Lobito Atlantic Railway (LAR): Operates a 1,300 km line connecting Lobito port to Luau, plus a 450 km section to Kolwezi, under a 30-year concession. It collaborates with the DRC’s SNCC to modernize and operate the railway section within the DRC.
This milestone positions the DRC as a strategic supplier of critical metals, reinforcing ethical mining standards and enhancing regional export infrastructure.
*IMF Warns of Rising Public Wage Bill as Major Fiscal Risk in DRC
Kinshasa, February 15, 2026 – The International Monetary Fund (IMF) has identified the public wage bill as a major fiscal risk for the Democratic Republic of Congo under the Extended Credit Facility (ECF) program.
According to the IMF’s second review, rapidly increasing salaries in the education, health, and justice sectors are straining budget execution and reducing available fiscal space, particularly in a context already burdened by security expenditures.
The report highlights that the growth of the wage bill stems from salary adjustments, recruitment, administrative regularization, and the continuation of previously undertaken commitments by authorities. This increase has placed pressure on current expenditures in 2024–2025, exceeding initially projected levels.
The IMF warns that the public wage bill is consuming a growing share of budgetary resources, limiting the government’s capacity to fund public investment and social spending, which are priorities under the program. Rising security expenditures further exacerbate this fiscal strain.
For 2025 and 2026, the IMF notes that the widening deficit is partly explained by increased compensation in social and judicial sectors. The fund underscores that this trend, coupled with limited revenue mobilization, heightens the risk of reduced macro-budgetary flexibility.
The IMF calls on the Congolese authorities to:
Contain the growth of the public sector wage bill.
Develop and implement a coherent wage policy.
Strengthen control over civil service size.
Integrate these measures into broader public financial management reforms.
While the IMF did not provide specific timelines or instruments for these reforms, it emphasized that controlling the wage bill is critical for the credibility of the medium-term program and the pursuit of stabilization and development objectives.
Without corrective action, the rigidity of current expenditures could further reduce fiscal space, complicating efforts to maintain macroeconomic stability and support growth.

*Justice at a Standstill in Bulungu – 93 Detainees, Only 25 Convicted
February 12, 2026 – The justice system in Bulungu, Kwilu province, is facing a critical impasse, leaving detainees to die without knowing their fate.
Local sources report that at least five detainees have died since January while awaiting trial, due to a severe shortage of magistrates and the closure of the Bulungu High Court since December 15, 2025.

*Divergent Views on the Humanitarian Situation in Eastern DRC
February 21, 2026 – Goma, North Kivu, DRC – The humanitarian crisis in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo is being framed in starkly contrasting terms by two key actors. Hadja Lahbib, European Commissioner for Crisis Preparedness, Management, and Equality, described the situation as “catastrophic” after her regional tour, while Corneille Nangaa, political coordinator of the AFC/M23, portrayed territories under his control as “the most secure area in the country.”
Hadja Lahbib’s Assessment
During her tour of the DRC, Burundi, and Rwanda, Commissioner Lahbib highlighted severe humanitarian challenges:
Over 21 million people in eastern DRC require emergency aid.
Nearly 28 million people face food insecurity.
Violence continues to cause repeated displacement, human rights violations, conflict-related sexual violence, and disease outbreaks.
Refugee flows into neighboring countries, particularly Burundi, strain limited resources.
Lahbib stressed the urgent need to improve humanitarian access, open safe corridors, and ensure the continuation of basic services.
Corneille Nangaa’s Perspective
In contrast, Nangaa claimed that areas controlled by the AFC/M23 constitute the most secure regions of eastern DRC. He cited:
Territories under his control cover 62,000 km², hosting around 20 million Congolese.
He rejects notions of foreign occupation, asserting, “We are not Rwandans, we are in Congo. A territory cannot be occupied by its own citizens.”
Nangaa pledged that humanitarian access will be guaranteed within M23-controlled zones.
He indicated that catastrophic situations are concentrated in Minembwe, Uvira, Beni, and Ituri, highlighting ongoing attacks and massacres outside his areas of influence.
Conflict Data Highlights
Despite Nangaa’s claims, available data shows continued violence across the region:
Uvira and Fizi plateaus: FARDC, supported by Burundi’s FDNB, launched offensives against M23 positions; Twirwaneho-M23-Red Tabara coalition engaged in multiple clashes.
North Kivu: 52 of 113 clashes documented in January occurred here; Rutshuru, Masisi, and Walikale saw heavy fighting.
Civilians targeted: Drone strikes, grenade attacks, and armed sweeps resulted in dozens of deaths, including in Mpombi, Kirumba, Sange, and other villages.
Uvira recapture: January 2026 operations by FARDC and Wazalendo led to at least 151 civilian deaths.
Refugee crisis: Tens of thousands of Congolese fled to Burundi, exacerbating humanitarian needs.
Analysis
The conflicting narratives underscore a diffuse conflict with no clearly demarcated front lines. While the European Commission emphasizes a dire humanitarian emergency, the AFC/M23 stresses relative security within its territories. Ground reports, however, indicate ongoing violence, civilian casualties, and displacement, casting doubt on claims of stability.
The situation highlights the complexity of eastern DRC: a multi-layered conflict involving government forces, local armed groups, and rebel coalitions, with civilians disproportionately affected.

Conclusion
The period from February 8 to 23, 2026, underscores the Democratic Republic of Congo’s position at a critical juncture, marked by a delicate balance between diplomatic engagement, economic ambition, and persistent humanitarian and security challenges.
Diplomatically and politically, the DRC is actively engaging regional and continental partners to stabilize its eastern provinces. President Félix Tshisekedi’s discussions in Luanda with Angolan and African Union leaders, alongside the acceptance of a regional ceasefire scheduled for February 18, signal a commitment to dialogue. Yet, the process remains fragile: the AFC/M23 continues to denounce what it sees as “diplomatic fragmentation” and alleges ongoing government offensives, highlighting the tenuous nature of peace efforts.
Economically, progress is visible in the formalization of the extractive sector. The first legal artisanal gold exports from Haut-Katanga and the inaugural copper and cobalt shipment via the Lobito Atlantic Railway demonstrate tangible steps toward transparency, traceability, and integration into global markets. At the same time, structural challenges persist. The IMF identifies the public wage bill as a major fiscal risk, while institutional weaknesses and entrenched corruption—as reflected in the DRC’s continued low ranking on the Corruption Perceptions Index—threaten to undermine these gains.
Socially and judicially, reforms aim to strengthen the rule of law, notably through the training of 2,500 new magistrates and the prosecution of law enforcement abuses. Nonetheless, systemic deficiencies remain: regions such as Bulungu illustrate the deadly consequences of judicial inaction, with detainees languishing without trial.
On the security and humanitarian fronts, the situation remains acute. Despite the formal ceasefire, attacks by the ADF and clashes involving AFC/M23 and Wazalendo forces perpetuate instability. The resulting humanitarian crisis is severe, with over 21 million people in need of assistance and thousands displaced, facing critical shortages of food, water, and education.
In conclusion, the DRC is laying the foundations for long-term stability through international partnerships, governance reforms, and sectoral modernization. Yet the immediate well-being of its citizens remains precarious, constrained by corruption, fragile institutions, and unresolved conflict. The coming months will be pivotal in determining whether diplomatic, economic, and institutional initiatives can translate into sustainable peace and tangible prosperity for the Congolese people.
