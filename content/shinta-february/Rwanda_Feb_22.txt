*Nyarugabo Warns Tshisekedi and Ndayishimiye Over Civilian Deaths in Minembwe

Kigali February 11, 2026 - Rwanda’s Minister of Foreign Affairs and Cooperation, Olivier Nduhungirehe, has called on the United Nations Security Council and the African Union to clarify whether they will continue to remain silent over what he described as the unlawful use of mercenaries by the government of the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

*Rwanda Grants Citizenship to 1,500 Foreigners Since 2009
KIGALI, February 12, 2026 - The Rwanda Immigration Service announced that since 2009, Rwanda has granted citizenship to 1,500 foreigners, drawn to the country for its security, peace, cleanliness, and ease of investment.
Rusanganwa Jean Damascène, Director of Citizenship Services, told RBA that the latest round included 101 individuals, with 80 residing in Rwanda and 21 in Rwandan embassies abroad, who will gradually receive their citizenship.
Citizenship is granted based on criteria such as birth in Rwanda, adoption on Rwandan soil, marriage, national interests, special knowledge or talents, large investments, residence in Rwanda, or honorary recognition. Rusanganwa emphasized that naturalized citizens have the same rights and obligations as all Rwandans, without distinctions by ethnic group, noting that the previous categories of Hutu, Tutsi, Twa, and Naturalized no longer apply.
Regarding revocation, Organic Law No. 002/2021.OL of July 16, 2021, allows citizenship to be revoked under four circumstances: use of fraudulent documents, betrayal of the Republic, marriages intended solely to gain citizenship, or behavior contrary to Rwandan values. To date, no naturalized citizen has had their Rwandan citizenship revoked.
The authority to grant Rwandan citizenship lies with the Cabinet, while citizenship granted on the basis of merit is under the discretion of the President of the Republic of Rwanda.

*Before U.S. Congress, Byamungu Says DRC Must Address Internal Failures Instead of Blaming Rwanda
WASHINGTON, D.C., February 11, 2026 - Appearing before members of the U.S. Congress, Umunye-Congo researcher Christian-Géraud Neema Byamungu argued that the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) should not place primary blame on Rwanda for its persistent instability, warning that such an approach risks overlooking the country’s deeper structural problems.
Byamungu told lawmakers that focusing solely on Kigali would be “like treating a disease with the wrong medicine.” While acknowledging accusations that Rwanda has undermined security in eastern Congo, he stressed that multiple armed groups operating within the DRC are major drivers of violence.
“Stopping Rwanda alone will not solve the problem of armed groups in the DRC such as the ADF,” he said, referring to the Allied Democratic Forces, which has been blamed for deadly attacks in the east. “The ADF kills hundreds of people almost every week, but there is little mention of CODECO, Red Tabara, the Wazalendo, or the Mobondo militia near Kinshasa.”
Byamungu further emphasized that externalizing responsibility would not address what he described as chronic poor governance, misappropriation of resources, and weak state institutions. According to him, these longstanding deficiencies have enabled armed groups to flourish for more than two decades.
“It will not solve the problem of poor governance, incompetent institutions, and the systemic weaknesses that allow these groups to grow,” he stated. “If these root causes remain unaddressed, insecurity will return, as it has repeatedly over the past 20 years.”
The researcher also challenged the widely held assumption that precious minerals are the principal driver of violence in the DRC. While acknowledging their role in criminal networks, he argued that many conflicts stem from communal tensions, disputes over traditional leadership, and the rise of self-defense groups formed in response to state failures.
“For many years, insecurity in the DRC has been caused by reasons other than precious metals,” Byamungu said. “Resources are sometimes used for criminal activities, but they are not the primary cause of the crisis.”
He concluded by urging U.S. policymakers to adopt a more comprehensive approach to the Congolese crisis-one that not only seeks to end current conflicts but also prevents future ones. Key stakeholders, he noted, must include civil society organizations, religious institutions, and church leaders, whose involvement he described as essential for sustainable peace.
Byamungu’s remarks come amid heightened diplomatic attention on the Great Lakes region, as Washington reassesses its engagement strategy in Central Africa.

*UN Plans Aerial Reconnaissance Before Peacekeepers Deploy to Uvira
KINSHASA, February 11, 2026 - The United Nations will deploy reconnaissance aircraft before sending peacekeepers to Uvira Territory in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, as part of a phased plan to monitor a ceasefire in the volatile border region.
The announcement was made by Jean-Pierre Lacroix, Under-Secretary-General for Peacekeeping Operations, during his working visit to the country on February 10.
Mandate Backed by the UN Security Council
The move follows the January 2026 withdrawal of fighters from the city of Uvira by the M23/AFC coalition, which requested the deployment of neutral forces to prevent reprisals against civilians by government troops.
The United Nations Security Council has mandated the UN mission MONUSCO to oversee implementation of the peace arrangement - a framework accepted by both the Congolese authorities and the armed group.
Phased Deployment for Security Reasons
According to Lacroix, troops will not be deployed immediately. Instead, reconnaissance aircraft will first collect intelligence on security conditions before personnel are transported.
He warned that sending helicopters without prior assessment could expose peacekeepers to attack.
“We will not send helicopters to take our personnel into areas where there are dangers or where drones are present,” Lacroix said, adding that security guarantees are required before the mission proceeds.
He also called on international partners and the parties involved to ensure the safety of peacekeepers as they begin monitoring the ceasefire.
Flights from Goma
The reconnaissance aircraft are expected to operate from Goma International Airport, currently under the control of the AFC/M23 coalition. The airport has remained closed since January 2025, and UN officials did not confirm whether reopening is imminent.
Gradual Arrival of Peacekeepers
Once aerial surveillance is completed, a small contingent of troops will be deployed to Uvira, with numbers increasing progressively depending on developments on the ground.
The UN says the cautious rollout is intended to prevent incidents and build confidence while stabilizing the region after months of fighting.

*South Africa Announces Withdrawal of Troops from Eastern DRC
KINSHASA, February 10, 2026 - South Africa has announced it will withdraw its troops from the UN peacekeeping mission in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, citing the need to consolidate its military forces, financial constraints, limited operational results, and a renewed focus on domestic priorities.
The troops are part of MONUSCO, where South Africa has maintained between 700 and 800 soldiers, primarily within the Force Intervention Brigade (FIB) - a unit authorized to conduct offensive operations against armed groups.
The withdrawal, announced on February 8, is scheduled to take place gradually from the first quarter of 2026 through December 2026.
While Pretoria has officially framed the move as strategic and financial, critics argue the decision is also influenced by broader political and economic interests, particularly in mineral-rich regions of eastern Congo. South African deployments have largely been concentrated in North Kivu, South Kivu, and Ituri - provinces known for deposits of gold, coltan, gas, and copper.
Since the 1990s, South Africa has publicly justified its military engagements across the continent as part of efforts to restore peace and stability. However, observers note that troop deployments have frequently coincided with areas of significant natural resource wealth.
Allegations have also surfaced over the years accusing certain elements within the mission of involvement in illegal mining activities, including the illicit trade of gold and coltan. Some reports claim that military-controlled airports were used in mineral transactions and that intermediaries facilitated cross-border trading networks. These allegations have not led to formal international prosecutions but continue to fuel debate over the economic dimensions of foreign troop deployments.
South Africa remains one of the largest contributors to MONUSCO’s Force Intervention Brigade, whose mandate differs from traditional UN peacekeeping contingents by allowing proactive combat operations against armed groups.
The gradual withdrawal marks a significant shift in regional security dynamics, raising questions about the future balance of forces in eastern Congo and the long-term sustainability of international stabilization efforts.

*DRC Distributes Cash to Soldiers and Wazalendo Amid Ongoing Battles
South Kivu, DRC - February 21, 2026 - The Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) government has begun distributing cash payments to its national army soldiers and Wazalendo fighters actively engaged against the AFC/M23 coalition in South Kivu province.

Political analysts suggest that Tshisekedi’s strategy-simultaneously escalating military operations while showing readiness for dialogue-is aimed at consolidating the DRC government’s leverage ahead of potential peace talks. They urge regional and international mediators to take concrete steps to curb violence rather than relying on declarations alone.
CONCLUSION
This period was marked by a complex interplay of escalating military operations and high-stakes diplomacy in the Great Lakes region. Although President Félix Tshisekedi of the DRC publicly accepted an Angola-brokered ceasefire scheduled for 18 February 2026, its effectiveness remained uncertain as military actions-including drone strikes and airstrikes-continued against AFC/M23 and Twirwaneho positions. Rwanda expressed skepticism about Kinshasa’s commitment to peace, citing the DRC’s ongoing use of heavy artillery, airstrikes, and foreign mercenaries.
Security concerns remained deep-rooted. Rwanda, supported by members of the U.S. Congress, highlighted the DRC’s alleged continued cooperation with the FDLR, a group linked to the 1994 Genocide against the Tutsi. Kigali stated that its defensive measures would only be lifted once the FDLR is fully dismantled. Meanwhile, the humanitarian situation in South Kivu deteriorated, with Banyamulenge civilians targeted through drone attacks and the burning of homes, prompting condemnation and accusations of genocide from political leaders and community organizations.
Beyond the conflict, Rwanda remained active on the international stage, advancing strategic, technological, and social initiatives:
Strengthened global ties: The opening of a new Danish embassy in Kigali and continued bilateral cooperation with Barbados and Italy.
Technological and social progress: Talks with Russia on developing nuclear energy and the launch of the digital consular platform “Nshuti” to support the Rwandan diaspora.
National unity and reconciliation: Efforts to reintegrate former combatants and genocide convicts, promoting a unified Rwandan identity (“Ndi Umunyarwanda”) to counter lingering genocide ideology.
In conclusion, while Rwanda continues to advance internal development and international partnerships, the regional security landscape remains volatile, characterized by mutual distrust between Kinshasa and Kigali and highlighting the urgent need for impartial international mediation to address the root causes of the violence.
