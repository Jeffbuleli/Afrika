*Burundi Counts the Cost as AFC/M23 Seizes Uvira
Burundian President Évariste Ndayishimiye believed his calculations were solid. After Burundian troops were pushed out of Masisi territory, Bujumbura had reinforced its military posture with long-range heavy weapons and combat drones, hoping to outmaneuver AFC/M23 fighters and restore national prestige shaken by months of fighting on Congolese soil.
When Burundian forces began shelling AFC/M23-held positions in South Kivu Province, including the Kamanyola Center, the objective was clear: retake lost ground, reassert control, and reclaim what officials described as the honor of Burundi.
Instead, the offensive backfired.
In rapid succession, AFC/M23, which had also strengthened its supply lines, pushed back a coalition of forces comprising the Burundian army, the DRC armed forces (FARDC), Wazalendo militias, and the FDLR as they moved on the strategic city of Uvira. As the situation deteriorated, statements from Burundian authorities began to reflect growing anxiety.
On December 10, 2025, Burundi’s Foreign Affairs Minister, Dr. Edouard Bizimana, warned in an interview with Radio France Internationale (RFI) that the fall of Uvira would pose a direct threat to Burundi’s security.
“If Uvira were to be captured, the economic capital would be threatened,” Bizimana said, referring to Bujumbura, located just minutes away. “The movement of goods and people between our two countries would be cut off. With the influx of refugees, our capacity would be strained. This is therefore a direct problem for Burundi.”
That same day, AFC/M23 announced the capture of Uvira.
Within hours, the Burundian government closed the Kavimvira and Vugizo border crossings with the Democratic Republic of Congo, suspending cross-border trade and movement for an indefinite period.
What Did Burundi Lose?
The city of Uvira, covering more than 200 square kilometers, lies along the northern shores of Lake Tanganyika and was home to an estimated 700,000 residents last year. Its proximity-about 30 kilometers-to Bujumbura makes it a vital economic and social hub for both countries.
Following the fighting, thousands of residents fled toward Burundi, mainly through the Kavimvira border, which leads into the Gatumba Zone.
Daily trade between Congolese and Burundians has long depended on Kavimvira. Commonly traded goods include maize, beans, yams, and fish from Lake Tanganyika.
According to Faustin Ndikumana, head of the Burundian civil society organization PARCEM, the economic impact is severe.
“More than 60% of Burundi’s exports go to the DRC,” Ndikumana said. “This activity generates significant foreign exchange. Its disruption will only worsen an already fragile foreign currency market.”
Data from the Central Bank of Burundi show that in 2024, Burundi exported 135,300 tons of goods, of which 69,400 tons-more than half-were destined for the DRC, largely via the Kavimvira border.
Fuel Shortages and Rising Prices
Burundi has long faced chronic shortages of petroleum products, which officials attribute to a lack of foreign exchange and illegal trading networks. Many Burundians previously relied on fuel purchased in South Kivu, where prices were lower, crossing through Kavimvira.
Since the border closure, that lifeline has vanished.
Ndikumana noted that Burundians are now unable to source gasoline and diesel from the DRC, worsening the crisis.
RFI journalist Esdras Ndikumana reported a sharp spike in prices: a 20-liter jerrycan of fuel that previously sold for 150,000 Rwandan francs now costs as much as 400,000 francs. At the same time, the US dollar has surged from 5,400 to 6,500 Rwandan francs, further straining the economy.
Rising Tensions, Uncertain Future
The Burundian government insists it will not tolerate AFC/M23’s control of Uvira, vowing to do everything necessary to safeguard Bujumbura’s security, including the possible use of military options.
For its part, AFC/M23 says it seeks peaceful coexistence with Burundi, not confrontation. The group maintains that it favors good-neighborly relations and claims that, if necessary, it can allow the reopening of borders and the resumption of movement and trade.
As diplomatic tensions rise, Uvira’s capture stands as both a military setback and a major economic shock for Burundi-one whose full consequences are still unfolding.
*“Burundi Has a New Neighbor It Doesn’t Like” - Senator Evode Uwizeyimana Warns After Uvira Falls to AFC/M23
Burundian Senator Evode Uwizeyimana has warned that Burundi must come to terms with AFC/M23 following the group’s capture of Uvira, cautioning that continued confrontation could plunge the country into intractable political, economic, and security problems.
Speaking in an interview aired on National Television, Uwizeyimana said the seizure of Uvira, a strategic city in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), has fundamentally altered Burundi’s regional environment, leaving the country with what he described as a new and unwelcome neighbor.
On December 15, 2025, the senator explained that Uvira’s importance lay in its dual role as both the center of military planning against AFC/M23 and the backbone of Burundi’s cross-border trade.
“All those who were defeated in Goma and Bukavu, including the Governor of Kivu and Wazalendo fighters, were there,” Uwizeyimana said.
According to the senator, the concentration of forces hostile to AFC/M23 in Uvira turned the city into a prime military target.
He said the arrival of the AFC/M23 coalition in Uvira, where it regrouped and prepared new operations, ultimately led to the capture of the city, the neutralization of armed opponents, and the creation of a strategic dilemma for Burundi-one that may force Bujumbura to negotiate directly with the fighters now controlling the area.
“If M23 continues to advance southwards, Burundi would be isolated, with only Tanzania left,” Uwizeyimana warned. “There is also the port of Mpurungu in Zambia, but it is far away. What is clear is that trade between Bujumbura and Uvira shows how much this city means to Burundi.”
Military Setbacks and a Growing Dilemma
Uwizeyimana also pointed to the plight of Burundian soldiers deployed in eastern DRC, describing the situation as increasingly untenable.
“Some Burundian soldiers have returned home, which is good, but others remain trapped in the mountains,” he said. “The question is: where will those who remain go? Burundi will have to reach an agreement with M23 to allow them a way out.”
He stressed that Burundi now faces a harsh reality.
“Burundi today has a new and unfriendly neighbor-one that came to us against our will, because we opposed it, failed to repel it, and could not drive it away,” Uwizeyimana said.
Why Uvira, and Why Now?
Addressing the timing of the offensive, the senator dismissed claims that the capture of Uvira was linked to the peace agreement signed in the United States between Rwanda and the DRC.
Instead, he said AFC/M23 acted after months of careful preparation, fully aware that large numbers of troops and heavy equipment had been deployed in Uvira following its victories in Goma and Bukavu.
Uwizeyimana added that while the DRC government had entered negotiations with the group and agreed to a cessation of hostilities, the failure to implement those agreements convinced AFC/M23 that the talks were yielding no results.
As a result, the group resumed fighting and succeeded in capturing additional territories, including Uvira.
Borders Closed, Tensions Rising
Following the fall of Uvira in South Kivu Province, the Burundian government closed the Kavimvira and Vugizo border crossings with the DRC, halting trade and movement.
The decision came amid an already tense regional climate, after borders between Burundi and Rwanda had also been closed for a prolonged period.
Uwizeyimana’s remarks underscore growing concern within Burundi’s political class that the country is entering a new geopolitical reality, one in which dialogue with AFC/M23 may no longer be optional, but unavoidable.
*AFC/M23 Announces Withdrawal from Uvira in Support of Peace Process
The Alliance Fleuve Congo/March 23 Movement (AFC/M23) has announced a unilateral withdrawal of its forces from the city of Uvira, citing its commitment to ongoing peace efforts in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC).
In a statement dated December 15, 2025, the movement said the decision follows progress made under the Doha Peace Process, including the signing of the Doha Framework Agreement on November 15, 2025. AFC/M23 said the withdrawal was carried out at the request of United States mediation, as a confidence-building measure aimed at giving peace negotiations the best chance of success.
Despite accusing the Congolese army (FARDC) and allied Wazalendo militias of continued provocations and abuses, the group said it chose to pursue de-escalation to help achieve a lasting solution to the conflict.
AFC/M23 called on guarantors of the peace process to put in place measures to secure Uvira, including demilitarization, protection of civilians and infrastructure, and neutral monitoring of the ceasefire.
The group also warned that it would not allow armed groups hostile to the governments of the DRC or Burundi to use areas under its control as rear bases, stressing its commitment to regional stability and good-neighborly relations.
The statement was signed by Corneille Nangaa Yobeluo, Coordinator of the Alliance Fleuve Congo.

*UK Imposes Visa Restrictions on DRC Over Migrant Repatriation
LONDON / KINSHASA - December 28, 2025 - The United Kingdom has imposed visa restrictions on citizens of the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) after the country failed to implement measures to repatriate illegal immigrants and other individuals wanted by UK authorities.
According to the UK government, the DRC did not comply with new asylum rules announced in November 2025. As a result, DRC citizens will no longer have access to fast-track visa services, and dignitaries and politicians from the DRC will no longer receive special entry privileges previously reserved for state leaders.
The UK also reported that Angola and Namibia have agreed to increase efforts to repatriate their citizens, following warnings that non-compliance could lead to similar sanctions. Countries that fail to adhere to the new rules risk denial of visas for their citizens.
Under the new UK measures, asylum seekers will be granted temporary rather than long-term residence, and support for accommodation will be removed. A new entry system limits the number of people who can enter the UK through this route. The UK government had previously warned that countries refusing to accept their nationals could face punitive actions.

*Eastern DRC: Violence Against Civilians Surges in November
Eastern DRC - December 18, 2025 - Violence against civilians in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo saw a sharp increase in November 2025, despite a slight decline in overall security incidents.

Lubero territory in North Kivu, particularly the Bapere sector and Baswagha chiefdom, remains the epicenter of violence, with 121 civilians killed, including 22 women.
The Allied Democratic Forces (ADF) were identified as the deadliest armed group, active in North Kivu and Ituri, while the AFC/M23 coalition continues its expansion to the west and south of the country, despite the recent Doha agreement signed on November 15.

Conclusion
The latter half of December 2025 finds the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) in a state of profound complexity, where significant economic progress and humanitarian efforts are overshadowed by a volatile security landscape and shifting regional geopolitics.
Security and Geopolitics: The fall and subsequent disputed withdrawal of AFC/M23 from the strategic city of Uvira has fundamentally altered the regional power balance. While the rebel group claims a tactical withdrawal in support of peace processes, the Congolese government remains skeptical, viewing these moves as calculated diversions backed by Rwanda. The resulting closure of borders with Burundi has triggered a severe economic shock for the neighbor and left thousands of Congolese refugees in dire humanitarian conditions.
Internal Political Tensions: Domestically, the arrest of senior opposition figure Emmanuel Ramazani Shadary has drawn international criticism, notably from Belgium, and risks further polarizing the political environment at a time when national unity is critical for addressing eastern aggression.
Economic Resilience vs. Human Cost: Paradoxically, while the IMF reports resilient GDP growth exceeding 5% and stabilized inflation, the everyday reality for many Congolese is defined by tragedy and displacement. Deadly shipwrecks, chronic cholera outbreaks in refugee camps, and forced civilian marches in occupied territories highlight the severe human cost of ongoing instability.
Governance and Reform Efforts: Amidst these crises, the government continues to push for structural reforms. Initiatives like the roadmap for human rights in the mining sector, the modernization of public services, and strategic partnerships for digital finance (e.g., Rawbank and Visa) signal a long-term commitment to state efficiency and economic formalization.
Ultimately, the report underscores that while the DRC is making strides in macro-economic stability and governance reform, its future stability hinges on the effective implementation of peace agreements and the restoration of state authority in the East. Without a verifiable cessation of hostilities and a reduction in political friction, the human and social development of the Congolese people remains precarious
