*Diplomacy and Tribal Loyalties Redefine Sudan’s Civil War Frontlines
KHARTOUM, July 1, 2026 - More than three years into Sudan’s civil war, the conflict is increasingly being shaped by political calculations and tribal loyalties rather than military strength alone, as both the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) adjust their strategies to changing realities on and off the battlefield.
For the Sudanese army, preserving international legitimacy has become a growing strategic priority. In recent months, the military has reduced the role of some allied armed groups whose presence has attracted international scrutiny, while strengthening cooperation with local resistance groups and former rebel movements that joined the 2020 Juba Peace Agreement.
Security analysts say the shift reflects an effort by the army to safeguard its international standing amid mounting diplomatic pressure and sanctions targeting some of its former battlefield partners. While the broader coalition has reinforced troop numbers, it has also introduced new challenges related to coordination, command structures, and operational discipline.
On the opposing side, the RSF has experienced a series of high-profile defections involving senior commanders and advisors. Although these defections have provided a political and symbolic boost to the Sudanese military, they have had only a limited impact on the military balance.
Many former RSF commanders have reportedly remained away from active combat zones after switching sides, reflecting the complex tribal and family ties that continue to connect fighters across opposing camps. Analysts note that deep-rooted kinship networks have discouraged direct confrontations between former comrades and relatives, reducing the tactical value of the defections.
As both forces reshape their alliances, the focus of the conflict has increasingly shifted toward securing strategic supply corridors, border crossings, and economically important areas rather than pursuing large-scale territorial advances.
El Obeid has become one of the war's most contested locations, with RSF forces maintaining pressure around the city while the Sudanese army continues to reinforce its defenses. The city remains a critical logistical hub linking central Sudan to the Kordofan region, making its control strategically important for both sides.
Meanwhile, fighting has intensified in South Kordofan and Blue Nile states, where the conflict now involves not only the SAF and the RSF but also the Sudan People's Liberation Movement-North (SPLM-N), creating a complex three-way confrontation.
Military observers say the war has entered a new phase in which controlling supply routes, border regions, and key economic assets has become more important than achieving decisive battlefield victories. As the conflict evolves, localized alliances and regional power dynamics are increasingly shaping the course of the war, while prospects for a comprehensive military resolution remain uncertain.

*Displacement Crisis Deepens in El Obeid as Conflict Tightens Grip on City
EL OBEID, July 10, 2026 - El Obeid, the capital of Sudan’s North Kordofan State, is facing mounting humanitarian pressure as tens of thousands of displaced people continue to arrive despite an ongoing siege and repeated drone attacks.
The city has become a major refuge for civilians fleeing violence in South and West Kordofan, even as it remains surrounded on three sides by the Rapid Support Forces (RSF). The deteriorating security situation has heightened concerns over the safety of residents and the possibility of a broader military offensive.

Recent drone attacks on fuel facilities have further worsened the crisis, causing severe fuel shortages and driving prices sharply higher on the black market.
As conflict continues to disrupt livelihoods and humanitarian access, aid agencies warn that the needs of displaced communities are increasing rapidly, placing additional strain on the city's already overstretched resources.
*Report Accuses Sudan’s Warring Parties of Systematic Air Attacks on Civilians in Darfur
KAMPALA, July 12, 2026 - A new report has accused both the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) of repeatedly carrying out air and drone strikes that have deliberately targeted civilians across Darfur, raising serious concerns over violations of international humanitarian law.
The report documents 17 air and drone attacks between October 2024 and April 2026, alleging that marketplaces, hospitals, wedding celebrations, mosques, and shelters for displaced people were among the locations hit, resulting in hundreds of civilian deaths and injuries.
According to the findings, investigators identified repeated failures by both sides to distinguish between military objectives and civilian sites during military operations.
Among the incidents highlighted are airstrikes that reportedly struck crowded public areas in Nyala and Tora, as well as a drone attack on a mosque in El Fasher during dawn prayers. The report also alleges the use of so-called "double-tap" strikes, in which a second attack follows shortly after an initial strike, hitting first responders and civilians attempting to rescue victims.
The document describes Sudan’s conflict as increasingly dominated by drones and other advanced aerial weapons, arguing that the growing use of unmanned aircraft has become a major driver of civilian casualties.
The report calls on the international community to strengthen enforcement of existing arms embargoes, curb the flow of weapons into the conflict, and provide greater support for efforts to document violations and monitor arms supply networks.
Researchers behind the report said the continued attacks on civilian infrastructure demonstrate a persistent disregard for the laws of armed conflict and stressed the need for accountability through independent investigations and international justice mechanisms.
Conclusion
The developments between July 1 and 13, 2026 illustrate a conflict that is becoming increasingly entrenched, with military, humanitarian, economic, and diplomatic crises reinforcing one another. The war has evolved beyond conventional frontlines into a struggle for strategic towns, transport corridors, and economic assets, while shifting local alliances and repeated territorial gains and losses continue to fuel instability across Darfur, Kordofan, and the Blue Nile region.
The humanitarian toll continues to deepen. Intensified fighting, drone strikes, and restrictions on humanitarian access have displaced thousands more civilians, overwhelmed cities such as El Obeid, and further strained already fragile health, water, and food systems. Reports of attacks on civilian infrastructure, markets, medical facilities, and displacement sites, alongside mounting allegations of violations of international humanitarian law, have heightened concerns over civilian protection and accountability.
Diplomatic efforts have gained momentum but remain uncertain. Sudan has engaged with international peace initiatives, including a U.S.-backed mediation framework, while reaffirming conditions centered on national sovereignty, territorial integrity, and security arrangements. At the same time, the African Union, the United Nations, and other regional and international partners have intensified efforts to revive negotiations, reflecting growing recognition that a military solution alone is unlikely to end the conflict.
International scrutiny has also increased through legal and political actions, including calls for tougher sanctions, investigations into alleged war crimes, and judicial proceedings against senior figures accused of atrocities. These developments underscore the expanding international dimension of the crisis and the growing emphasis on accountability alongside conflict resolution.
Meanwhile, Sudan's economy remains under severe pressure. Inflation, disrupted trade, declining agricultural production, and damaged infrastructure continue to undermine livelihoods despite government measures to stabilize the currency, support farmers, modernize strategic infrastructure, and strengthen regional economic partnerships.
Taken together, these developments point to a country facing a prolonged and multidimensional crisis. Without sustained progress toward a nationwide ceasefire, expanded humanitarian access, meaningful political dialogue, and economic recovery, Sudan is likely to remain caught in a cycle of violence, displacement, and institutional fragility with consequences extending well beyond its borders.
