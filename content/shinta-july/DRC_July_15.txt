*AFC/M23 Pursues Territorial Expansion and Political Recognition, UN Report Says
Kinshasa, July 2, 2026 — The AFC/M23 armed movement continues to pursue its political and military ambitions in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo, despite ongoing diplomatic efforts aimed at ending the conflict and restoring stability in the region.
According to a new assessment by the United Nations Group of Experts on the DRC, the movement, which controls significant areas of North Kivu and South Kivu provinces, is seeking to strengthen its territorial influence, establish parallel governance structures, and obtain official recognition through political negotiations.
The report indicates that the group’s long-term objective remains the removal of the Congolese government, while also considering the creation of an autonomous entity in eastern DRC as an alternative scenario. The movement has reportedly promoted the idea of a “Federal Republic of Congo” for territories under its control, accompanied by the establishment of its own security and defense structures.
The experts note that AFC/M23 leaders have shifted their position on the future of their fighters, moving away from previous calls for integration into the Congolese armed forces (FARDC). Instead, the group is reportedly seeking to form and lead the armed forces of a future federal entity.
The report also highlights the increasing use of narratives related to the protection of Tutsi, Banyamulenge, and Hema communities from alleged ethnic violence as part of the movement’s public justification for its military actions and political demands.
Despite international calls for the withdrawal of foreign forces and the cessation of external support to armed groups, the UN assessment states that AFC/M23 has continued expanding its areas of influence. The movement has reportedly intensified military operations with ambitions to extend its control toward strategic locations, including Walikale, Kisangani, Kalemie, and Beni.
The experts further indicate that since the launch of the Doha peace process in 2025, the territory controlled by AFC/M23 has significantly increased, raising concerns over the effectiveness of diplomatic initiatives and existing security commitments.
The publication of the report comes as regional and international mediation efforts continue to face major challenges. The implementation of agreements supported by international partners remains difficult amid persistent disagreements between Kinshasa and Kigali over security issues and political commitments.
Meanwhile, negotiations between the Congolese government and AFC/M23 under the Doha framework have yet to resolve key points of disagreement. Recent diplomatic meetings, including talks aimed at revitalizing the process, have failed to produce major breakthroughs, while worsening international crises have further slowed efforts to achieve a lasting settlement.
*Russia Raises Alarm Over Drone Warfare and Alleged Foreign Mercenary Presence
New York, July 4, 2026 — Russia has expressed concern over the changing nature of the conflict in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo, particularly the growing use of armed drones and reports of foreign fighters allegedly involved in training and drone operations.
Speaking during a United Nations Security Council briefing on the security situation in eastern DRC, Russia’s representative to the UN, Anna Evstigneeva, warned that the transformation of military tactics is increasing risks for civilians, humanitarian workers, and international personnel operating in conflict zones.
According to the Russian diplomat, the widespread use of combat drones has significantly altered the dynamics of the conflict, with attacks increasingly threatening civilian populations and aid operations.
Evstigneeva referred specifically to a drone strike in Goma on March 11 that resulted in the death of a UNICEF staff member. She also raised concerns over reports that foreign mercenaries with battlefield experience from Ukraine were allegedly being recruited as instructors and drone operators in the eastern DRC conflict.
Beyond drone warfare, Russia also highlighted the threat posed by the Allied Democratic Forces (ADF), describing the armed group as a major security concern due to its alleged links with international extremist networks.
Moscow called for stronger regional and international cooperation to address the threat posed by the ADF, emphasizing the need for coordinated action involving countries in the Great Lakes region and international partners.
The Russian position comes as insecurity continues to affect eastern DRC despite ongoing diplomatic initiatives, including the Washington Agreement and the Doha peace process. Tensions between Kinshasa and Kigali remain unresolved, with both sides maintaining different interpretations of the commitments made under the peace frameworks.
Negotiations between the Congolese government and AFC/M23 under the Doha process have also struggled to achieve significant progress, with disagreements persisting over key political and security issues. Recent diplomatic efforts have failed to produce the expected breakthroughs, while broader international crises have further slowed mediation efforts.
As fighting continues, regional and international actors are increasing calls for all parties to respect peace commitments. However, the gap between diplomatic agreements and developments on the ground remains a major obstacle to achieving a lasting resolution to the conflict in eastern DRC.

*DRC Uses UN Security Council Presidency to Push Global Action Against Conflict-Related Sexual Violence
New York, July 7, 2026 – The Democratic Republic of Congo is preparing to use its presidency of the United Nations Security Council to place the protection of civilians, the fight against impunity, and the Women, Peace and Security agenda at the center of international discussions.
For the first time in more than three decades, Kinshasa is holding the rotating presidency of the Security Council, which began on July 1, 2026. The government intends to use this diplomatic platform to highlight issues drawn from its own experience with prolonged conflict, particularly in eastern DRC.
As part of this agenda, Prime Minister Judith Suminwa will chair a high-level public debate in New York on July 8 under the theme: “Women, Peace and Security – Honoring the Promise of International Law to Survivors of Conflict-Related Sexual Violence.”
The meeting comes amid renewed concerns over conflict-related sexual violence in several regions worldwide, including eastern Democratic Republic of Congo, where civilians continue to face serious security challenges.
According to the Congolese government, the debate aims to give greater visibility to survivors and encourage the international community to transform existing commitments into concrete measures. Discussions are expected to focus on strengthening accountability mechanisms, improving access to healthcare and justice for victims, expanding reparations programs, and ensuring long-term reintegration support.
Kinshasa also intends to highlight the links between sexual violence, conflict economies, and the illegal exploitation of natural resources, arguing that these factors contribute to the continuation of instability in conflict-affected areas.
The debate will mark the beginning of a broader diplomatic initiative focused on peace, security, and natural resource governance. A meeting under the Arria formula is scheduled for July 13 to examine the relationship between natural resources and conflict, followed by a high-level debate on July 21 dedicated to “Natural Resource Governance: The Foundation of Peace, Security, and Prosperity.”
Through its Security Council presidency, the DRC says it aims to contribute its experience to global discussions on peacebuilding, justice, and civilian protection while advocating for stronger international responses to crises.
The country joined the United Nations Security Council as one of five new non-permanent members for the 2026–2027 term, alongside Bahrain, Colombia, Latvia, and Liberia. Although elected members do not hold veto power, they participate fully in negotiations and voting processes and can influence the Council’s agenda and resolutions.
During its mandate, the DRC is also part of the A3 group, which brings together African countries serving on the Security Council. This framework allows coordination with other African members and contributes to the representation of African Union positions on issues affecting the continent.
*DRC Strengthens Anti-Corruption Framework as Government and Private Sector Join Forces on New Law
Kinshasa, July 8, 2026 – The Democratic Republic of Congo is advancing efforts to strengthen its legal framework against corruption, with the government and private sector reaffirming their commitment to closer cooperation in promoting transparency and good governance.
A working session held in Kinshasa on July 7 brought together the Ministry of Justice, the United Nations Global Compact Network in the DRC, and private sector representatives under the leadership of Minister of State, Minister of Justice and Keeper of the Seals, Guillaume Ngefa.
The meeting focused on the latest developments in the draft anti-corruption law recently adopted by the Council of Ministers, including technical adjustments made to the text and preparations for its broader dissemination among stakeholders.
Jessica Webe, Executive Director of the UN Global Compact Network DRC, welcomed the progress achieved, noting that around 75 percent of recommendations submitted by the organization's anti-corruption commission had been integrated into the draft law following months of consultations with government authorities.
Private sector representatives also expressed support for the reform. Hugues Boketsu Efole, Deputy Managing Director of Equity BCDC and head of the UN Global Compact's anti-corruption commission on a rotating basis, said businesses are committed to contributing to the creation of a more transparent economic environment and strengthening trust between institutions and companies.
As part of the implementation process, stakeholders announced plans to launch awareness campaigns targeting businesses and civil society organizations to promote understanding of the new legal framework and encourage effective compliance.
Adopted by the Council of Ministers on June 26 under the chairmanship of President Félix-Antoine Tshisekedi Tshilombo, the draft law seeks to modernize the country's approach to fighting corruption through four main pillars: prevention, protection, prosecution, and judicial procedures.
The proposed legislation also introduces measures on international cooperation, recovery of assets obtained through illicit activities, and obligations for companies to establish internal mechanisms to prevent and combat corruption.
The reform comes as the DRC continues to face significant governance challenges, with corruption remaining a major concern in public administration and economic sectors. Authorities argue that the new legal framework will help strengthen accountability mechanisms and improve the country's business climate.
Following its adoption by the government, the bill will now proceed to Parliament for examination by the National Assembly and the Senate before being submitted for presidential promulgation.
Since President Félix Tshisekedi took office, several institutional reforms have been launched, including efforts to strengthen oversight bodies such as the General Inspectorate of Finance (IGF) and the Court of Auditors. However, civil society organizations and economic experts continue to call for stronger enforcement mechanisms and more binding measures to achieve lasting progress in the fight against corruption.

*DRC Joins Egmont Group, Strengthening Fight Against Financial Crime and Boosting International Confidence
Baku, July 10, 2026 – The Democratic Republic of Congo has reached a major milestone in its efforts to strengthen financial governance and combat illicit financial activities with the admission of its National Financial Intelligence Unit (CENAREF) into the Egmont Group, a global network of financial intelligence units.
The decision was announced during the Egmont Group General Assembly held in Baku, Azerbaijan, where members formally approved the DRC’s entry into the organization. The move marks international recognition of reforms undertaken by Kinshasa to improve its anti-money laundering, counter-terrorist financing, and counter-proliferation financing systems.
The Congolese government considers this membership a significant step in restoring confidence in the country’s financial institutions and aligning national mechanisms with international standards on transparency and financial integrity.
According to the Ministry of Finance, the reform process has been ongoing since 2019 and was strengthened from 2024 through coordination between government institutions, including the Ministry of Finance, CENAREF, and other actors involved in financial oversight.
Finance Minister Doudou Fwamba Likunde Li-Botayi said the admission reflects the progress made by the DRC in improving its credibility within the international financial system and demonstrates the relevance of reforms aimed at strengthening governance and cooperation.
Through its membership in the Egmont Group, CENAREF will gain access to secure information-sharing mechanisms with more than 170 financial intelligence units worldwide. This cooperation is expected to facilitate investigations into money laundering, terrorist financing, illicit financial flows, criminal assets, and the identification of beneficial owners behind financial operations.
Beyond technical cooperation, authorities view the membership as a positive signal for international partners and investors. The move is expected to contribute to improving the country’s business climate, strengthening economic transparency, and enhancing the attractiveness of the Congolese economy.
The development comes as the DRC continues efforts to complete its exit process from the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) list of jurisdictions subject to enhanced monitoring. Authorities say joining the Egmont Group demonstrates the country’s commitment to implementing structural reforms aimed at protecting the integrity of its financial system.
The government has reaffirmed its intention to continue modernizing financial governance mechanisms, improving transparency, and building a more resilient financial sector capable of supporting sustainable economic growth and restoring international confidence in the Democratic Republic of Congo.

Addis Ababa, July 11, 2026 – Congolese Senator Salomon Kalonda Idi Della has called for stronger protections for democratic processes in Africa, warning against what he described as the growing risk of “constitutional coups” during a parliamentary forum on intelligence, security, and electoral integrity held in Ethiopia.
Speaking before the Ethiopian Parliament, the senator from Haut-Katanga used the situation in the Democratic Republic of Congo as a case study, arguing that ongoing discussions around constitutional reform could potentially open the way for President Félix Tshisekedi to seek a third term, which he considers contrary to constitutional principles.

“There are no bad coups on one side and good coups on the other,” Kalonda argued, calling for equal international attention to all forms of unconstitutional changes of power.
Beyond the Congolese context, the senator highlighted broader challenges affecting electoral credibility across Africa. He pointed to concerns over the independence of electoral commissions, the use of judicial institutions to exclude political candidates, and restrictions on internet access during electoral result announcements.
According to Kalonda, these practices weaken public confidence in electoral systems and contribute to political instability on the continent.
To address these challenges, the Congolese senator proposed several measures, including the creation of a permanent African electoral monitoring mechanism, greater investment in digital electoral sovereignty, transparent and inclusive electoral audits, and a parliamentary observation framework covering the entire electoral cycle from voter registration to the announcement of final results.
He also linked democratic governance to economic competition, particularly in the mining sector, arguing that weak institutions and limited accountability can affect the ability of African countries to negotiate fairly with international economic partners.
Through his intervention in Addis Ababa, Kalonda called on African institutions and international actors to strengthen democratic safeguards and ensure that electoral processes remain transparent, credible, and inclusive.
*Internal Rivalries and Power Struggles Expose Deep Divisions Within AFC/M23
Kinshasa, July 11, 2026 — Internal tensions over political ambitions, military strategy, and control of resources are increasingly dividing the Congo River Alliance/March 23 Movement (AFC/M23), according to findings highlighting growing disagreements within the armed group operating in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo.
The leadership structure of the AFC/M23 remains marked by a fragile balance between its political and military wings. The movement’s military command continues to be attributed to General Sultani Makenga, although his influence has reportedly faced increasing challenges from within the organization and from external actors. Political leadership remains associated with Bertrand Bisimwa and Corneille Nangaa, the former president of the Independent National Electoral Commission (CENI).
The group’s leaders have continued to benefit from support and guidance from Rwandan authorities and intelligence structures, according to the findings. However, beyond external backing, internal divisions have become more visible, particularly over the movement’s objectives and future direction.
Political ambitions appear to be one of the main sources of disagreement. Figures such as Corneille Nangaa and former Congolese President Joseph Kabila have reportedly expressed ambitions linked to gaining influence over national power in Kinshasa, while several senior M23 commanders have opposed expanding military operations beyond North and South Kivu.
Disputes over the management and distribution of resources have also fueled tensions among senior officials. Some members of the movement have reportedly raised concerns about perceived preferential treatment benefiting certain Tutsi officers and fighters, adding another layer of friction within the organization.
Militarily, the AFC/M23 territories under its control have reportedly been reorganized into three main defense zones. The first zone, covering Nyiragongo, Rutshuru, and Lubero territories, is led by General Baudoin Ngaruye. The second zone, covering Masisi and Walikale, is commanded by Brigadier General Justin Gacheri Musanga, while General Innocent Byamungu oversees operations in South Kivu.
The movement’s armed forces are estimated at around 30,000 fighters. This force reportedly includes former members of the National Congress for the Defence of the People (CNDP), fighters from the original M23 movement, new recruits mobilized since 2021, and individuals coming from diaspora communities and refugee camps in Rwanda.
The ranks have also reportedly expanded through the integration of former members of the Congolese army (FARDC), the Congolese National Police, Wazalendo fighters, local defense groups, and newly established police units, following battlefield developments and the capture of strategic areas in eastern DRC.
These developments come as diplomatic efforts aimed at restoring peace in eastern Congo continue to face major obstacles. Despite the signing of the Washington Agreement and several follow-up discussions, insecurity and humanitarian challenges remain severe, while disagreements between Kinshasa and Kigali continue to complicate implementation of the commitments.
The Doha peace process, facilitated by Qatar, has also struggled to achieve a breakthrough. Several rounds of negotiations between the Congolese government and the AFC/M23 have failed to resolve key disagreements, while additional mediation efforts, including talks held in Switzerland, have yet to produce significant progress.
As diplomatic initiatives slow down and security conditions deteriorate, the future of eastern DRC remains uncertain, with political rivalries, military fragmentation, and competition over strategic resources continuing to shape the conflict landscape.

Through this new directive, Kinshasa aims to reinforce state authority over natural resources, improve the credibility of the mining sector, and align the country’s mineral governance with international standards at a time when global demand for strategic minerals continues to grow.

*Stable Congolese Franc Offers Economic Relief and Strengthens Confidence in Local Markets
Kinshasa, July 14, 2026 – The relative stability of the Congolese franc against foreign currencies is increasingly becoming a key factor influencing the daily economic realities of households, businesses, and consumers across the Democratic Republic of Congo.
Although exchange rate figures published daily by the Central Bank of Congo (BCC) may appear to be technical data reserved for economists and financial institutions, they directly affect the prices of goods, household purchasing power, and business decisions.
The latest indicative exchange rates published by the BCC show that the Congolese franc is operating in a more stable monetary environment, a development that authorities and economic observers consider important for controlling inflation and improving economic predictability.
A stable exchange rate matters because a significant portion of goods consumed in the country depend on imports or foreign currency transactions. Fuel, medicines, construction materials, spare parts, industrial equipment, electronic products, and some food items are often affected by movements in the dollar exchange rate.
When the dollar rises sharply against the Congolese franc, import costs increase, and businesses frequently pass these additional expenses on to consumers through higher prices. Conversely, greater exchange rate stability allows companies to better anticipate costs and avoid frequent price adjustments.
The role of the Central Bank is not to impose a fixed exchange rate but to maintain an orderly market through monetary policy tools. These include managing liquidity in circulation, adjusting interest rates, intervening in the foreign exchange market when necessary, and managing foreign exchange reserves.
The objective is to limit excessive fluctuations that could destabilize economic activity while maintaining confidence among households, investors, and businesses.
The daily publication of indicative exchange rates also contributes to greater transparency in the currency market. While banks and authorized exchange offices may apply slightly different rates, the official benchmark provides citizens and economic operators with a reference point for comparing offers and making better-informed decisions.
This transparency helps reduce information gaps between market participants and encourages healthier competition among foreign exchange operators. With a publicly available reference rate, excessive margins become easier to identify, pushing financial institutions and exchange bureaus to offer more competitive conditions.
For businesses, currency stability provides greater visibility when planning investments, importing goods, signing contracts, or managing operational costs. Predictable exchange conditions reduce uncertainty and allow companies to develop longer-term strategies.
Economists also emphasize the importance of expectations in maintaining monetary stability. When businesses and households believe that a currency will lose value rapidly, they may rush to purchase foreign currency, contributing to additional pressure on the exchange rate.
By contrast, confidence in the ability of monetary authorities to preserve stability can reduce speculative behavior and reinforce market confidence.
The benefits of a stable Congolese franc extend across the economy. For households, it helps preserve purchasing power and improve visibility over daily expenses. For traders, it facilitates stock management and pricing decisions. For companies, it supports investment planning. For financial institutions and public authorities, it contributes to a more predictable economic environment.
However, maintaining this stability will depend on the continuation of consistent macroeconomic policies and the confidence of economic actors in the national currency.
Beyond exchange rate figures, the stability of the Congolese franc reflects a broader economic challenge: building lasting trust between citizens, businesses, and institutions. A currency gains strength not only through monetary policies but also through the confidence placed in it by the society it serves.

Representing Beni Territory, Batsotsi conveyed the concerns of local communities affected by repeated attacks and urged the government to ensure that military operations are backed by adequate funding, logistics, and infrastructure.
He stressed that military campaigns cannot succeed without sufficient operational resources, expressing confidence that the government would provide the army with the support needed to sustain operations on the ground.
During the meeting, General Ychaligonza reportedly outlined the implementation of an integrated interprovincial operational plan designed to strengthen coordination among security forces across provinces affected by ADF activities. The strategy aims to improve joint military operations and enhance efforts to neutralize the armed group.
According to the lawmaker, the senior military officer also reaffirmed the General Staff's commitment, under the leadership of President Félix Tshisekedi, to restoring the country's territorial integrity and addressing ongoing security threats.
Batsotsi further emphasized the importance of strengthening cooperation between civilians and the military, describing public support as a key element in efforts to combat both the ADF insurgency and broader security challenges in eastern DRC.
He also called for stricter military discipline, criticizing the existence of illegal checkpoints allegedly established by some soldiers in operational areas. Such practices, he argued, restrict civilian movement and undermine public confidence in the armed forces, stressing that professionalism and discipline remain essential to the success of military operations against armed groups.
Conclusion
The first half of July 2026 underscores the complex and multifaceted challenges confronting the Democratic Republic of Congo, revealing a persistent gap between notable diplomatic advances, institutional reforms, and the difficult realities unfolding on the ground.
On the international stage, the DRC continued to strengthen its diplomatic positioning by leveraging its rotating presidency of the United Nations Security Council to advance discussions on conflict-related sexual violence and promote natural resource governance as a key pillar of sustainable peace. At the national level, Kinshasa pursued institutional consolidation through reforms aimed at strengthening anti-corruption mechanisms, enhancing financial transparency with CENAREF’s strategic integration into the Egmont Group, and promoting economic participation through the implementation of the Local Content Law.
Nevertheless, these political and diplomatic achievements remain overshadowed by the worsening security and humanitarian situation in eastern DRC. Despite ongoing mediation efforts, including the Doha peace process and the Washington Agreement framework, armed violence continues to intensify, with the AFC/M23 maintaining territorial influence through external support and benefiting from illicit mineral networks operating across borders. Internal tensions within the movement, combined with the continued activity of other armed groups such as the ADF, further complicate efforts to restore stability, while civilians remain exposed to displacement, human rights violations, and public health emergencies, including the ongoing Ebola outbreak.
At the same time, the DRC has continued to reinforce its image as a strategic actor in global climate and resource governance, highlighting its role as a “solution country” through initiatives focused on forest conservation, sustainable mining, and energy development. However, translating this international recognition into lasting domestic transformation remains the central challenge.
Ultimately, the first half of July demonstrates that the DRC’s future trajectory will depend on its ability to bridge the gap between diplomatic commitments and realities on the ground. Sustainable peace and development will require not only stronger security capabilities and effective enforcement of state authority, but also deeper reforms in governance, responsible management of natural resources, and renewed confidence between institutions, communities, and international partners.
