*Army Chief Al-Burhan Retires 21 Generals in Major Military Reshuffle
Khartoum, April 11, 2026 - Sudan’s military leadership has undergone a significant restructuring after army chief Abdel Fattah al-Burhan ordered the retirement of 21 senior officers and the promotion of others, in what authorities described as a routine administrative measure.
According to a statement from the military, the reshuffle affects officers holding the rank of major general, including prominent figures such as Diab Badr Diab, Khalid al-Qadi, Qandeel Bashir, and Khalid Abdel Mutaal. The army said the decisions were taken in accordance with institutional regulations governing promotions and retirements within the armed forces.
However, the timing of the move has sparked criticism in some political and military circles. Observers noted that several of the retired officers had played key roles in the ongoing conflict against the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), particularly within the framework of what the military refers to as the “Battle of Dignity.”
The reshuffle marks the second major reorganization of the military hierarchy since the outbreak of the conflict, following a similar wave of promotions and retirements in August 2025.
The changes come shortly after a series of internal disciplinary measures. Earlier in the week, Major Sir al-Khatim al-Salmabi was dismissed from the army’s media wing after publishing inaccurate information regarding a senior command appointment.
The restructuring also follows a broader overhaul of the General Staff. Under the new configuration, Yasser al-Atta was appointed Chief of Staff, while the positions of deputy and assistant commanders were abolished.
As part of the reorganization, several senior figures were reassigned to strategic roles. Shams al-Din Kabbashi was appointed assistant for construction and strategic planning, Ibrahim Jaber took on responsibilities for international relations and military cooperation, and Mirghani Idris was named assistant for military industries.
The developments highlight ongoing efforts by Sudan’s military leadership to consolidate command structures and adapt to the evolving dynamics of the conflict, now entering its third year.
*Sudan Crisis “Internationalized” as Berlin Conference Seeks Aid Mobilization and De-escalation
Addis Ababa, April 12, 2026 - The conflict in Sudan has taken on an increasingly international dimension, according to regional and global actors preparing for the Berlin conference aimed at mobilizing humanitarian support and reducing hostilities.
A five-member mechanism comprising the African Union, the United Nations, the European Union, the Arab League, and the Intergovernmental Authority on Development (IGAD) is leading efforts to address the crisis between the Sudanese army and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF).
Speaking ahead of the conference, Lawrence Korbandy warned that the Sudanese conflict now extends beyond national borders, with significant implications for international humanitarian law and regional stability.
He noted that the Berlin meeting is part of a broader series of diplomatic initiatives focused on addressing the worsening humanitarian situation while building political momentum for de-escalation. However, he acknowledged that global attention has been diverted by other geopolitical tensions, limiting the visibility of the Sudan crisis despite its scale.
Korbandy emphasized that while humanitarian assistance is a central priority, it cannot be entirely separated from the political dynamics of the conflict. He called for a balanced approach that reduces excessive politicization, which he said risks prolonging the war.
The envoy also highlighted the need to unify multiple international initiatives, warning that fragmented efforts could delay meaningful progress in a context where civilians continue to bear the brunt of daily violence.
Despite international engagement, he stressed that a lasting solution must ultimately come from within Sudan. He urged domestic actors to prioritize national interests, while calling on external partners to align their efforts in support of peace.
Korbandy indicated that IGAD is preparing to resume operations in Khartoum, reaffirming the organization’s role as a neutral mediator despite past tensions with Sudanese authorities.
He also cautioned that the prolonged conflict could lead to severe consequences, including the potential fragmentation or collapse of the Sudanese state if no resolution is found.
The Berlin conference is expected to focus on humanitarian access, coordination of aid, and diplomatic pathways to reduce violence, as the international community seeks to respond to one of Africa’s most pressing crises.

*Inflation Eases to 40.22% in March Amid Declining Purchasing Power
Khartoum, April 16, 2026 - Sudan’s annual inflation rate slowed to 40.22% in March, marking a sharp decline from 56.39% recorded in February, according to official data released by the national statistics authority.
Despite the drop, the easing reflects a slower pace of price increases rather than a reduction in the cost of living. Economists point to weakening purchasing power as the primary driver, as households struggle with rising prices and a depreciating national currency.
The slowdown was observed across both urban and rural areas. Inflation in cities fell to 41.33% from 66.98% the previous month, while rural inflation declined to 39.53%, down from 50.86%.
The country’s inflation rate has been on a downward trajectory since reaching a peak of 422% in July 2021. However, the broader economic environment remains strained, with conflict continuing to disrupt production, trade, and income generation.
Data collection challenges persist in conflict-affected regions. Price figures for some states, including North and Central Darfur, were estimated due to limited field access caused by ongoing insecurity.
Inflation trends varied across the country, declining in most states while remaining stable or rising in others. The highest increase was recorded in Blue Nile state, while the most significant declines were observed in North Darfur, Al-Jazirah, and Khartoum.
Food and beverages remain the largest component of household spending, accounting for more than half of total consumption. Housing-related costs and transport also represent significant shares of expenditure.
For many Sudanese, the economic reality remains difficult. With livelihoods disrupted and job opportunities limited, households continue to face mounting pressure as the cost of essential goods and services rises in a fragile economic context shaped by prolonged conflict.

Conclusion
The period between April 12 and 29, 2026, highlights a Sudan caught between intensified internal restructuring and a complex international diplomatic landscape. Domestically, the Sudanese military has undergone significant leadership changes aimed at consolidating command as the conflict enters its third year. Simultaneously, political alliances such as the Democratic Block are attempting to broaden their national representation to establish a viable path toward stability.
On the international stage, the crisis has become increasingly "internationalized," with major conferences in Berlin and Antalya seeking to mobilize humanitarian aid and build political momentum for de-escalation. While civilian groups have successfully adopted a joint peace roadmap, the exclusion of government authorities from key forums remains a point of contention that may challenge the effectiveness of these initiatives.
Economically, the country remains in a fragile state despite a slowing inflation rate and surging artisanal gold revenues. Massive industrial damage, a looming collapse of the strategic sugar sector, and a sharp depreciation of the national currency continue to erode the quality of life for the Sudanese population. These economic hardships are further exacerbated by a collapsing healthcare system, now struggling to contain simultaneous outbreaks of dengue fever and measles.
Ultimately, while the introduction of new UN sanctions and the defection of key paramilitary commanders signal shifting dynamics, a lasting resolution remains dependent on unifying fragmented international efforts and prioritizing national interests over external interference. The restoration of state institutions and the successful re-engagement with global financial entities like the World Bank will be critical prerequisites for any meaningful reconstruction and long-term peace.
