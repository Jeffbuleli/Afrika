*Tshisekedi calls for cooperation between provincial institutions amid recurring governance tensions
Bandundu March 25, 2026 - President Félix Tshisekedi has urged stronger cooperation and accountability between provincial governments and provincial assemblies in the Democratic Republic of Congo, warning against institutional rivalry and political instability that continues to disrupt governance at the provincial level.
Speaking at the opening of the 13th Conference of Governors held on March 24 in Bandundu (Kwilu Province), the Head of State stressed that decentralization was intended to bring governance closer to citizens, not to create competing or paralyzed institutions.
He emphasized that provincial oversight by assemblies is legitimate and necessary in a democracy, but warned it should not be used for political pressure, personal interests, or actions that undermine public service delivery.
“The provinces need institutions that cooperate, respect each other, and hold each other accountable within the framework of the law,” President Tshisekedi said, adding that institutional balance is essential for effective governance and development.
He noted that persistent conflicts between provincial executives and assemblies have repeatedly undermined policy implementation, particularly in key sectors such as agriculture, livestock, fisheries, and rural development.
According to the President, no serious development agenda can succeed in an environment marked by political instability, institutional confrontation, or unclear division of responsibilities.
The speech comes against a backdrop of recurring tensions since the 2006 Constitution established decentralization, with provinces frequently experiencing disputes over governance, oversight, and leadership accountability.
Government officials have previously attempted to curb instability, including directives from the Interior Ministry aimed at temporarily suspending motions and petitions in provincial assemblies to restore order during periods of heightened political tension.
However, critics argue that such measures do not address the underlying causes of institutional conflict and call for deeper reforms to strengthen governance and clarify the balance of power within decentralized structures.
*AfDB pledges $80 million to support long-delayed national census in the DRC
Kinshasa March 25, 2026 - The African Development Bank (AfDB) has committed $80 million to support the Democratic Republic of Congo’s second General Population and Housing Census (RGPH-2), a major statistical exercise aimed at updating national demographic data nearly four decades after the last census.
The announcement was made during a donor roundtable focused on mobilizing financing for the nationwide operation, which the government estimates will cost around $192 million in total.
According to AfDB officials, the pledged funding will be split into two components: $50 million dedicated to field operations and $30 million to strengthen national statistical institutions, including the National Institute of Statistics (INS) and other agencies involved in planning, budgeting, and monitoring.
The AfDB described the initiative as a “historic” step for the country, noting its experience in supporting large-scale statistical operations across Africa. The institution also indicated that additional funding could be considered pending internal approval.
The Congolese government is seeking to establish a “basket fund” mechanism to pool contributions from multiple development partners in order to ensure coordinated and transparent financing of the census process.
Authorities say the census is critical for improving development planning, reducing inequalities, and supporting progress toward the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), which are approaching the 2030 deadline.
The last full population census in the DRC dates back to 1984, leaving major gaps in demographic and socio-economic data used for national policy design.
Officials argue that the absence of reliable population data has hindered effective governance and resource allocation, making the upcoming census a key national priority and a long-standing political commitment.
*CENCO warns constitutional revision is possible but risky amid DRC’s security crisis
Kinshasa March 26, 2026 - The National Episcopal Conference of Congo (CENCO) has stated that while revising the Constitution of the Democratic Republic of Congo is legally possible, it would be risky under the country’s current security and political conditions.
Speaking during a press conference following its extraordinary plenary session held from March 23 to 25 in Kinshasa, CENCO leaders expressed concern over worsening insecurity, persistent instability, and a deepening humanitarian crisis affecting the population.
CENCO Secretary General Donatien Nshole reiterated the Church’s position that constitutional revision is allowed under existing legal procedures, but warned that opening such a process in the current climate could be dangerous due to lack of national consensus.
He stressed that the Constitution already outlines which provisions can or cannot be revised, and insisted that any reform process must be carefully managed to avoid further political tension.
The remarks come amid an ongoing national debate over possible constitutional changes, with supporters arguing that the 2006 Constitution no longer reflects current governance realities, while critics warn that reform efforts could be used for political advantage, including the removal of presidential term limits.
Opposition figures and civil society groups have raised concerns that such changes could extend the current president’s tenure beyond 2028, while the ruling camp maintains that reforms are needed to improve state efficiency.
CENCO called for caution and dialogue, emphasizing that any constitutional process must take place in a stable and consensual environment to avoid deepening existing national divisions.
*Katumbi rejects constitutional change, calls 2006 charter “the best in the world”
Kinshasa March 27, 2026 - Opposition leader Moïse Katumbi has strongly opposed any attempt to revise the Constitution of the Democratic Republic of Congo, urging citizens to resist proposed reforms amid an ongoing national debate over constitutional change.

Katumbi argued that the Constitution is not responsible for the country’s governance failures, stating that issues such as poor infrastructure, low public sector wages, unemployment, and poverty stem from leadership and policy choices rather than the constitutional framework itself.
He described the 2006 Constitution as “the best in the world” and warned that changing it would constitute a betrayal of the Congolese people.
Addressing President Félix Tshisekedi directly, Katumbi recalled past cooperation on safeguarding constitutional order and the country’s first peaceful transfer of power, urging respect for the aspirations of citizens and national stability.
The debate over constitutional revision has intensified in recent months, with the ruling party arguing that reforms are needed to improve state efficiency, while opposition figures and civil society groups warn that such changes could undermine term limits and consolidate political power beyond 2028.
The controversy continues to deepen political divisions in the country as both sides maintain sharply contrasting interpretations of the Constitution’s role in governance and development.
*DRC lawmaker proposes reforms to limit multiple public offices and improve governance in state-owned enterprises
Kinshasa March 29, 2026 - A member of the National Assembly from Lukunga, Dunia Mangu Mangu, has introduced a series of proposed amendments aimed at improving governance in state-owned enterprises in the Democratic Republic of Congo by restricting the accumulation of public offices.
The proposals were presented during parliamentary debates on March 27 on a draft law revising the legal framework governing the organization and management of public enterprises, initiated by MP Jean-Pierre Pasi Zapamba.
At the center of the amendments is a push to prohibit individuals from simultaneously holding positions in state-owned companies and political offices, or engaging in competing commercial activities linked to those enterprises.
The lawmaker argued that such overlaps create inefficiencies, conflicts of interest, and public frustration, particularly among unemployed youth.
He also proposed stricter rules preventing individuals from holding multiple senior positions across different public institutions, citing examples such as incompatibility between ministerial roles and leadership positions in entities like the Civil Aviation Authority (RVA) or the National Insurance Company.
Dunia Mangu Mangu praised the original legislative initiative, saying it responds to public demands for greater transparency and fairness in the management of state institutions.
However, he also suggested expanding recruitment criteria for public officials beyond competitive examinations to include political considerations and national representation, arguing that this could strengthen cohesion and reduce political tensions.
He maintained that aligning appointments with both merit and political balance would help reinforce unity within state institutions.
The proposals have sparked debate in parliament, highlighting ongoing tensions in the DRC over governance standards, merit-based appointments, political inclusion, and transparency in the management of public enterprises.
*DRC welcomes UN resolution recognizing transatlantic slave trade as “most serious crime against humanity”
March 29, 2026 - The Democratic Republic of Congo’s Ministry of Culture, Arts and Heritage has welcomed a United Nations General Assembly resolution recognizing the transatlantic slave trade and slavery of Africans as the most serious crimes against humanity.
Adopted during the 80th UN General Assembly session, the resolution highlights the scale, systemic nature, and long-term consequences of slavery, stating that its legacy continues to fuel structural inequalities in the modern world.
According to the Congolese Ministry, the resolution represents an important step toward international recognition of historical injustices and strengthens calls for remembrance, justice, and reparations.
The text was adopted on March 25 with 123 votes in favor, 3 against (United States, Israel, and Argentina), and 52 abstentions, including several European countries. It was sponsored by Ghana and supported by the African Union as part of broader continental advocacy for reparative justice related to the transatlantic slave trade.
Ghanaian President John Mahama, speaking after the vote, described the resolution as “historic,” emphasizing the need for global acknowledgment of the estimated 13 million Africans forcibly displaced over centuries.
The resolution also encourages the restitution of cultural property to countries of origin, reinforcing ongoing international debates about the return of artifacts taken during colonial and slavery-era injustices.
In line with this agenda, the DRC Ministry highlighted its ongoing efforts to preserve historical memory, including a memorandum of understanding signed in January 2025 for the creation of a national memorial dedicated to Ota Benga, a Congolese man who was taken to the United States in the early 20th century and displayed at the Bronx Zoo in 1906.
Authorities in Kinshasa say the project reflects a broader commitment to historical recognition, cultural restoration, and the promotion of African heritage as part of international justice and memory initiatives.

*Norbert Basengezi elected Second Vice-President, replacing Bahati Lukwebo
Kinshasa April 3, 2026 - Senator Norbert Basengezi Katintima has been elected Second Vice-President of the Senate of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, securing a clear majority in Friday’s vote.
According to results announced by Senate President Jean-Michel Sama Lukonde, Basengezi received 87 votes in favor, against 5 votes opposed and 3 invalid ballots.
A former head of the Independent National Electoral Commission (CENI) and senator representing South Kivu, Basengezi was designated by the ruling coalition, the Sacred Union of the Nation, to succeed Modeste Bahati Lukwebo in the Senate bureau.
His election follows a process marked by the withdrawal of other contenders. Senator Françoise Bemba Ndokwa, initially in the race, saw her candidacy withdrawn by her party, the Movement for the Liberation of Congo. Another candidate, Mariane Bakiele, also stepped down on the eve of the vote, citing alignment with the majority coalition’s choice and respect for the Head of State, Félix Tshisekedi.
With no remaining challengers, Basengezi stood as the sole candidate, a position validated by a special commission set up to review applications.
The vacancy followed the resignation of Bahati Lukwebo, which the Senate formally acknowledged during a plenary session on March 21. His departure came amid a petition seeking his removal over alleged “incompetence.” A special parliamentary commission had been tasked with examining the case but closed its proceedings after his resignation.
Basengezi’s election consolidates the Sacred Union’s hold over key positions within the upper chamber, at a time of ongoing political realignments within the country’s institutions.
*Sankuru gubernatorial race: Legal challenge threatens April 15 election timetable
Sankuru April 4, 2026 - A legal dispute over candidate eligibility has introduced fresh uncertainty into the electoral process for the governorship of Sankuru province, with a key court hearing scheduled for April 10.
The Council of State is set to examine a case filed by gubernatorial candidate Justin Omokala Wemambolo, who is contesting the final list of candidates published by the Independent National Electoral Commission (CENI).
Omokala is challenging the inclusion of fellow candidate Jules Lodi Emongo, arguing that his candidacy as an independent violates constitutional and electoral provisions. According to him, Emongo-elected as a senator under the banner of the Union for Democracy and Social Progress-failed to formally resign from his party before declaring an independent bid, as required by law.
He also contests the eligibility of Emongo’s running mate for vice-governor, claiming the individual, a civil servant, did not submit a resignation or official leave of absence prior to entering the race, in breach of electoral regulations.
The dispute follows a decision by the Sankuru Court of Appeal, which rejected Omokala’s appeal on procedural grounds, ruling that he lacked standing despite appearing on the provisional list of candidates. Omokala has since escalated the matter to the Council of State, the country’s highest administrative jurisdiction.
He further criticized CENI for publishing the final list before the conclusion of ongoing legal proceedings, arguing that the electoral body should have awaited the outcome of the April 10 hearing.
The controversy comes as the gubernatorial election, initially delayed due to funding constraints, is now scheduled for April 15. The final list includes 10 candidates, down from 11 following the withdrawal of one contender.
With legal proceedings still underway, the outcome of the Council of State hearing could prove decisive in determining whether the electoral calendar in Sankuru can be maintained.

Officials also indicated that additional measures will be implemented to ensure the steady import and distribution of petroleum products via regional supply routes through Kenya and Tanzania, while monitoring freight and insurance costs used to calculate average border pricing.
Despite global market volatility, the government has reassured the public that national fuel reserves remain sufficient to meet demand until June, with additional shipments expected in the coming weeks to reinforce supply.
The policy follows discussions in a recent Council of Ministers meeting chaired by President Félix Tshisekedi, during which officials emphasized the need for coordinated action to maintain macroeconomic stability in the face of external shocks.

Conclusion
The period from late March to early April 2026 reflects a Democratic Republic of Congo navigating between meaningful institutional progress and enduring structural fragility. While authorities in Kinshasa advanced key reforms and strengthened international partnerships, the eastern regions remain gripped by insecurity and humanitarian strain.
Institutional and Economic Foundations
The launch of the second General Population and Housing Census marks a historic step toward modern governance, supported by an $80 million commitment from the African Development Bank. This initiative aims to close a decades-long data gap, laying the groundwork for better public policy and equitable development. At the same time, renewed mining diplomacy-through agreements with China and strategic discussions on lithium and cobalt-highlights the country’s ambition to move up the global value chain and assert greater economic sovereignty.
Political Tensions and Constitutional Debate
However, political dynamics remain fragile. The debate over constitutional revision continues to polarize national actors. The National Episcopal Conference of Congo has warned against initiating reforms without broad consensus, citing the current security climate. Meanwhile, opposition figure Moïse Katumbi has firmly rejected any attempt to amend the constitution, framing it as a potential threat to democratic stability. In parallel, President Félix Tshisekedi has called for improved cooperation between provincial institutions, underscoring persistent governance challenges linked to decentralization.
Security and Humanitarian Crisis
Despite diplomatic initiatives, the eastern provinces remain the epicenter of the country’s most pressing crisis. Ongoing clashes involving the Armed Forces of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the AFC/M23, and other armed groups continue to drive displacement and civilian casualties. The humanitarian toll is compounded by economic collapse in conflict-affected areas, with provinces such as South Kivu reportedly losing the vast majority of their revenue due to loss of territorial control.
Final Outlook
The DRC stands at a critical juncture. Progress in sectors such as mining, energy, and digital infrastructure offers a credible path toward long-term development. Yet these gains remain vulnerable in the face of persistent insecurity and political divisions. Sustainable stability will depend not only on the implementation of reforms but also on restoring state authority, strengthening institutions, and fostering inclusive national dialogue across all regions of the country.
