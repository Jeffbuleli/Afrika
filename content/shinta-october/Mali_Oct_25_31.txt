Meanwhile, clashes between Malian forces and jihadist groups near Bamako have heightened fears of further violence.

The embassy’s guidance to U.S. citizens included:
Avoid public demonstrations and large gatherings
Do not attempt to drive through roadblocks
Keep travel documents and communication devices readily available
Maintain a low profile and remain vigilant about surroundings
Stock supplies and review emergency plans
Britain’s Foreign Office issued a similar warning on Thursday, confirming that “non-essential British Embassy staff have been temporarily withdrawn from Bamako” and advising nationals to leave “immediately by commercial flight if safe to do so.”
Other countries - including Italy, Germany, and Canada - have also called on their citizens to evacuate Mali amid deteriorating conditions.
The crisis stems from a fuel blockade enforced by Jama’at Nusrat al-Islam wal-Muslimin (JNIM), an Al-Qaeda-linked group that has attacked tankers and convoys transporting fuel from Senegal and Côte d’Ivoire since September. The blockade has cut off critical supplies to Bamako, leading to severe fuel shortages, economic paralysis, and growing public unrest.
Multiple tankers have been torched, and several drivers and soldiers killed or kidnapped in recent ambushes. The blockade has effectively isolated the capital, pushing the landlocked Sahel nation deeper into crisis as authorities struggle to restore supply lines and maintain security.

*Mali’s BNDA Secures $40 Million IFC Loan to Boost Agricultural Lending
Bamako, Mali - The Banque Nationale de Développement Agricole (BNDA) has secured a $40 million loan from the International Finance Corporation (IFC) to expand lending to small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in the agricultural sector. The agreement, announced on October 30, 2025, aims to strengthen financing for smallholder farmers, cooperatives, and agribusinesses across Mali.
The IFC package also includes an additional $10 million trade finance facility, designed to facilitate the import and export of essential agricultural goods and inputs.
Under the five-year program, BNDA plans to grow its SME loan portfolio to more than $270 million, channeling funds toward women-led businesses and climate-smart agribusiness projects. About 25 percent of the loans will target women-owned enterprises, while 10 percent will be reserved for projects promoting renewable energy, sustainable irrigation, and green agriculture.
According to IFC estimates, the partnership could expand BNDA’s green financing portfolio by 90 percent and create between 8,600 and 14,200 jobs - both direct and indirect - over the next five years.
“This partnership with IFC will enable us to increase our support for farmers, SMEs, and women entrepreneurs across the country,” said Badara Aliou Coulibaly, BNDA’s Managing Director. “It will also allow us to integrate digital and sustainable solutions, expanding access to finance and reinforcing national food security.”
Beyond financial support, the IFC will provide technical assistance to help BNDA strengthen its agricultural lending strategy, risk management systems, and gender inclusion framework. The program will also introduce customized credit scoring tools to improve access to loans for small businesses and cooperatives.
The deal comes as Mali seeks to revitalize an agricultural sector that employs nearly 70 percent of its population but contributes only 38 percent of GDP. Most farms remain small-scale - averaging 4.8 hectares - and face chronic challenges such as limited irrigation, low yields, and underinvestment in modern production systems.
The IFC’s intervention is expected to bolster the sector’s resilience and productivity, aligning with Mali’s broader goal of achieving food security and inclusive economic growth.
*Mali Launches Advanced Drought Monitoring System to Protect Agriculture
Bamako, Mali - In response to growing concerns over recurring droughts that threaten food security and rural livelihoods, Mali has introduced a new digital platform designed to track and analyze drought conditions in real time. The Mali Drought Monitoring System (MaliDMS) aims to strengthen early warning capacities and support data-driven decision-making for agricultural resilience.
The Challenge
Droughts remain one of the most severe natural hazards affecting Mali’s economy and food production. The National Directorate of Agriculture (Direction Nationale de l’Agriculture, DNA) has long stressed the urgent need for a reliable monitoring system to identify drought-affected areas and evaluate their impact on key crops. The initiative seeks to improve the accuracy of national agricultural statistics and help authorities craft targeted policies to mitigate the effects of water scarcity.
The Solution
Developed using Water Productivity through Open-access Remotely sensed data (WaPOR) from FAO and other high-resolution satellite sources, the MaliDMS provides near real-time assessments of drought onset, duration, and severity. Its interactive interface displays dynamic maps and graphs that visualize changes in precipitation, vegetation health, and soil moisture, giving policymakers and farmers a clearer picture of evolving drought conditions.
Key Technical Features
MaliDMS employs Level 1 WaPOR data with a spatial resolution of 300 meters, updated every 10 days, to deliver detailed insights on indicators such as actual and reference evapotranspiration (AETI and RET), precipitation (P), and net primary production (NPP). These datasets enable national, regional, and local assessments of drought impact.
Users can access, analyze, and download results in multiple formats, with the platform available in both English and French. By integrating advanced satellite analytics with open data principles, MaliDMS represents a significant step toward improving drought preparedness and safeguarding agricultural productivity across the country.
*President Assimi Goïta Inaugurates Mali’s Second Lithium Mine to Boost Economic Sovereignty
Bougouni, Mali - On Monday, November 3, 2025, President of the Transition, General Assimi Goïta, officially inaugurated the Bougouni Lithium Mines SA (LMLB SA), Mali’s second operational lithium mine. The event highlights the Transitional Government’s determination to strengthen economic sovereignty and leverage the country’s natural resources despite a complex security and geopolitical environment.
A Strategic Step for Mali’s Economy
The Bougouni mine represents a key milestone in Mali’s ambitious policy of resource-driven, inclusive, and sustainable growth. Positioned in the Bougouni region, the project aims to transform the nation’s rich lithium reserves into a driver of local development and global energy participation. Lithium, essential for batteries in electric vehicles, smartphones, and energy storage, places Mali among countries actively contributing to the energy transition.
Project Structure and Investment
The Bougouni Lithium Mines SA project is structured in two main phases. The first phase at the N'Gouanala site, already underway, involves a $65 million investment and will produce nearly 120,000 tons of lithium concentrate annually over four years. The second phase, scheduled for 2028, will expand operations to the Sogola-Baoulé and Boumou sites, requiring $180 million in investment and increasing production to over 200,000 tons per year. In total, $245 million (over 150 billion CFA francs) will be invested, reflecting strong investor confidence in Mali’s stability and governance.
Modern and Environmentally Responsible Technology
The mine employs advanced, environmentally friendly technologies to ensure efficient extraction while minimizing ecological impact. The project’s operations will create jobs, improve local infrastructure, and support community development through education, health services, and local entrepreneurship initiatives.
Economic and Social Benefits
The Malian state holds a 35% stake in the company, including a 10% free share under the new Mining Code, ensuring fair distribution of wealth and long-term benefits. Revenues for Mali are projected at over $380 million (approximately 230 billion CFA francs) from taxes, royalties, and dividends. Beyond financial gains, the project will generate direct and indirect employment, develop regional infrastructure, and empower local communities.
Symbol of National Sovereignty
The inauguration of the Bougouni Lithium Mines SA underscores Mali’s commitment to economic autonomy. By focusing on local value creation and responsible management, the government aims to make natural resources a catalyst for national transformation. The project exemplifies state-investor partnerships based on transparency, social responsibility, and sustainable growth.
A Vision for the Future
The Bougouni mine represents both a national pride and a strategic vision for Mali. By responsibly developing lithium resources, the country is positioning itself as a key player in the global energy transition while ensuring that benefits accrue primarily to its citizens. The mine will contribute to long-term economic stability, technological advancement, and community empowerment, reaffirming Mali’s path toward economic, technological, and energy sovereignty.

*Mali Settles 30-Year Dispute Over Voluntary Retirements (1986-1993)
BAMAKO, MALI - After three decades of waiting, the Government of Mali has authorized the payment of benefits and compensation to former public servants who voluntarily retired between 1986 and 1993 under the country’s structural adjustment program. The decision was formalized through a Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) signed on Tuesday, November 4, 2025, between the Government and the National Union of Workers of Mali (UNTM).
“It is with deep emotion and a real sense of accomplishment that I speak on this solemn occasion,” declared Yacouba Katilé, Secretary General of the UNTM, highlighting the significance of this long-awaited social justice measure.
The agreement marks the end of a protracted struggle that began when thousands of civil servants accepted voluntary retirement during a period of severe economic austerity. Many were left without due compensation for decades. The new MoU reflects the transitional government’s commitment to justice, fairness, and social cohesion.
According to Dr. Fassoun Coulibaly, Minister of Labour, Public Service, and Social Dialogue, the commission handling the case focused on three key components:
Social treatment and reintegration measures;
Reimbursement of the employer’s 8% contribution share;
Preservation of pension rights for 61 employees who had completed 15 years of service.
To settle the issue, the government has allocated over nine billion CFA francs, an act described by Katilé as both “moral and social redress.” “By signing this protocol, we are taking an act of justice toward these former workers who contributed their sweat and loyalty to the building of Mali,” he stated.
The agreement includes a three-year payment moratorium, with compensation to be deposited directly into the individual accounts of the beneficiaries. This landmark decision not only closes a painful chapter in Mali’s labor history but also signals the government’s determination to restore trust and equity between the state and its workforce.

BAMAKO, MALI - Concerns are mounting over the fate of Cheick Oumar Diallo, a well-known critic of Mali’s military junta and former trade unionist, who was forcibly taken by gendarmes in Bamako on October 25 and has not been seen since.

Human rights observers say Diallo’s case bears the hallmarks of an enforced disappearance-an arrest carried out without acknowledgment or information on the detainee’s whereabouts-placing him outside legal protection. Such disappearances violate international laws prohibiting arbitrary detention, torture, and extrajudicial killings.
Mali is a signatory to the International Convention for the Protection of All Persons from Enforced Disappearance, ratified in 2009.
Rights advocates and colleagues are calling on the Malian authorities to immediately disclose Diallo’s location, ensure his safety, and end their campaign of intimidation against trade unionists and government critics.

Analysts warn that the militants are tightening their hold around the capital. In areas under JNIM control, residents have reported the enforcement of strict dress codes and religious rulings through makeshift courts that lack fair-trial guarantees, according to a 2024 Human Rights Watch report.
Western governments have raised the alarm. The United States this week issued two security advisories urging its citizens to leave Mali “immediately,” citing the deteriorating security situation. Australia, Germany, and Italy issued similar warnings.
A former Malian minister, now in exile, told The Guardian that the country risks “collapsing before our eyes,” warning of the potential for another coup - Mali’s third in five years and sixth since independence in 1960.
“Before 31 December, a coup will happen in the Sahel,” the former official said. “Mali will go first, and then the same domino effect could follow, just like between 2020 and 2023.”
Experts say the coming weeks will be decisive. Ulf Laessing, head of the Sahel Programme at Germany’s Konrad Adenauer Foundation, said the situation could worsen as remaining fuel reserves are depleted.
“Next week will be really bad,” he warned. “It’s difficult to see how they can resupply the capital in sufficient quantities.”
Observers fear that if Bamako were to fall, Mali could face the grim prospect of an Islamist republic under JNIM, mirroring Taliban-ruled Afghanistan - a development that could destabilize the entire Sahel region.
*Mali’s Fragile Regime Confronts Rising Islamist Threat and Internal Strains
Bamako, Mali - Mali faces a precarious crossroads as a deepening insurgency and a worsening fuel crisis compound the challenges confronting the country’s military government. Analysts warn that the collapse of the junta could create a vacuum exploited by Islamist groups seeking to establish an Islamic state.
According to Ulf Laessing, head of the Sahel Programme at Germany’s Konrad Adenauer Foundation, popular unrest has been muted largely due to fear of what might follow."So far nobody is demonstrating against the government because people know that if they bring it down, the next one could be an Islamist regime," Laessing said. "That fear is, paradoxically, helping to strengthen the junta’s hold on power."
From Protest Movement to Military Rule
Mali’s political turmoil began in June 2020 with the emergence of the June 5 Movement - Rally of Patriotic Forces (M5-RFP), a coalition of civil society groups, opposition parties, and religious leaders. The coalition led mass protests against then-President Ibrahim Boubacar Keïta, accusing his administration of corruption and failing to curb jihadist violence.
Imam Mahmoud Dicko, a prominent and polarizing cleric, played a leading role in mobilizing the demonstrations. Dicko first gained national attention during the 1991 uprising that toppled President Moussa Traoré.
During mediation talks led by the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS), Keïta reportedly warned that Dicko sought to turn Mali into an Islamic state under sharia law - a claim that triggered a heated reaction among regional leaders.
Weeks later, soldiers led by then-Captain Assimi Goïta overthrew Keïta’s government. A second coup in 2021 cemented Goïta’s authority, installing him as President of the Transition.
Broken Promises and Escalating Violence
The junta initially pledged to restore civilian rule through elections in February 2022, but the process has been repeatedly delayed. Meanwhile, violence has surged.
Data from the Africa Center for Strategic Studies indicate that more than 17,700 people have been killed in Mali’s conflict since 2012, with over two-thirds of those deaths occurring after 2020. The al-Qaida-affiliated group JNIM has expanded operations beyond Mali into Benin and Nigeria, fueled in part by ransom payments from high-profile kidnappings.
Human rights organizations accuse Malian forces, allied militias, and Russian Wagner mercenaries of committing widespread abuses during counterinsurgency operations.
Isolation and Internal Tensions
Diplomatically, Mali is increasingly isolated. The country withdrew from ECOWAS, forfeiting vital regional support, and instead signed a mutual defense pact with Burkina Faso and Niger, both under military rule. Relations with Western governments have soured, prompting several embassies and aid agencies to scale back operations.
Inside the junta, tensions are reportedly rising among the five colonels who orchestrated the 2020 coup. Colonel Malick Diaw, head of the National Transitional Council, and Defence Minister Sadio Camara are reportedly positioning themselves as potential successors to Goïta.
The Possible Return of Imam Dicko
Exiled in Algeria since 2023 after falling out with the junta, Imam Mahmoud Dicko could soon return to Mali.
"Some within JNIM are calling for Dicko’s return, hoping to negotiate with him instead of the Malian government," a political insider said. "Their ultimate goal is to establish an Islamic state - and they are closer than ever."
As Mali grapples with internal divisions and external threats, the country’s fragile transition remains under intense pressure, leaving the future uncertain.
*Mali: JNIM Frees Three Hostages After Record $50 Million Ransom and Arms Exchange Amid Capital Embargo
Bamako, Mali - Three foreign nationals held by the Al-Qaeda-linked jihadist group JNIM in Mali have been released following a record ransom and arms exchange, highlighting the group’s growing influence amid the country’s ongoing security crisis.
The hostages-two Emirati citizens, including retired General Joumaa bin Maktoum al-Maktoum, a member of Dubai’s ruling family and a gold trader, and an Iranian national-were abducted in Bamako on 23 September. They were freed on 29 October near the northern city of Gao, after more than a month in captivity, in exchange for at least $50 million and several tonnes of ammunition, according to security sources.
The previous hostages were typically ransomed for $6-10 million. Joumaa bin Maktoum al-Maktoum’s high-profile capture allowed JNIM to command an unprecedented price. The other two hostages included a Pakistani man, initially thought to be Emirati, and the Iranian national.
JNIM, which has shifted its focus from targeting Western nationals to countries it considers supporters of the Malian, Burkinabe, and Nigerien regimes, currently holds a range of foreign nationals, including Chinese, Indians, Ukrainians associated with Wagner, an Iranian, and Egyptians, whose connections to Malian authorities are reportedly under scrutiny.
The ransom comes amid JNIM’s continued embargo on fuel imports to the Malian capital, Bamako, in effect since early September. The blockade has triggered a severe gasoline shortage, paralyzing the economy, and hundreds of tanker trucks coming from Abidjan and Dakar have been set ablaze by the group. JNIM has stated that its ultimate goal is to destabilize the Malian authorities-who seized power in the 2020 and 2021 coups-and establish Islamic governance.
Negotiations for the hostages, believed to have been mediated through secret channels, underscore the growing financial and political leverage of jihadist groups in Mali, where abductions have become a lucrative source of funding amid ongoing insecurity and political instability.
*Malian Military Secures Fuel Convoy, Pledges to Prevent Future Attacks
Bamako, Mali - The Malian military has successfully thwarted an attack on a strategic fuel convoy, assuring citizens that measures are in place to prevent further disruptions to the country’s energy supply.
On Sunday, October 26, 2025, the Malian Armed Forces (FAMa) launched an operation in the Bougouni region, neutralizing dozens of armed terrorists who were preparing to target a fuel convoy. The operation, carried out under the command of General Oumar Diarra, was hailed for its speed, professionalism, and coordination, allowing the convoy to resume its journey and securing vital fuel supplies for Bamako and other regions.
The attack had contributed to an ongoing fuel shortage in the capital and across Mali, which authorities attribute to external terrorist pressures seeking to destabilize the country’s economy. Military officials emphasized that the government remains committed to protecting national resources and maintaining sovereignty against foreign exploitation.
“The speculation and external pressure will not deter us. The sovereignty of our country cannot be compromised,” a statement from the General Staff said. Authorities confirmed that they are taking decisive steps to secure fuel supplies, diversify partnerships, and safeguard Mali’s economic stability.
The operation in Kolondiéba highlights the Malian Armed Forces’ continued efforts to combat terrorism and protect critical infrastructure, ensuring the uninterrupted delivery of essential resources to citizens.
Conclusion : Mali at a Critical Crossroads (Oct. 25 - Nov. 8, 2025)
The period between October 25 and November 8, 2025, reveals a Mali operating under immense pressure from both external threats and internal political and economic reforms. The country is simultaneously asserting its national sovereignty and regional leadership while grappling with a severe, existential security and humanitarian crisis.
Key Takeaways from the Reporting Period
The most immediate challenge remains the crippling fuel blockade imposed by the al-Qaeda-linked Jama’at Nusrat al-Islam wal-Muslimin (JNIM). This blockade has severely disrupted daily life, leading to the nationwide closure of schools and universities , rising food prices, and economic paralysis. The crisis prompted the withdrawal of non-essential embassy staff by the United States and the United Kingdom, with France also urging its citizens to leave immediately. The release of three foreign hostages, including a member of Dubai's ruling family, for a record $50 million ransom and arms exchange further underscored JNIM's growing financial and political leverage.
In response to the escalating crisis, Malian authorities are focusing on twin strategies:
Security Mobilization: The Malian Armed Forces (FAMa) successfully secured a strategic fuel convoy, pledging to prevent future attacks and asserting the government's commitment to stability. Foreign Minister Abdoulaye Diop sought to reassure partners of the government's capacity to manage the crisis.
Economic Sovereignty & Reform: Despite the turmoil, the government pressed forward with major long-term initiatives. President Goïta inaugurated the Bougouni Lithium Mine, Mali’s second operational lithium mine, a project intended to boost economic sovereignty and generate over $380 million in revenue for the state. Additionally, the Council of Ministers approved key treasury reforms to enhance financial oversight and adopted new regulations for architects in line with UEMOA standards. The country also secured a $40 million loan from the IFC to expand agricultural lending and launched the Mali Drought Monitoring System (MaliDMS) to protect the crucial agricultural sector.
Social & Political Tensions
The transition government's stance on dissent continues to generate controversy. Former Prime Minister Moussa Mara was sentenced to one year in prison for expressing solidarity with political prisoners on social media, a ruling widely condemned by human rights groups. Furthermore, a prominent critic of the junta, Cheick Oumar Diallo, was reportedly forcibly disappeared by gendarmes. These events highlight tightening restrictions on free expression.
On a positive note, the government reached a landmark agreement to settle a 30-year-old dispute by allocating over nine billion CFA francs in compensation to former civil servants who accepted voluntary retirement between 1986 and 1993, an act described as "moral and social redress".
In summary, the military-led transitional government is facing the most serious test of its authority since seizing power, with analysts warning of potential political collapse or the rise of an Islamist republic. While the government is asserting its regional role-most notably by hosting the WCO-WCA Regional Customs Intelligence Meeting -and pushing forward a resource-driven economic agenda, its capacity to manage the escalating security and humanitarian fallout from the JNIM blockade in the coming weeks will be decisive for Mali's future stability.
