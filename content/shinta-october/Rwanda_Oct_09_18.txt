*Fact-Checking President Tshisekedi’s Address at the Global Gateway Forum 2025 in Brussels
Brussels, October 9, 2025 -During his address at the Global Gateway Forum 2025, Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) President Félix Tshisekedi delivered a speech calling for peace and regional cooperation, particularly with neighboring Rwanda and Uganda. However, a closer examination of his statements reveals several inconsistencies with facts and recent developments in the region.
Claim 1: “Since I was elected in 2019, I have visited all nine neighbouring countries, particularly Rwanda and Uganda, to propose working together for development and well-being.”
While it is true that President Tshisekedi initiated diplomatic visits to several neighboring states early in his tenure, relations with Rwanda and Uganda have since deteriorated sharply.Since 1999, more than ten peace agreements have been signed between the DRC and its neighbors but rarely implemented - largely due to Kinshasa’s preference for military solutions and persistent political mistrust.
Analysts also point to anti-Rwandan rhetoric and internal persecution of certain Congolese communities, notably Congolese Tutsi, which has intensified in recent years under Tshisekedi’s administration.
Claim 2: “At no time have I displayed any belligerent attitude towards Rwanda or Uganda.”
This statement contradicts numerous public speeches and interviews in which President Tshisekedi used hostile language toward Rwanda and its leadership.During his 2023 re-election campaign, Tshisekedi referred to Rwandan President Paul Kagame as “Hitler”, threatened to “bomb Kigali,” and vowed to “work with Rwanda’s enemies.”
Such rhetoric has also echoed in regional and international fora, where DRC officials have repeatedly accused Rwanda of backing the M23 rebellion and called for sanctions against Kigali.Locally, Congolese officials have often blamed domestic challenges - from corruption scandals to economic stagnation - on Rwanda, fueling anti-Rwanda sentiment and hate speech within the country.
Claim 3: “We are the only two capable of stopping this escalation.”
Observers argue that escalation largely depends on Kinshasa’s decisions, particularly regarding the FDLR militia, a Rwandan rebel group based in eastern Congo and accused of participating in the 1994 Genocide against the Tutsi. Despite regional commitments through the EAC, AU, and SADC, the DRC has continued to collaborate with FDLR units, foreign mercenaries, and local militias such as the Wazalendo, worsening instability. Rwanda maintains that peace can only be achieved through direct dialogue between the DRC government and AFC/M23, a Congolese rebel movement involved in the Doha peace process.
Claim 4: “Rwanda boycotted a near-final peace ceremony for no reason.”
Rwanda’s government publicly stated that it refused to participate in the Luanda ceremony because the DRC failed to honor prior commitments, including talks with internal rebel movements. Kigali described the event as a “photo opportunity” that did not address core security issues.
Claim 5: “M23 must stop its escalation supported by Rwanda.”
The DRC government continues to blame Rwanda for supporting the M23, a claim Kigali denies.Independent observers and regional mediators, however, note that Kinshasa’s own actions - such as the failure to demobilize the FDLR and recruitment of foreign mercenaries - have equally fueled the conflict.
Multiple cross-border incidents have been recorded since 2019, including artillery shelling in January 2025 that killed at least 16 Rwandan civilians in Rubavu District.
Claim 6: “We have been counting them in the millions for years.”
Tshisekedi’s reference to “millions” of victims has no verified statistical basis. While the humanitarian toll of the eastern Congo conflict is indeed catastrophic, experts caution against politically motivated exaggerations that distort the scope of the crisis.
Claim 7: “We remain grateful to SADC troops who fell on the field of honour.”
SADC forces, deployed in late 2024 to support the Congolese army, suffered heavy losses during clashes in North Kivu. Following the fall of Goma, surviving troops and foreign mercenaries were reportedly granted safe passage through Rwanda after withdrawal.
Claim 8: “The DRC is not belligerent and wants peace.”
Despite Tshisekedi’s final appeal for peace, his government’s ongoing military offensives, hate-driven narratives, and failure to implement regional peace accords contradict this claim.Analysts and regional mediators stress that peace will only come from sincere political will - notably, through dismantling the FDLR, ceasing anti-Rwanda provocations, and re-engaging in Doha and Luanda peace mechanisms in good faith.
President Tshisekedi’s speech in Brussels projected an image of a leader committed to peace and dialogue. However, a detailed fact-check reveals a pattern of contradictions between his public statements and the DRC’s actions on the ground. While the DRC continues to frame itself as a victim of external aggression, evidence points to internal political choices, military miscalculations, and regional defiance as major obstacles to peace in the Great Lakes region.

*AFC/M23 Warns DRC: “No Escaping the Doha Peace Talks”
The AFC/M23 coalition has urged the government of the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) to fully commit to the ongoing peace talks in Doha, warning that they remain the only viable path to ending the protracted conflict in eastern Congo.
The negotiations, mediated by the Qatari government since March 2025, have struggled to produce tangible results, with several key issues still unresolved. After being suspended earlier this year, the talks resumed in July, leading to partial progress as both sides agreed on guiding principles toward a lasting peace.
Despite these advances, implementation has stalled. Fighting in North Kivu and surrounding areas continues, undermining expectations that hostilities would cease following initial commitments.
In a message published on X, the AFC/M23 referenced recent meetings between U.S. Presidential Advisor on Africa, Massad Boulos, and Presidents Félix Tshisekedi of the DRC and Paul Kagame of Rwanda. According to the group, discussions focused primarily on the U.S.-brokered peace initiative and the resumption of the Doha process.
“The Doha talks are not merely a platform for dialogue but a genuine path to peace,” the AFC/M23 statement read. “Both parties must build mutual trust, release political prisoners, halt hostilities, and fully adhere to the agreed principles.”
The group further stressed that, as the talks prepare to reconvene for a sixth time, Kinshasa must participate “with mutual respect and equality.”
“The government has previously attempted to avoid these discussions,” the statement continued. “But in Doha, there is no superior or subordinate side-both parties sit at the same table to discuss the same nation, the Democratic Republic of Congo.”
AFC/M23 also warned that the broader U.S.-supported peace framework, particularly regarding the management of Congo’s mineral resources, cannot move forward without meaningful progress in the Doha negotiations.
*Nyamasheke Launches Construction of 21-Kilometer Road to Connect Three Isolated Sectors
The Nyamasheke District administration has begun construction of the 21-kilometer Bushenge-Shangi-Nyabitekeri road, a long-awaited project aimed at easing transportation, boosting trade, and ending the isolation of residents in three sectors along Lake Kivu.
Bushenge, Shangi, and Nyabitekeri are among ten sectors in Nyamasheke District that border Lake Kivu and have long struggled with poor accessibility due to the deteriorated road linking them to the main Kivu asphalt route.
Local residents have welcomed the project, hoping it will ease their daily travel and reduce transportation costs.Dan Nkurunziza, a project manager working in Nyabitekeri Sector, told IGIHE that residents currently pay as much as 16,000 Rwandan francs for a round trip to nearby areas.
“We are happy that this road is finally under construction-it will reduce travel costs. Once completed, public transport vehicles that stopped operating here will return, and residents will no longer be cut off,” Nkurunziza said.
The road is also expected to improve access to Bushenge Hospital, which serves the three sectors. Previously, heavy rains often made it impossible to transport patients, as the road became impassable during the rainy season.
Nyamasheke District Mayor, Mupenzi Narcisse, confirmed that construction is underway, with three contractors assigned to accelerate the project.
“The road, which runs from Bushenge Hospital to Nyamirundi, will be built as a feeder road with strong foundations, bridges, and drainage systems,” Mupenzi said. “It will facilitate communication, improve trade, and enhance agricultural productivity.”
The mayor added that upon completion, the road is expected to significantly boost local economic activity by easing the transport of agricultural produce to the main Kivu asphalt road.
The project is scheduled to be completed within one year and eight months at a total cost of over 8 billion Rwandan francs.
*U.S. welcomes DRC call for FDLR to disarm, urges orderly surrender under Washington agreement
The United States on Friday welcomed a decision by the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) calling on fighters of the Democratic Forces for the Liberation of Rwanda (FDLR) to lay down their arms and surrender to either Congolese authorities or the United Nations peacekeeping mission, MONUSCO. The U.S. reaction framed the move as a key step in implementing the Washington peace agreement signed by Kinshasa and Kigali on June 27, 2025.
A statement issued by the DRC Armed Forces (FARDC) and signed by Major General Sylvain Ekenge Bomusa on October 10, 2025, urged FDLR fighters to disarm and surrender “to the government or MONUSCO.” The military message said the order follows an operational directive adopted on October 1 and reiterated that FARDC personnel are forbidden from cooperating with the FDLR in any form. It also warned that the FARDC “will disarm by force” those who refuse to lay down arms.
U.S. Senior Advisor on Africa Massad Boulos echoed those calls on X, describing Kinshasa’s directive as “a major step in the implementation of the Washington Peace Agreement.” Boulos said the move would facilitate the transfer of refugees, consolidate government authority, and bolster regional efforts to restore peace and security in the Great Lakes region. His post said the DRC’s announcement aligns with provisions of the June 27 accord that envisage the neutralization of the FDLR and related security measures monitored by a Joint Security Coordination Mechanism.
Why the DRC move matters - and why it is complicated
The Washington agreement between the DRC and Rwanda commits both countries to a package of steps including cessation of hostilities, disengagement of forces, and a harmonized plan to neutralize the FDLR. The Joint Security Coordination Mechanism (JSCM) created under that deal is tasked with overseeing aspects of the FDLR neutralization and other security provisions. International backers - led by the United States - have made implementation of those provisions central to the broader push for stability.
But convincing FDLR elements to disarm - and ensuring they do so without fueling further violence - will be difficult. United Nations and independent reporting over recent years has documented complex and sometimes opaque ties between some FDLR elements and Congolese armed groups, including allegations that former FDLR combatants or sympathizers have embedded in local militias or obtained protection from elements within the FARDC. Those overlapping loyalties, and the presence of rival armed formations such as the so-called “Wazalendo” groups, complicate identification and separation of genuine FDLR combatants from other fighters.
Questions of verification, protection and political will
Observers say three practical challenges will determine whether the DRC’s call leads to meaningful disarmament rather than a brief public relations gesture:
Verification: Who will verify that those surrendering are bona fide FDLR fighters and not members of other groups or irregulars attempting to avoid accountability? The JSCM and MONUSCO have roles to play, but their access and acceptance on the ground will be essential.
Security for surrendering combatants: Disarmament programs usually require guarantees on how former fighters will be treated - repatriation, demobilization, reintegration, or prosecution. The Washington agreement sketches a framework but does not eliminate the practical risks fighters fear if they give up arms.
Domestic enforcement: The FARDC’s own conduct will be scrutinized. The new military directive forbids cooperation with the FDLR, but critics note past instances where lines were blurred - making consistent enforcement critical to credibility.
Reactions and regional stakes
Washington’s public support for Kinshasa’s call signals continued U.S. investment in the Washington process and in mechanisms - such as the JSCM - expected to monitor implementation. U.S. officials have tied political and technical support for the region to tangible progress on neutralizing the FDLR and reducing cross-border security risks. Reuters, AP and U.S. State Department material released around the June 27 signing likewise framed the deal as a package that links security, political confidence-building and regional economic cooperation.
Bottom line
The DRC’s October 10 directive - and U.S. endorsement - mark a potential turning point in the Washington-led push to address one of the most intractable armed groups in the eastern DRC. Yet success will depend on careful, credible verification, protections for surrendering fighters, and a demonstrable, consistent break between elements of the FARDC and armed proxies. Without those safeguards, the order could struggle to produce the disarmament and stabilization its authors envision.
*Boris Johnson Urges UK to Revive Rwanda Deportation Plan Amid Debate Over New ID Card System
Former UK Prime Minister Boris Johnson has urged the British government to reinstate the controversial Rwanda deportation program for undocumented migrants, saying that new plans to introduce electronic ID cards will be costly and ineffective in curbing illegal immigration.
In an interview with GB News, Johnson criticized Prime Minister Keir Starmer’s proposal to issue digital identity cards - including to children as young as 13 - as part of efforts to tighten immigration controls. The government argues that the cards, which would store extensive personal data, will allow authorities, employers, and landlords to easily verify the legal status of individuals living in the UK.
Starmer’s Labour government says the initiative forms part of a broader strategy to reduce irregular migration, combining enhanced border technology and stricter enforcement measures.
But Johnson, who led the government from 2019 to 2022, dismissed the plan as “an expensive distraction.” He argued that reinstating the Rwanda deportation scheme - suspended by Starmer shortly after taking office in July 2024 - would be a far more effective deterrent.
“If we had the Rwanda programme, we wouldn’t have let them score a goal. It would have been a back-and-forth move,” Johnson said, likening the flow of migrants across the English Channel to a football match.
Johnson’s Conservative government first signed the UK-Rwanda Migration and Economic Development Partnership in April 2022, under which asylum seekers who entered Britain illegally could be relocated to Rwanda for processing and potential resettlement. In return, Rwanda would receive financial support to facilitate their accommodation and reintegration.
The plan, however, faced immediate legal challenges. The European Court of Human Rights (ECHR) halted the first scheduled deportation flight in June 2022, following complaints from human rights groups. Despite revisions and renewed bilateral commitments in late 2023 - including approval by both Houses of Parliament - the programme remained stalled.
When Starmer took office in mid-2024, his government formally suspended the Rwanda scheme, arguing that it failed to deter illegal crossings and raised serious human rights concerns.
Home Office figures show that 29,437 undocumented migrants entered the UK in 2023, a number that climbed to 43,640 in 2024. Between January and September 2025, the total reached 33,556, underscoring the continued challenge of managing irregular migration across the Channel.
Johnson’s comments have reignited debate over the future of Britain’s asylum and immigration policies - with some Conservative MPs backing a return to the Rwanda plan, while Labour insists its digital ID strategy represents a “fairer, more practical” long-term solution.

*DR Congo and AFC/M23 Agree to Form Joint Ceasefire Monitoring Body
Doha, Qatar, October 14, 2025-The Government of the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) and the AFC/M23 coalition have reached an agreement to create a joint body responsible for overseeing the implementation of the ceasefire in North Kivu and South Kivu provinces.
This development marks the outcome of the sixth round of peace talks between both sides, held in Doha, Qatar, from October 13 to 14, 2025, under the mediation of international partners. The new mechanism is designed to strengthen transparency, rebuild trust, and prevent further violations of the fragile truce in eastern DRC.
According to the joint communiqué, the monitoring body will include:
Equal representation from the DRC government and the AFC/M23;
Observers from the African Union (AU), the State of Qatar, the United States of America, and the International Conference on the Great Lakes Region (ICGLR).
The committee will have the mandate to review, investigate, and document alleged ceasefire violations, and to issue weekly reports on the status of the truce and progress toward peace implementation. The first meeting of the body is expected to take place within seven days of its formation to outline its operational procedures and next steps.
The establishment of this joint mechanism follows months of disagreements over who should oversee the ceasefire. Earlier proposals to have MONUSCO, the UN peacekeeping mission in the DRC, play that role were rejected by the AFC/M23, which accused the mission of bias due to its past collaboration with the Congolese army in combat operations.
Speaking after the agreement, AFC/M23 spokesperson Lawrence Kanyuka expressed optimism, saying:
“The government of Kinshasa has signed an agreement establishing a body to monitor the ceasefire. This is a major step. We hope that this agreement will stop the repeated attacks by the Kinshasa military coalition on the civilian population.”
The latest breakthrough builds on the “Doha Principles” signed on July 19, 2025, which outlined the foundations for a broader peace agreement. While both parties had agreed to a complete cessation of hostilities, recurring clashes and mutual accusations of violations have continued to undermine trust.
Observers believe that this new step could revive momentum for peace if both sides demonstrate genuine commitment to implementing previous accords, particularly those linked to power-sharing arrangements, disarmament, and the return of displaced civilians.
The Doha process, supported by Qatar and the United States, remains one of the most significant diplomatic efforts to resolve the decade-long instability in the Great Lakes region, where overlapping security, ethnic, and political crises have persisted despite multiple peace initiatives.

*Six Rwandan Senators Conclude Their Five-Year Terms
Kigali, Rwanda, October 14, 2025-Six members of Rwanda’s Senate are preparing to complete their five-year terms in office next week, marking the end of their mandate that began in 2020. Those concluding their service include Senators Evode Uwizeyimana, André Twahirwa, Clotilde Mukakarangwa, Epiphanie Kanziza, Alexis Mugisha, and Jean-Pierre Dusingizemungu.
All six were sworn in before President Paul Kagame on October 22, 2020, meaning their constitutional term officially ends on October 22, 2025.
Composition and Appointment Background
Out of the six outgoing Senators:
Four were appointed by President Paul Kagame:
Evode Uwizeyimana, known for his active participation in political and legal debates.
Jean-Pierre Dusingizemungu, a historian and advocate for genocide remembrance.
Epiphanie Kanziza, a senior educator and administrator.
André Twahirwa, a long-serving public servant.
Two were elected through the Forum of Recognized Political Parties in Rwanda (NFPO):
Alexis Mugisha, representing the Democratic Green Party of Rwanda (DGPR).
Clotilde Mukakarangwa, representing the Democratic Party for the Unity of Rwanda (PDC).
Their departure opens the way for either reappointment or replacement, depending on the decisions of the President and the outcomes of internal political processes within the NFPO.
Constitutional Framework
According to Article 80 of the Constitution of the Republic of Rwanda, the Senate consists of 26 members selected through different mechanisms designed to ensure political balance and institutional representation.The composition is as follows:
12 Senators are elected in accordance with the Constitution.
8 Senators are appointed by the President of the Republic.
4 Senators are elected by the Forum of Recognized Political Parties.
2 Senators are elected by higher education institutions, both public and private.
Each Senator, whether elected or appointed, serves a five-year term, which can be renewed once. The only exception applies to a former Head of State, who may serve as a Senator for life without term limits.
Senate Term Rotation and Renewal
The rotation system of appointments and elections ensures continuity within the Senate’s operations. Of the eight Presidential appointees, four are appointed at the start of a new Senate term, while the remaining four are appointed one year later, maintaining institutional balance.
Similarly, among the four Senators elected by political parties, two are chosen at the beginning of the term, and two others a year later.
The elections for the fourth term of the Senate took place in September 2024, as part of Rwanda’s continuing effort to strengthen its legislative institutions and uphold democratic governance.
Looking Ahead
As the six Senators prepare to conclude their service, discussions are already underway regarding potential reappointments and replacements. Some of the outgoing members, particularly those appointed by the President, remain eligible for a second term if reselected.
Political analysts in Kigali note that this transition provides an opportunity for renewed perspectives within the Senate, while maintaining the institution’s balance between experience, political diversity, and technocratic expertise - a hallmark of Rwanda’s legislative system.
*Ambassador Ngoga Urges UN to Respect Rwanda-DRC Peace Framework
New York, October 13, 2025 - Rwanda’s Ambassador to the United Nations, Martin Ngoga, has called on UN Security Council member states to refrain from interfering in the ongoing peace process between Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), emphasizing that the two nations have already defined a cooperative framework to end years of conflict.
Speaking during a UN Security Council session on the security situation in the African Great Lakes Region, Ambassador Ngoga expressed concern that some member states appear to have limited or inaccurate information about the progress being made under the Washington Agreement, a bilateral peace accord signed in June 2025 with the support of the United States.
Call for Non-Interference and Accurate Understanding
Ngoga stressed that external interference risks undermining regional peace efforts, particularly when based on misinformation or political bias. He specifically requested that the U.S. Ambassador to the UN brief the Security Council on the steps already taken under the Washington Agreement, to ensure that all nations share a factual and coordinated understanding of developments.
“The Republic of Rwanda and the DRC have agreed on how to resolve our differences,” Ngoga said.“We ask this Council to support this process. Rwanda will honor its commitments through the Washington Accords and will continue to support the Doha process.”
The Doha process, facilitated by the State of Qatar, runs parallel to the Washington Agreement and focuses on consolidating ceasefire arrangements and political dialogue between the DRC government and the AFC/M23 coalition.
Recognition of Mediation Efforts
Ambassador Ngoga commended Qatar and the African Union (AU) for their constructive roles in promoting dialogue and stability in the Great Lakes region. However, he warned that international involvement should be supportive, not substitutive, of African-led peace mechanisms.
“We want the contribution of this Council,” he told members.“But we also want you to give us the opportunity to fully respect these agreements - and to avoid behaving in a way that would complicate or undermine the process. What is needed is reconciliation.”
Ngoga’s remarks reflect Rwanda’s consistent stance that peace must be owned and driven by regional stakeholders, with the UN’s role focused on support and coordination, not direct intervention.
The Washington Agreement: Key Provisions
The Washington Accords, signed on June 27, 2025, outline a comprehensive framework to restore security, cooperation, and trust between Rwanda and the DRC after years of mutual accusations and armed confrontations.Its main provisions include:
Elimination of the FDLR - Both countries commit to neutralizing the FDLR (Democratic Forces for the Liberation of Rwanda), a militia that Rwanda classifies as a terrorist organization responsible for cross-border attacks.
Dismantling Rwanda’s defensive security measures - Rwanda will gradually reduce its border defense deployments once the FDLR is disbanded and the DRC ensures that no hostile forces operate from its territory.
Repatriation of refugees - Rwanda and the DRC agreed to facilitate the voluntary return of refugees in both directions, under international supervision.
Economic cooperation - The two countries will sign a joint economic agreement to promote cross-border trade, infrastructure development, and investment benefiting citizens on both sides.
Establishment of a Joint Security Cooperation Mechanism (JSCM) - This body, headquartered in Washington, is tasked with monitoring compliance with the agreement and overseeing demobilization and reintegration programs.
Implementation Progress and Challenges
Following the signing of the accord, the DRC Armed Forces (FARDC) called on FDLR fighters to surrender to the government or the UN peacekeeping mission (MONUSCO), warning that those who refuse will face military operations in accordance with the Washington provisions.
Once the FDLR disarmament campaign begins in earnest, Rwanda is expected to start withdrawing its enhanced security measures, a process that will be supervised by the JSCM.
Despite the cautious optimism expressed by both sides, tensions remain high, particularly due to ongoing mistrust and sporadic violations of ceasefire arrangements in eastern Congo. Analysts note that Ngoga’s statement was a diplomatic reminder that Rwanda expects UN member states to support existing peace frameworks, not introduce parallel or conflicting initiatives that could reignite tensions.
*AFC/M23-DRC Prisoner Release Deal Stalls Despite Qatar Talks
The government of the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) and the AFC/M23 rebel coalition have yet to agree on the release of prisoners, despite signing a framework document on September 15, 2025, committing to the process.
Under the agreement, the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) was tasked with identifying detainees, verifying their status and facilitating their transfer between the two parties. However, one month later, no prisoners have been released, and both sides continue to accuse each other of violating the ceasefire in North Kivu and South Kivu provinces.
Disagreements Block Progress
Talks resumed on October 13, 2025, in Qatar, where delegations from both sides met to review the implementation of the ceasefire and discuss the prisoner exchange before moving toward a broader peace agreement.
A joint monitoring mechanism was established on October 14 to oversee the ceasefire, made up of representatives from both parties, a mediator and international observers. But the prisoner issue remains unresolved.
According to sources close to the mediation, key obstacles include:
Incomplete lists of detainees
Unconfirmed locations of some prisoners
Pending judicial cases against certain detainees
Without resolving these legal and logistical gaps, the transfer of prisoners cannot proceed, negotiators say.
International Pressure Mounts
The mediation team-which includes observers from the United States and the African Union-has urged both sides to accelerate progress. The peace roadmap initially aimed for a final agreement by August 2025, but the deadline has already been missed.
Talks aimed at removing the barriers to the prisoner exchange are expected to continue for up to 10 days.
The AFC/M23 has already submitted a list of detainees it wants released by the Congolese government. The list includes fighters and political allies, among them Eric Nkuba Shebandu, a former adviser to Corneille Nangaa, leader of the Congo River Alliance, who is aligned with AFC/M23.
Despite diplomatic efforts, the stalled prisoner release remains a major stumbling block in the peace process, raising fears that delays could escalate tensions on the battlefield.

*Western Province: 15 Tons of Sambaza Caught in One Night as Lake Kivu Reopens After Two-Month Fishing Ban
Fishermen operating on Lake Kivu are celebrating a record haul after catching more than 15 tons of sambaza (small sardine fish) on the first night following the end of a two-month fishing ban. The surge in catches has sparked optimism among local cooperatives and district officials across Rwanda’s Western Province.
The temporary closure, known as the “fishermen’s holiday,” was instituted by the Ministry of Agriculture and Animal Resources (MINAGRI) at the request of local fishing cooperatives. The measure, which ran from August 9 to October 9, 2025, was aimed at allowing fish stocks to regenerate after months of sharp decline in yields observed between June and July.
According to district reports, fishermen caught:
4.5 tons in Nyamasheke,
5 tons in Rutsiro,
1.7 tons in Karongi,
2 tons in Rubavu, and
2.3 tons in Rusizi.
Overall sambaza production nearly doubled compared to the same reopening period last year, rising from 7 tons to 15 tons.
Fishermen credit tighter controls and anti-poaching efforts
The head of the Karongi District Fishermen’s Association, Ndagijimana Emmanuel, attributed the increase to coordinated efforts between fishermen, local authorities, and security agencies to combat illegal fishing during the closure.
“We have arrested several poachers who continued fishing when the lake was closed,” he said. “Previously, a kilo of sambaza sold for between 6,000 and 7,000 francs, but now with increased supply, prices are stabilizing.”
He added that production per boat had also risen dramatically:
“Before the closure, a group of three boats using 35,000 francs worth of fuel could barely catch three to five kilos. Now, a successful fisherman can catch up to 80 kilos, while others bring in at least 15 kilos. The current market price is around 3,350 francs per kilo.”
A long journey for sambaza in Rwanda
Sibomana Jean Bosco, head of the Karongi branch of the national fisheries project, hailed the progress in Rwanda’s fishing industry, recalling that sambaza were first introduced into Lake Kivu in 1959 from Lake Tanganyika, with commercial fishing beginning in 1978.
“At that time, many Rwandans didn’t eat sambaza - people believed it was harmful to cows and milk,” he explained. “Fishermen used to throw sambaza back into the lake because there were no buyers. Today, the situation is completely different: the market demand is strong, and people understand the nutritional value of fish products.”
Challenges remain despite progress
While celebrating the bumper catch, Kwitonda Théoneste, chairperson of the Karongi District Commission for Monitoring Fish Quality and Pricing, warned that illegal fishing remains a challenge.
“Although we’ve made good progress against poaching, some illegal fishers still sneaked onto the lake during the closure period,” he said.
Officials say continued collaboration between cooperatives, local government, and law enforcement will be key to maintaining sustainable fishing practices and protecting the lake’s ecosystem.
*Rwanda Needs $12 Billion to Cut Greenhouse Gas Emissions by 2035
The Rwanda Environment Management Authority (REMA) has announced that the country will require over $12 billion in funding to achieve its target of reducing greenhouse gas emissions by 53% by 2035, as part of its long-term climate resilience strategy.
According to REMA, the investment will support nationwide efforts to transition toward a low-carbon and climate-resilient economy, with key funding directed toward renewable energy, sustainable agriculture, green infrastructure, and industrial efficiency.
A substantial share of the funds will go to clean energy projects, including the expansion of solar power and other renewable sources. Rwanda also plans to promote electric mobility, reduce dependence on fossil fuels such as wood and coal, and scale up the use of biogas and methane gas generation.
Officials estimate that these measures could cut up to 6.24 million tonnes (Mt) of greenhouse gas emissions.
Beyond energy, part of the investment will focus on enhancing energy efficiency in both industrial and household activities to make clean energy affordable and sustainable across the country.
The initiative aligns with Rwanda’s Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) under the Paris Agreement, reaffirming its commitment to environmental protection and a green economy.

*Rwandan Private Sector Says Border Incident with Burundi Sparked Local Maize Seed Production
The Head of Advocacy at the Private Sector Federation (PSF), Callixte Kanamugire, has revealed that a 2013 border standoff with Burundi over a shipment of maize seeds pushed Rwanda to accelerate local seed production and reduce reliance on imports.
Speaking during the PSF General Assembly held in Kigali on October 17, 2025, Kanamugire said the private sector has been instrumental in cutting Rwanda’s import dependence by investing in domestic production.
For years, Rwanda imported agricultural seeds, especially maize, from Zambia, Kenya and Tanzania due to limited local production. However, Kanamugire recalled a turning point more than a decade ago when Burundi blocked Rwandan-bound trucks carrying maize seeds from Zambia.
“Trucks came from Zambia, about 18 or 20, and reached the Burundi-Rwanda border. The Burundians refused to let them cross. That was around 2013,” Kanamugire recounted. “The maize had to be returned to Bujumbura, shipped back to Zambia and re-exported to Rwanda through Tanzania.”
The disruption delayed maize planting by up to three weeks and caused significant losses, affecting harvests and crippling maize processing factories that relied on the delayed supply.
According to Kanamugire, the incident exposed Rwanda’s vulnerability to external supply shocks and “woke up the private sector,” which began investing in seed multiplication programs to boost local production.
“Within two years, maize seeds started being produced in Rwanda,” he said. “The country needed 3,500 tonnes per quarter, and local producers were already supplying 5,000 tonnes. This shows the potential we have but are not fully using.”
Call for Import Substitution
Kanamugire emphasized that Rwanda must increase domestic production to cut imports and save foreign currency.
“Buying goods from abroad consumes a lot of foreign exchange. We must produce locally and export to earn forex, which can be used for essential imports like medicines and medical equipment,” he said.
Rwanda currently has 85 large-scale agro-processing industries and 608 small-scale processing plants. Despite this growth, the country still relies heavily on imports. In 2024, Rwanda imported goods worth $6.5 billion, up from $3.1 billion in 2017, leaving a trade deficit of $2.3 billion.
The government has recently prioritized agricultural transformation and industrialization to reduce its import bill. Last year, Rwanda also announced that fruits consumed locally are now being sourced from within the country, further boosting import substitution efforts.
*Gisagara Women Save Over Rwf 140 Million Through Community Groups
Women in Gisagara District have saved more than Rwf 143 million through small neighborhood savings groups, a model they say has transformed their economic lives and strengthened financial independence in rural communities.
The achievement was celebrated in Kibilizi Sector during Rwanda’s commemoration of the 28th International Day of Rural Women.
According to district figures, the savings come from 126 groups, 85% of whose members are women. Each group is made up of 25 members who contribute Rwf 500 per week, with a maximum of five shares per person, equivalent to Rwf 2,500 weekly. The groups distribute accumulated funds based on individual contributions and savings records.
Members also say the initiative has enabled them to pay Mutuelle de Santé (community-based health insurance) and invest in small businesses without difficulty-seen as one of the most immediate benefits of the saving scheme.
Women Turning Savings into Business Capital
The groups not only save money but also engage in income-generating activities using simple digital financial services. As of October 1, 2025, one group had saved Rwf 2 million, while another had invested Rwf 6 million in trading.
One member, Mukaneza Clémentine, shared her success story. Initially a rice and cassava farmer, she said agriculture alone was not lifting her out of poverty. After joining a savings group, she took a loan of Rwf 200,000 to start trading electronically. With discipline and training from World Relief Rwanda, her capital has now grown to over Rwf 1.5 million. She has also built a family home and runs a boutique selling electronic accessories.
Another member, Bampire Béatha, from the KOABIDU Maize Cooperative, which brings together over 700 women, testified that saving culture has transformed their cooperative. They now plan to buy a transport vehicle and build commercial structures worth over Rwf 50 million.
World Relief Rwanda Says Savings Model Reduces Poverty
Yannick Niyireba, Project Manager at World Relief Rwanda, said the organization works with more than 400 savings groups, including those in Gisagara. He noted that over 80% of participants improve their livelihoods significantly through savings, while over 50% move out of extreme poverty categories.
“These groups teach financial discipline, loan management and confidence to invest. Even farmers are able to borrow and repay after harvest without falling into debt traps,” he explained.
Local Leaders Endorse Savings Culture
The Deputy Mayor of Gisagara District in charge of Economic Development, Jean Paul Habineza, hailed women-led savings and credit groups as a powerful driver of development.
“Beyond helping pay for Mutuelle and Ejo Heza long-term savings, these groups support entrepreneurship and reduce financial waste. They empower women and strengthen families,” he said.
Local leaders and partners called for continued support to expand the initiative across rural communities, promoting financial literacy and economic resilience among women.

*Rwandan Prisons See Gradual Decline in Overcrowding, Yet Some Facilities Remain Strained
Rwanda’s National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) has reported that the country’s prison population has decreased, though several facilities remain overcrowded, according to its 2024/2025 activity report.
As of August 31, 2025, 74,253 people were held in Rwanda’s 14 prisons, including 68,944 men, 4,773 women, 516 boys, and 20 girls. The overall prison overcrowding rate has dropped from 134.3% to 110%, representing a 24.3% reduction, but certain facilities continue to operate beyond capacity.
Most Overcrowded Prisons
Rubavu Prison: Holds 6,214 inmates, including 1,206 convicted of genocide, with a capacity of 4,480. Overcrowding stands at 138.7%.
Rwamagana Prison: 17,046 prisoners, 2,258 convicted of genocide, capacity 13,797 (123.5% overcrowded).
Muhanga Prison: 6,544 inmates, 700 for genocide, capacity 5,250 (124.6% overcrowded).
Nyarugenge Prison (Mageragere): 10,785 inmates, 1,126 genocide convicts, capacity 8,662 (124.5% overcrowded).
Rusizi Prison: 3,069 inmates, 792 for genocide, capacity 2,500 (122.7% overcrowded).
Huye Prison: 10,146 inmates, 3,841 genocide convicts, capacity 9,700 (104.5% overcrowded).
Bugesera Prison: 2,879 inmates, 1,017 genocide convicts, capacity 2,800 (102.8% overcrowded).
Prisons Operating Below Capacity
Nyamagabe Prison: 1,839 inmates, 833 genocide convicts, capacity 3,035 (60.5% capacity).
Mulindi Military Prison: 610 inmates, 12 genocide convicts, capacity 800.
Musanze Prison: 4,886 inmates, 150 genocide convicts, capacity 5,887 (82.9% capacity).

The report emphasizes that while Rwanda has made progress in reducing overcrowding, several major facilities, particularly those housing genocide convicts, continue to exceed capacity, highlighting the need for ongoing reforms in the corrections system.
*Rwanda Reaffirms Commitment to Refugee Rights and Welfare
Rwanda has reiterated its dedication to protecting the rights of refugees, ensuring they live in security and dignity across the country, according to the Rwanda Human Rights Commission (NHRC). Chairperson Umurungi Providence highlighted these achievements while presenting the Commission’s 2024/2025 report and outlining plans for 2025/2026.
Refugee Population and Distribution
Monitoring activities covered five refugee camps, two temporary sites, and urban areas. The NHRC recorded a total of 135,952 refugees, including:
Camps: 120,618 (27,372 men, 32,989 women, 30,185 boys, 30,072 girls)
Temporary camps: 3,627 (1,138 men, 977 women, 749 boys, 763 girls)
Urban and other areas: 11,707 (5,457 men, 2,718 women, 1,842 boys, 1,690 girls)
As of October 10, 2025, Rwanda hosted 137,773 refugees, including 83,923 Congolese, 52,614 Burundians, and 1,236 from other countries.
Rights and Freedoms
The Commission confirmed that refugees enjoy full civil, political, economic, social, and cultural rights. They can move freely within Rwanda and, except for their country of origin, travel internationally using a refugee travel document issued by the National Immigration Agency.
Refugees and asylum seekers are provided with identification documents. Refugee identity cards, now valid for 10 years instead of five, allow holders to access services and, if they meet legal requirements, marry before a civil registry office.
Access to Justice and Security
Refugees are represented in courts for legal disputes or criminal accusations, with support from Prison Fellowship Rwanda. Security within camps is maintained jointly by the Rwandan Police, refugee patrols, and the Judicial Police Department.
Umurungi noted that infrastructure improvements are needed, citing the Mahama camp, where poor road conditions and limited electricity pose safety risks.
Livelihoods and Assistance
The Commission found that many refugees are economically active, engaging in agriculture, livestock, handicrafts, and small businesses. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization provides monthly assistance based on vulnerability:
Most needy: Rwf 5,600 per person
Least needy: Rwf 2,800 per person
Despite these efforts, some refugees in the most vulnerable categories continue to face financial difficulties. The Commission also reported isolated cases of fraud by refugees using small loans.
Access to Services
Rwanda ensures refugees have access to education, employment, healthcare, and other essential services, demonstrating the country’s ongoing commitment to integrating displaced populations safely and respectfully.
The NHRC plans to continue monitoring refugee rights and addressing infrastructure, safety, and livelihood challenges in the coming year.

*UN to Cut 25% of Peacekeeping Troops Amid U.S. Funding Reductions
NEW YORK - The United Nations has announced plans to reduce its global peacekeeping force by 25%, a move prompted by significant U.S. funding cuts under President Donald Trump’s foreign aid reduction plan.
According to a senior UN official, between 13,000 and 14,000 troops and police officers will be withdrawn from the organization’s nine active peacekeeping missions in the coming months. The cuts will impact several regions, including Africa and the Middle East, with the Somalia mission among those expected to be most affected.
Currently, the UN maintains around 50,000 uniformed personnel across its peacekeeping operations. The new directive also includes a 15% reduction in the UN’s military budget for the current fiscal year, as part of broader cost-saving measures.
The largest missions - including those in the Democratic Republic of Congo (MONUSCO), the Central African Republic (MINUSCA), South Sudan (UNMISS), Lebanon (UNIFIL), Cyprus (UNFICYP), and Kosovo (UNMIK) - are expected to undergo significant troop drawdowns.
The decision comes amid growing concerns about the impact of reduced peacekeeping capacity on conflict zones already facing instability and humanitarian crises. Analysts warn that troop cuts could weaken fragile security gains and limit the UN’s ability to protect civilians in volatile regions.
Despite these concerns, UN leadership has defended the move as a necessary adjustment to shifting global priorities and funding realities, emphasizing that peacebuilding and local capacity-building efforts will remain central to the organization’s mission.
*Shield Tech Hub Leads Rwanda’s Fight Against Cybercrime
Kigali, Rwanda - Shield Tech Hub, a Rwandan cybersecurity company based at Norrsken House Kigali, is gaining recognition for its innovative approach to fighting cybercrime. Founded three years ago by technology expert Joel Gashayija, the firm infiltrates cybercriminal networks to uncover their methods and alert Rwandan companies to potential threats before attacks occur.
The company currently serves over 30 public and private organizations, providing digital defense services, security audits, and specialized training. It also collaborates closely with the National Cyber Security Authority (NCSA) to build local expertise.
Shield Tech Hub showcased its work during the launch of the International Model Institute for Cyber Security and Privacy (Cyber Academy) - a new center expected to train more than 200 cybersecurity professionals annually, with women representing at least 30% of trainees.
One of Shield Tech Hub’s signature innovations is Threat Informant, a Rwandan-built system that scans dark web markets to detect leaked corporate data such as passwords, financial information, and internal documents. The system analyzes these threats and provides actionable intelligence to clients, helping them close security gaps.
“When we tell them what cybercriminals are planning, they strengthen their systems and eliminate vulnerabilities,” said Gashayija. “This is homegrown technology built in Rwanda.”
The company’s team of 13 trained young professionals-many recruited as students-has become part of Rwanda’s growing pool of cybersecurity specialists. Yet, Gashayija acknowledges that as cybercriminals evolve their tactics, constant innovation is required to stay ahead.
According to Interpol’s 2025 Cybersecurity Report, cybercrime in East and West Africa has risen by 30% since 2019, costing the continent more than $3 billion. To address these threats, Rwanda is investing in education and capacity-building through initiatives such as the Cyber Academy, the University of Rwanda Coding Academy, and national programs aimed at training one million coding experts and 500,000 advanced technology specialists by 2029.

Conclusion :
This report illustrates a marked contrast between Rwanda's domestic situation and the tense regional context in which the country operates. Internally, Rwanda is demonstrating sustained development momentum, marked by strategic investments in road infrastructure, health (with EU support for vaccine production), and local economic empowerment, particularly for women and SMEs.
However, this progress is overshadowed by persistent tensions with the Democratic Republic of Congo. Despite attempts at dialogue within the Washington and Doha peace processes, deep mistrust persists. Mutual accusations between Presidents Tshisekedi and Kagame, contradictions within the Congolese government itself, and, above all, the extremely volatile security situation in eastern DRC-with clashes involving the M23, the Congolese army, and various militias-continue to threaten regional stability.
In summary, while Rwanda is consolidating its economic and social development, its geopolitical environment remains precarious, with the resolution of the conflict in eastern DRC being the key to lasting peace in the Great Lakes region.
