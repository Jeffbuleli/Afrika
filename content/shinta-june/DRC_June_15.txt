*Burundi Calls for Stronger International Action Over Continued Violence in Eastern DRC
Bujumbura, June 4, 2026 - Burundi has urged the international community to increase its engagement in addressing the ongoing insecurity in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, citing continued fighting and what it describes as non-compliance with the Washington Peace Agreement.

Bizimana expressed concern that despite diplomatic efforts linked to the Washington process-facilitated by international partners including the United States-violence involving the Congolese army and the AFC/M23 rebellion continues in parts of eastern DRC. He said the persistence of hostilities has led to renewed displacement and insecurity in several areas.
He also noted that while some progress has been made, including the return of displaced populations in certain regions, other zones remain unstable, calling for stronger international coordination to support regional peace initiatives.

Congolese Minister Floribert Anzuluni welcomed Burundi’s continued diplomatic and humanitarian support, particularly its assistance to Congolese refugees hosted in Burundi.
Burundi reiterated its commitment to regional peace efforts and its role within African Union frameworks aimed at stabilizing conflict-affected areas.
The statement comes amid ongoing tensions in eastern DRC, where multiple armed actors remain active despite repeated ceasefire agreements and international mediation efforts. Both Kinshasa and regional partners continue to accuse various parties of violating peace commitments, further complicating implementation of the Washington agreement.

*Global Witness Investigation Alleges Smuggled DRC Coltan Reached Global Supply Chains via Rwanda
Kinshasa, June 13, 2026 - A new investigation by the international non-governmental organization Global Witness alleges that smuggled coltan originating from conflict-affected areas of eastern Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) has entered global supply chains through Rwanda, exposing weaknesses in international mineral traceability systems.
In a report published on June 10, Global Witness examined the movement of more than 2,000 tons of coltan allegedly smuggled from the Rubaya mining area in North Kivu, a region estimated to produce a significant share of the world’s tantalum, a mineral widely used in smartphones, laptops, vehicles, and other electronic products.
The investigation claims that the Rubaya mines have become an important revenue source for the AFC/M23 rebellion, which controls parts of eastern DRC. According to the report, minerals extracted from the area are allegedly transported across the border into Rwanda, where their origin becomes difficult to trace.
Global Witness says its year-long investigation tracked the route of coltan from mining sites in eastern DRC to international supply chains, including smuggling networks and export channels. The organization alleges that several Rwanda-based exporters purchased coltan linked to conflict zones before reselling it to processing facilities, particularly in China and Kazakhstan.
According to the report, the coltan is processed into tantalum at smelters before entering the manufacturing chain of electronic components used in consumer technology products. Global Witness further claims that conflict-linked minerals may have entered supply chains connected to several multinational technology and manufacturing companies.
The investigation also raises concerns about the effectiveness of international traceability systems designed to prevent conflict minerals from entering global markets. Global Witness argues that existing mechanisms, including mineral certification and due diligence frameworks, have failed to fully sever links between armed conflict and mineral exploitation in the Great Lakes region.
The report comes amid continued insecurity in eastern DRC, where ongoing fighting and armed group activity continue to raise concerns over the exploitation of natural resources and their role in financing conflict.

*Mining Revenue Audit in DRC: Carter Center Calls for Structural Reforms
KINSHASA, May 13, 2026 - The Carter Center has welcomed the Democratic Republic of Congo’s decision to audit the mining sector, while urging authorities to undertake broader structural reforms to improve transparency and accountability in the management of mineral revenues.
In a statement issued Tuesday, the organization described the audit announced by President Félix Tshisekedi during the 87th Council of Ministers meeting as an important opportunity to strengthen governance in one of the country’s most strategic economic sectors.
The audit is expected to examine compliance with obligations related to the repatriation of mining export revenues, the governance of mining joint ventures, and the management of state-owned mining assets.
“This audit is an important step in addressing long-standing concerns regarding the management of state-owned enterprises and public revenues in the mining sector,” the Carter Center said in its statement.
The organization, which has monitored extractive sector governance in the DRC for more than fifteen years, said it possesses data and expertise that could contribute to the effectiveness of the process.
It referenced findings from its 2017 report, State Affair, which alleged that more than USD 750 million in revenue from mining partnerships involving state-owned mining company Gécamines had not been recorded in official accounts.
The Carter Center further stated that studies conducted between 2013 and 2024, in collaboration with Congolese civil society organizations, identified tax avoidance practices in several major mining projects. According to the organization, these practices may have resulted in nearly USD 4 billion in lost public revenue linked to financial flows owed to the state.
The group also cited findings from a 2025 study on the revised 2018 Mining Code, which identified structural challenges limiting revenue collection, including the complexity of tax systems, weak digitalization, limited institutional capacity, and fragmented tax administration.
To ensure the credibility of the audit, the Carter Center called for greater transparency throughout the process, including the publication of the terms of reference and final results, as well as full access to contracts, partnership registers, and financial statements of state-owned mining companies.
The organization also recommended involving experts in extractive taxation and transfer pricing to assess complex tax avoidance schemes identified in mining operations.
In addition, it urged authorities to include civil society actors and parliamentary oversight mechanisms in monitoring both the audit process and its outcomes.
“The Congolese people have the right to know how state-owned enterprises and the revenues generated by their mineral resources are managed. This audit is an opportunity to provide them with concrete answers,” said Fabien Mayani, as quoted in the statement.
The Carter Center said the initiative should be integrated into broader reforms of state-owned enterprises, mining taxation, and public financial administration to ensure mining wealth contributes more effectively to national development, particularly in communities affected by extraction activities.

Conclusion
The first half of June 2026 highlights the Democratic Republic of Congo’s persistent struggle to balance urgent security threats, political tensions, and evolving economic reforms, all within an increasingly fragile national environment.
1. A deepening security and humanitarian emergency
Eastern DRC remains trapped in a prolonged cycle of violence despite the continued state of siege in North Kivu and Ituri. Armed activity by the ADF in Beni, ongoing confrontations involving AFC/M23 and allied forces, and intermittent clashes with local militias in Maniema have resulted in significant civilian casualties and repeated population displacements. The security situation is further complicated by leadership reshuffles within the military administration in Ituri, reflecting attempts to adapt strategy in the face of limited operational gains. At the same time, the emergence and rapid spread of the Ebola outbreak in the same geographic corridor has intensified pressure on already overstretched health and security systems, creating a dual-layer crisis of conflict and public health.
2. Fragmented diplomacy and the persistence of cross-border instability
Efforts to resolve the conflict remain dispersed across multiple regional and international frameworks, including Washington, Doha, and African Union-led initiatives, with limited coherence in implementation. While MONUSCO and other international actors continue to advocate for coordinated action, progress on the ground remains limited. External pressure through targeted sanctions has begun to reshape diplomatic dynamics, but regional tensions persist. Meanwhile, allegations of illicit mineral flows from conflict-affected zones in eastern Congo toward regional and global supply chains continue to reinforce the economic dimension of the war economy, underscoring the difficulty of separating armed conflict from transnational resource networks.
3. Intensifying political polarization and institutional strain
In Kinshasa, political debate has increasingly shifted toward constitutional and institutional questions, further deepening divisions between the government and opposition. The adoption of legislation related to a potential referendum and the restructuring of constitutional processes has triggered strong resistance from opposition figures and former political leaders, who view these developments as a threat to constitutional term limits. This has fueled protests, strikes, and confrontations in the capital, particularly around symbolic state institutions. The result is a growing perception of institutional confrontation at a time when national cohesion is critically needed to address security and humanitarian emergencies.
4. Economic reforms under pressure from fiscal and global constraints
Despite the difficult environment, the DRC continues to advance significant economic and financial reforms. Improved access to international capital markets, including sovereign bond operations, has contributed to more favorable financing conditions and renewed investor interest. At the same time, initiatives aimed at industrializing the mining sector and strengthening regulatory frameworks reflect a long-term strategy to increase domestic value creation from strategic resources. However, these structural gains are tempered by short-term fiscal pressures, including a downward revision of budget forecasts linked to external revenue declines and global economic volatility. Delays in public salary payments further illustrate the gap between macroeconomic ambition and fiscal execution capacity. Efforts to improve transparency in the extractive sector, including revenue audits supported by international partners, remain central to restoring public trust and strengthening state capacity.
Final Outlook
Overall, the period under review reflects a country operating under sustained multidimensional stress, where progress in economic governance and institutional reform coexists with acute insecurity and political fragmentation. The DRC’s medium-term trajectory will depend on its ability to align political stability, security consolidation, and transparent resource management. Without stronger coordination between governance reforms and on-the-ground realities, structural gains risk being overshadowed by persistent conflict dynamics and recurring humanitarian crises.
