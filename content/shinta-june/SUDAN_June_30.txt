*Sudan Calls for International Backing to End War as Conflict Death Toll Surpasses 30,000
Geneva, June 15, 2026 - Sudan's Attorney General, Intisar Ahmed Abdel Aal, has called for greater international support to end the country's ongoing conflict, urging the United Nations Human Rights Council to back the government's efforts to restore peace and strengthen national accountability mechanisms.
Addressing the 62nd session of the UN Human Rights Council, Abdel Aal said Sudan had registered 149,860 legal cases related to crimes committed since the outbreak of the war, including 385 cases involving members of the regular armed forces whose legal immunity had been lifted.
She argued that Sudan's judicial institutions are capable of investigating and prosecuting violations and called for support for the National Committee for the Investigation of Crimes and Violations of National and International Humanitarian Law under the principle of complementarity. She also urged the Human Rights Council to end the mandate of the international Fact-Finding Mission, accusing it of politicizing its work.
The Attorney General further called for an immediate halt to external support for the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), alleging that foreign actors continue to provide assistance to the paramilitary group. She also appealed to states to prevent the recruitment, financing, and transfer of mercenaries into Sudan.
Abdel Aal accused the RSF of committing widespread violations against civilians, public infrastructure, and state institutions, including alleged war crimes, crimes against humanity, genocide, and serious breaches of national and international humanitarian law.
She said documented violations in Darfur and parts of Kordofan included killings, forced displacement, sexual violence, looting, abductions, and arbitrary detention in facilities allegedly operated by the RSF, including in El Fasher and Nyala.
According to figures presented during the session, investigators have documented 2,200 rape cases and 1,499 cases of detention and enforced disappearance since the conflict began.
The committee also reported that 30,071 people have been killed and 44,061 injured in documented violations. Of the nearly 150,000 legal cases registered, 6,158 were dismissed during investigations, while 21,787 cases were referred for trial and judicial rulings had been issued in 10,417 cases.
Authorities further estimated the preliminary economic losses resulting from the destruction of infrastructure at approximately US$771 billion, highlighting the immense financial cost of the conflict alongside its humanitarian toll.
The appeal came as international concern over the conflict continues to grow, with reports of escalating violence, increased use of drones in military operations, and continued attacks on civilians across several regions of Sudan.

Washington, D.C., June 18, 2026 - The U.S. Senate Foreign Relations Committee has approved legislation that would significantly tighten U.S. financial and diplomatic measures toward Sudan, including blocking support for government loans and debt relief through international financial institutions while expanding sanctions against individuals fueling the conflict.
The proposed Peace in Sudan Act of 2026 directs U.S. representatives at institutions such as the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund to oppose loans, debt relief, or other financial assistance to the Sudanese government, except for emergency humanitarian projects.
The bill also prohibits U.S. foreign assistance for non-humanitarian purposes and bars funding aimed at strengthening Sudanese government institutions or providing security and military assistance.
Under the legislation, the U.S. president would be authorized to impose sanctions on individuals and entities involved in supplying weapons or military training to the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF), the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), or other armed groups. The sanctions would also target those accused of obstructing the formation of a civilian government or interfering with the delivery of humanitarian assistance.
Additional sanctions could be imposed on individuals accused of recruiting child soldiers, committing war crimes, crimes against humanity, or genocide, targeting civilians, trafficking Sudan's natural resources-including gold and gum arabic-or threatening the country's peace, security, stability, and territorial integrity.
The proposed measures include asset freezes as well as visa bans or revocations for designated individuals.
The legislation states that U.S. policy should focus on dismantling networks that profit from the conflict, countering foreign military support for warring parties, promoting accountability for serious human rights violations, and supporting Sudan's transition to a democratic civilian government.
The bill also recognizes ongoing international mediation efforts led by regional and international partners and expresses support for diplomatic initiatives aimed at securing a negotiated settlement to the conflict.
If enacted, the legislation would require the U.S. administration to submit regular reports to Congress detailing foreign military support to Sudan's warring parties, including transfers of weapons, military equipment, drones, and the involvement of foreign fighters.
The reports would also monitor violations of the United Nations arms embargo, activities of non-state armed groups and foreign actors, human rights abuses-including child recruitment, conflict-related sexual violence, starvation tactics, and attacks on civilians and civilian infrastructure-as well as financial networks linked to Sudan's war economy.
In addition, the bill directs the U.S. Secretary of State, in coordination with the Departments of Defense and the Treasury, to develop a comprehensive strategy to support a permanent ceasefire, advance a political settlement, protect civilians, expand humanitarian access, and enhance protections for humanitarian workers and journalists operating in Sudan.

*Economist Says Monetary Measures Alone Cannot Stop Sudanese Pound's Decline
Port Sudan, June 28, 2026 - Sudan's continued currency depreciation has intensified concerns over the country's economic outlook, with an economist warning that administrative and monetary measures alone are unlikely to stabilize the Sudanese Pound (SDG) without restoring domestic production.
Within a week, authorities increased the indicative customs exchange rate for the US dollar from SDG 3,395 to SDG 3,517 and imposed restrictions on the import of several goods in an effort to ease pressure on the national currency. However, the Sudanese Pound continued to weaken, with the US dollar trading at approximately SDG 5,600 on the parallel market.
The widening gap between the official customs exchange rate and the parallel market has highlighted the limited effectiveness of conventional monetary policies amid the country's ongoing conflict and economic disruption.
Despite official data indicating that inflation has eased, economists argue that the reported decline has not translated into improved living conditions. Household purchasing power continues to erode as wages lose value against rising import costs.
According to economic analysis, Sudan's heavy dependence on imported goods, combined with years of declining agricultural and industrial production and the destruction caused by war, has left the economy highly vulnerable to exchange-rate fluctuations.
The apparent decline in inflation has been attributed partly to weaker consumer demand, which has limited traders' ability to increase prices despite persistently high costs. Analysts also point to the "base-year effect," whereby inflation appears lower when compared with exceptionally high price increases recorded during the previous year.
The recent increase in the customs exchange rate is also expected to place additional pressure on import costs. Higher import prices encourage traders to seek foreign currency on the parallel market, increasing demand for US dollars and accelerating the depreciation of the Sudanese Pound, creating a self-reinforcing cycle.
The conflict has also fragmented Sudan into separate currency markets with significant regional differences. Exchange rates in Nyala, under the control of the rival administration in South Darfur, remain considerably lower than those in Port Sudan and Khartoum, reflecting variations in trade routes, access to foreign currency, financial transfers, and local economic conditions.
Port Sudan continues to experience exceptionally high demand for foreign currency as the country's principal seaport and the primary entry point for government and commercial imports, including fuel, food, and medical supplies. In contrast, parts of Darfur have become increasingly dependent on informal trade networks and alternative supply routes through neighboring countries, with reduced circulation of Sudanese currency.
Economists argue that Sudan's currency crisis stems from the collapse of domestic production, shrinking export revenues, expanding money supply during wartime, and growing reliance on imports.
They contend that lasting stabilization of the Sudanese Pound will require rebuilding the country's agricultural, industrial, and mining sectors, increasing export earnings, and restoring productive economic activity rather than relying solely on exchange-rate adjustments and administrative controls.

*Climate Change and Conflict Push Sudan Among World's Most Vulnerable Countries
Khartoum, June 17, 2026 - Sudan has been ranked the fourth most vulnerable country to climate change among 186 nations, as the combined effects of armed conflict, environmental degradation, and climate change continue to intensify pressure on natural resources, livelihoods, and public health.
The country's environmental challenges have deepened amid the ongoing war, which has damaged infrastructure, ecosystems, forests, and agricultural land while accelerating soil degradation and contributing to the spread of disease. Growing displacement, shrinking natural resources, and declining public services have further increased Sudan's vulnerability to climate-related shocks.
Environmental experts warn that climate change remains widely underestimated in Sudan despite its growing impact. They argue that environmental issues are often treated as secondary concerns rather than strategic national priorities, limiting public awareness and policy responses.
According to the latest global climate vulnerability assessment, Sudan ranks fourth worldwide in its inability to withstand and adapt to climate change, reflecting severe weaknesses in environmental resilience and adaptation capacity.
Among the principal challenges identified are the degradation of agricultural land, declining vegetation cover, expanding desertification, and reduced soil moisture. These trends are contributing to lower agricultural productivity, worsening food insecurity, rising food prices, and greater dependence on imports, placing additional strain on the country's economy.
Competition over diminishing natural resources has also been linked to conflict. Analysts note that disputes between farmers and pastoralists in Darfur, which contributed to the outbreak of violence in 2003, have been widely cited as one of the world's earliest examples of a conflict exacerbated by climate-related resource scarcity.
Recurring floods have added to Sudan's environmental challenges. Severe flooding in recent years has damaged infrastructure, displaced communities, and increased outbreaks of malaria, dengue fever, cholera, and other waterborne diseases, creating additional humanitarian and economic burdens.
Experts estimate that Sudan requires billions of dollars annually to strengthen climate resilience and adapt to increasingly severe environmental conditions. They argue that although the country has contributed relatively little to global greenhouse gas emissions, it bears a disproportionate share of climate-related impacts because of its geographic location, reinforcing calls for greater international climate financing and compensation under global climate justice mechanisms.
Environmental specialists also stress that the ongoing conflict has compounded existing weaknesses in environmental governance. Limited coordination among government institutions, inadequate early warning systems, and shortages of technical expertise have reduced the country's ability to respond effectively to environmental risks.
Despite the challenges, some temporary environmental recovery has been observed during periods of reduced human activity, including the natural regeneration of acacia forests in conflict-affected areas. However, much of this recovery has been reversed as displaced populations returned and increasingly relied on firewood because of the lack of alternative energy sources and environmental oversight.
Experts are calling for stronger environmental governance, improved coordination among state institutions, expanded investment in climate adaptation, greater use of renewable energy, and the establishment of mechanisms to secure international climate finance and compensation to help Sudan address the mounting environmental and humanitarian consequences of both climate change and war.

The report also found evidence that sexual violence has been used as retaliation against individuals perceived to support opposing parties and as part of ethnically motivated attacks. Survivors from the Masalit community in West Darfur described being targeted after attackers identified their ethnic background.
The UN High Commissioner for Human Rights called for prompt, independent, and impartial investigations into all allegations of conflict-related sexual violence, emphasizing that persistent impunity continues to fuel further abuses.
The report urges all parties to the conflict to adopt concrete measures to prevent sexual violence, while calling on the international community to ensure that accountability, justice, and support for survivors remain central components of peace efforts and any future ceasefire negotiations.
Humanitarian and human rights organizations have continued to warn that widespread insecurity, limited medical services, stigma surrounding sexual violence, and restricted humanitarian access have prevented many survivors from receiving medical care, psychological support, and legal assistance, suggesting that the documented cases likely represent only a fraction of the true scale of the crisis.

*UN Warns Drone Strikes and Sexual Violence Are Intensifying Sudan's Conflict

The Fact-Finding Mission called for the immediate release of individuals held without legal basis, improved conditions for detainees, unrestricted access for humanitarian and human rights organizations to detention facilities, and stronger international efforts to ensure accountability for alleged international crimes.

Conclusion
The reporting period from June 17 to June 30, 2026 highlights the continued deterioration of Sudan’s political, security, economic, and humanitarian landscape. Despite ongoing diplomatic initiatives and international engagement, the conflict remains entrenched, with expanding military operations, worsening civilian suffering, mounting economic pressures, and growing regional implications. The cumulative developments during the period demonstrate that Sudan's crisis has become increasingly multidimensional, requiring coordinated political, humanitarian, economic, and security responses.
1. Intensifying Conflict and Protection Risks
Armed confrontations continued to expand across Darfur, Blue Nile, Kordofan, and other contested regions, with both the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) claiming successive territorial gains while neither side achieved a decisive military advantage. The increasing use of drone strikes against urban centers, markets, fuel facilities, and civilian infrastructure has further blurred the distinction between military and civilian targets, deepening humanitarian needs and disrupting essential services.
International organizations-including the United Nations, the European Union, and human rights bodies-issued repeated warnings over the growing risk of mass atrocities, particularly in El Obeid, while new UN documentation on conflict-related sexual violence confirmed the widespread and systematic use of rape, sexual slavery, forced marriage, and other forms of sexual violence throughout the conflict. These findings reinforce concerns that serious violations of international humanitarian and human rights law continue to occur with widespread impunity.
2. Increasing International Involvement and Geopolitical Competition
The reporting period also reflected growing international engagement in Sudan, although with contrasting approaches. The European Union resumed direct diplomatic engagement through a high-level mission to Sudan, emphasizing humanitarian access, civilian protection, and support for international mediation efforts.
At the same time, the United States significantly expanded pressure on the conflict by advancing new legislation restricting financial assistance to Sudan, imposing additional sanctions targeting networks supporting both the SAF and RSF, and tightening aviation and financial restrictions under the Chemical and Biological Weapons Control and Warfare Elimination Act. Conversely, China reinforced its economic relationship with Sudan by cancelling approximately US$50 million in debt, expanding development cooperation, and pursuing further investment opportunities, including proposed mining projects that have generated domestic political opposition.
Meanwhile, allegations involving Egyptian military operations against Sudanese miners along the northern border added another layer of regional tension, highlighting the increasingly transnational dimensions of Sudan's conflict.
3. Economic Decline Amid Institutional Fragmentation
Sudan's economic crisis continued to deepen during the reporting period. The Sudanese Pound experienced further depreciation on parallel markets despite government monetary interventions, while inflation, shortages of essential goods, and disruptions to productive sectors continued to erode household purchasing power.
Economic analysts increasingly argued that administrative and monetary measures alone cannot stabilize the economy without restoring agricultural, industrial, and mining production. Simultaneously, fragmented territorial control has contributed to the emergence of multiple parallel economic systems with differing exchange rates, supply chains, and financial networks, further complicating national economic governance.
4. Worsening Humanitarian Conditions and Displacement
Humanitarian conditions continued to deteriorate across the country. Renewed fighting displaced thousands of civilians in Blue Nile, Darfur, and Kordofan, while repeated attacks on infrastructure intensified shortages of fuel, water, electricity, healthcare, and food.
Sudanese refugees and displaced populations also faced increasing insecurity outside the country. Reports documented rising hostility toward Sudanese refugees in Libya, growing protection concerns for journalists in exile, and continued challenges for displaced communities seeking humanitarian assistance and durable protection. These developments illustrate that Sudan's humanitarian crisis now extends well beyond its borders.
Final Assessment
Overall, developments during the second half of June 2026 demonstrate that Sudan remains trapped in a protracted and increasingly complex crisis. Military confrontation continues to drive humanitarian deterioration, economic decline, institutional fragmentation, and regional instability, while diplomatic initiatives have yet to produce meaningful progress toward a comprehensive ceasefire or political settlement.
Without sustained international engagement, improved humanitarian access, accountability for violations of international law, and an inclusive political process capable of addressing the underlying drivers of the conflict, Sudan is likely to face continued instability, worsening humanitarian conditions, and further fragmentation in the months ahead.
