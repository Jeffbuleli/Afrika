*Experts Push for Mobile Money Tax Reform as Users Shift to Alternative Banking Channels
Kampala, June 2, 2026 - Financial experts have called for reforms to Uganda’s digital payments tax system, proposing a reduction in the mobile money withdrawal tax from 0.5 percent to 0.25 percent amid growing signs that consumers are shifting to mobile and agency banking services to reduce transaction costs.
The proposal comes as mobile money continues to dominate Uganda’s digital payments ecosystem, with active accounts reaching 33.7 million by 2025 and annual transaction values nearing Shs195 trillion. The platform remains central to financial inclusion and everyday commerce, particularly for low-value, high-frequency transactions commonly used by households, small businesses, and informal sector operators.
Industry figures indicate that mobile money continues to play a critical role in Uganda’s economy, with millions of users relying on the service for daily financial transactions. However, experts argue that rising transaction costs, particularly withdrawal taxes, are increasingly influencing consumer behaviour.
Analysts say many users are gradually turning to agency banking and mobile banking platforms, which often offer lower transaction costs for similar services. Recent industry data shows agency banking transaction values increased significantly between 2024 and 2025, accompanied by substantial growth in transaction volumes and the number of registered agents across the country.
Experts argue that the trend reflects a broader structural shift in digital finance, with consumers increasingly opting for cheaper alternatives to avoid the cumulative impact of repeated withdrawal charges.
They further contend that Uganda’s current tax structure creates an uneven playing field because mobile money withdrawals attract higher taxation than comparable banking transactions, potentially distorting competition within the digital payments sector.
Tax specialists have also noted that Uganda imposes a comparatively heavier tax burden on mobile money withdrawals than several neighbouring East African countries, where excise duties are generally applied without additional withdrawal taxes.
While acknowledging the need for domestic revenue mobilisation, experts warn that excessive taxation could discourage the use of digital financial services and slow progress toward broader financial inclusion.
They have proposed a lower but more evenly distributed tax across digital payment platforms, arguing that such an approach would maintain government revenue while reducing incentives for consumers to migrate from one platform to another based solely on cost considerations.
Supporters of the reform say a more neutral tax regime would encourage wider adoption of digital payments, strengthen competition between financial service providers, and support Uganda’s transition toward a less cash-dependent economy.
As debates over taxation and financial inclusion continue, digital payment policy is expected to remain central to discussions surrounding innovation, access to financial services, and economic growth in Uganda.

*Bank of Uganda Caps Cash Withdrawals in Drive Toward Digital Payments
Kampala, June 3, 2026 - The Bank of Uganda (BoU) has announced new limits on over-the-counter cash withdrawals, beginning January 1, 2027, as part of a broader strategy to accelerate digital payments, reduce dependence on cash, and modernise the country’s financial system.
Under the new regulations, individual account holders will be limited to cash withdrawals of up to Shs50 million per day and Shs500 million per week. Corporate entities and businesses will face higher thresholds, capped at Shs250 million per day and Shs2.5 billion per week.
The central bank said the policy marks a significant shift in Uganda’s payments landscape, aimed at increasing transparency in financial transactions and encouraging wider adoption of digital financial services.
According to BoU, the withdrawal restrictions will apply to all accounts held with supervised financial institutions. However, transactions conducted through electronic payment channels, including Real Time Gross Settlement (RTGS) transfers and Electronic Funds Transfers (EFTs), will not be affected.
Authorities say the decision was informed by changing consumer behaviour and the rapid growth of electronic transactions across the country, with digital payment systems increasingly preferred for their efficiency and convenience.
The central bank noted that electronic credit transfers have become one of Uganda’s most widely used payment methods, reflecting growing public confidence in digital financial services among individuals, businesses, and government institutions.
BoU acknowledged that certain sectors of the economy continue to rely heavily on cash transactions and said safeguards would be introduced to avoid disrupting legitimate business activities.
Under the guidelines, supervised financial institutions will be required to establish risk-based customer profiles that may allow for differentiated withdrawal limits depending on a customer’s transaction history and business activities.
Commercial banks and other regulated institutions will also be allowed to seek exceptional approval from the central bank for customers, transactions, or sectors requiring withdrawals beyond the prescribed limits.
The regulator further directed financial institutions to encourage customers to adopt alternative payment channels, including RTGS, EFT, and mobile money bank-to-wallet transfer services.
Financial analysts say the policy could significantly affect businesses and individuals that routinely depend on large cash transactions, particularly in sectors where cash remains dominant. However, the move is also expected to accelerate Uganda’s transition to electronic payments and deepen financial inclusion.
The withdrawal limits form part of Uganda’s broader e-payments strategy aimed at building a modern, secure, and efficient financial system capable of supporting economic growth and digital transformation.
BoU says it remains committed to developing an innovative, inclusive, and resilient national payments system to improve the safety, accessibility, and efficiency of financial services across the country.

*Uganda Retains Highest Treasury Bill Rate in East Africa Despite Regional Decline
Kampala, June 10, 2026 - Uganda maintained the highest 91-day Treasury Bill rate among major East African Community (EAC) economies during the first quarter of 2026, despite a broader regional decline in short-term government borrowing costs.
Latest regional monetary data shows Uganda’s 91-day Treasury Bill rate stood at 9.9 percent in Q1 2026, placing the country at the top of East Africa’s interest rate rankings. However, the figure marked a decline from the 10.7 percent recorded during the fourth quarter of 2025.
Burundi registered the second-highest Treasury Bill rate at 8.4 percent, followed closely by Rwanda at 8.3 percent. Kenya recorded a rate of 7.5 percent during the same period, while Tanzania posted the lowest rate among the five economies at 4.2 percent.
The difference between Uganda and Tanzania’s Treasury Bill rates stood at 5.7 percentage points, highlighting significant variations in short-term borrowing costs across the region.
Compared to the previous quarter, Treasury Bill rates declined in Uganda, Burundi, Kenya, and Tanzania. Rwanda was the only country to register an increase, with its rate rising from 7.6 percent in Q4 2025 to 8.3 percent in Q1 2026.
The figures indicate shifting monetary conditions across East Africa, even as Uganda retained the region’s highest short-term government borrowing cost.
Regional lending rates remained relatively stable during the quarter, although Uganda recorded the largest increase, with lending rates rising by 90 basis points.
At the same time, deposit rates generally declined across partner states, while interest rate spreads continued to vary considerably among regional economies. South Sudan recorded the highest interest rate spread at 15.9 percent, compared to Tanzania’s 6.8 percent, the lowest in the bloc.
Broader monetary indicators also pointed to continued economic activity across the region. Year-on-year growth in broad money supply, measured by M3, rose by 16.4 percent during the first quarter of 2026, while credit to the private sector expanded by 14 percent, reflecting sustained lending activity.
Net foreign assets also registered strong growth, increasing by 26 percent in the fourth quarter of 2025.
Analysts say the figures suggest that while Uganda continues to offer the region’s highest Treasury Bill returns, broader monetary conditions across the East African Community remain supportive of economic growth and private sector expansion.

*Uganda Unveils Climate Resilience Framework to Improve Credit Ratings and Attract Investment

For Uganda, the issue carries particular significance given the country’s reliance on agriculture and agro-industrialisation as central pillars of its economic transformation agenda. Officials note that prolonged droughts, floods, environmental degradation, and changing weather patterns continue to pose risks to agricultural productivity, food security, and long-term economic stability.
Government officials maintain that climate adaptation and resilience investments are essential to protecting development gains and strengthening macroeconomic stability.
The Ministry of Finance further argues that current global financial systems often focus on the immediate fiscal costs of resilience projects while overlooking their long-term economic and financial benefits, creating challenges for countries investing heavily in climate preparedness.
Officials say this imbalance has created a financing paradox in which countries making significant investments to reduce future climate risks may still face rising borrowing costs and constrained fiscal space.
Uganda has expressed optimism that the framework could influence broader reforms in international financial systems and encourage other countries to adopt similar approaches to evaluating resilience investments.
The initiative also aligns with Uganda’s growing role in international climate finance discussions and broader efforts to strengthen sustainable development financing.
Analysts say the framework could reshape how financial institutions, investors, and credit rating agencies assess climate-related investments by recognising the economic value of proactive resilience measures.
The move is being viewed as a significant step toward aligning global financial systems with climate realities while positioning Uganda as a pioneer in efforts to redefine how sustainable development investments are valued in sovereign credit assessments.

Conclusion
The first half of June 2026 presents a defining snapshot of Uganda at a moment of accelerated economic ambition, institutional reform, and persistent social and political pressure points.
Economic and Technological Momentum
Uganda’s macroeconomic outlook remains strongly expansionary, underpinned by record export performance reaching approximately $18.04 billion (about Shs68.55 trillion) over the 12 months to March 2026. The growth is driven primarily by gold and coffee, reinforcing the country’s strengthening position in global commodity and regional trade markets and contributing to a 15-year-high external surplus.
Government policy continues to prioritise structural transformation. A Shs1.6 trillion capital injection into the Uganda Development Bank signals a deliberate push to expand affordable credit for industrial producers and large-scale agriculture, while the forthcoming Shs84.4 trillion FY2026/27 budget reflects an increasingly expansionary, development-focused fiscal strategy aimed at boosting household incomes and productivity.
At the same time, financial system reforms are accelerating. The Bank of Uganda’s move to cap large cash withdrawals marks a clear shift toward a more digital-first economy, reinforcing formalisation and transparency in transactions. However, this transition is accompanied by growing debate over mobile money taxation, with stakeholders warning that uneven cost structures could distort user behaviour and undermine financial inclusion goals.
Political and Social Friction
Despite strong macroeconomic signals, the political environment remains contested and sensitive. Internal tensions within the ruling establishment are increasingly visible, particularly around unmet expectations among party supporters following electoral cycles and governance promises.
Opposition actors are simultaneously sharpening their scrutiny of state performance. Criticism of national addresses has centred on concerns over accountability frameworks and the perceived reliance on long historical comparisons rather than measurable annual performance indicators. Meanwhile, opposition formations continue to navigate internal consolidation challenges and heightened political scrutiny, including high-profile arrests and disputes that amplify debates on political space and democratic freedoms.
The result is a political landscape marked less by ideological contestation than by questions of trust, delivery, and institutional legitimacy across both ruling and opposition actors.
Public Health and Social Pressure Points
The health sector reflects a dual reality of expansion and strain. Funding has risen significantly to over Shs5 trillion, supporting infrastructure upgrades, medicine supply chains, and expanded diagnostic capacity across regional facilities. Yet persistent gaps in human resource financing-particularly the omission of Shs28 billion for medical interns-risk weakening service delivery in already overstretched hospitals.
Beyond systemic financing concerns, Uganda continues to grapple with layered public health vulnerabilities. Cross-border coordination with the Democratic Republic of Congo on Ebola response demonstrates proactive regional health security planning, while growing advocacy around men’s mental health highlights an often-overlooked crisis shaped by stigma, economic pressure, and limited access to psychosocial support.
Citizen Security and Rule of Law Challenges
Security trends reveal ongoing strain between enforcement efforts and underlying social drivers of violence. While official data points to shifts in crime patterns, incidents of mob violence remain a recurring challenge, complicating law enforcement efforts and raising concerns about public trust in formal justice systems.
Other flashpoints-including violent land disputes, fatal attacks in rural and peri-urban areas, and operational incidents involving security forces-underscore the complexity of maintaining public order amid socio-economic tensions. At the same time, targeted crackdowns on reckless youth driving networks reflect increasing state response to emerging urban disorder linked to mobility, youth unemployment, and informal group activity.
Summary Outlook
Overall, Uganda’s mid-2026 trajectory reflects a dual reality: a state advancing ambitious economic, fiscal, and institutional reforms, while simultaneously contending with political dissatisfaction, social stressors, and governance enforcement challenges.
Long-term stability and growth will depend not only on sustaining macroeconomic momentum and pioneering policy initiatives-such as climate-linked sovereign risk frameworks-but also on addressing structural vulnerabilities in public service delivery, strengthening accountability across political institutions, and restoring public confidence in justice and security systems at the community level.
