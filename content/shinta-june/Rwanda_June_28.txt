*Rwanda to Roll Out Disease-Resistant Potato Seeds to Boost Production
Kigali, Rwanda, June 21, 2026 - Rwanda is set to introduce improved potato seed varieties that are resistant to late blight disease, a move expected to significantly increase yields while reducing farmers' dependence on pesticides.
The Rwanda Agriculture and Animal Resources Development Board (RAB) announced that it has partnered with the International Potato Center (CIP) to develop and distribute the enhanced seeds following two years of research.
The research focused on improving the widely cultivated Victoria and Ndamira potato varieties by incorporating resistance to late blight, a disease that has long reduced potato yields and forced farmers to rely heavily on chemical pesticides.
Field trials conducted at RAB research stations in Musanze, Rwerere and Nyamagabe showed substantial increases in productivity.
According to RAB, the improved Victoria variety, enhanced with the Vict.185 resistance trait, can produce up to 33.93 tonnes per hectare without pesticide application, compared with about 2.75 tonnes per hectare for the conventional variety under the same conditions.
Similarly, the improved Ndamira variety, enhanced with the Sha.105 resistance trait, can yield up to 30.81 tonnes per hectare, compared with 9.15 tonnes per hectare for the traditional variety.
RAB said the research phase has been completed and preparations are underway to multiply and distribute the improved seed to farmers. The agency also plans to apply the same technology to other popular potato varieties, including Cyerecyezo and Kinigi, as part of efforts to increase national potato production by 48 percent by 2029.
The International Potato Center said the improved varieties are cultivated using the same farming practices as conventional potatoes, with the major advantage being their resistance to late blight and the reduced need for fungicides.
The Rwanda Inspectorate, Competition and Consumer Protection Authority (RICA) said it is expediting the registration process so the new varieties can be released to farmers in the next planting season.
Potato growers have welcomed the innovation, saying it will significantly reduce production costs. Farmers noted that fungicides and spraying services currently account for hundreds of thousands of Rwandan francs per hectare, making potato cultivation increasingly expensive.
Potatoes are Rwanda's second most consumed staple crop after cassava and remain one of the country's most important food and cash crops. Government statistics show potato production has grown substantially over the past decade alongside an expansion in cultivated land, reflecting the crop's growing importance to Rwanda's agricultural sector.

*Rwanda Advances $560 Million Lake Kivu Methane Gas Project

Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, June 22, 2026 - Rwanda, the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) and the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) have agreed on a series of measures to accelerate the voluntary repatriation of refugees, following a ministerial meeting held in Addis Ababa.
The meeting, held from June 20 to 21, reviewed progress in implementing the tripartite agreement on the voluntary return of Congolese refugees from Rwanda and Rwandan refugees from the DRC.
In a joint communiqué issued on June 22, the parties reaffirmed their commitment to ensuring that refugee returns are voluntary, safe and conducted with dignity, in accordance with international refugee protection standards and the 2010 tripartite agreement.
According to the communiqué, the DRC is currently hosting 196,289 Rwandan refugees and asylum seekers, of whom 75,421 have been officially registered. Rwanda, meanwhile, hosts 84,456 Congolese refugees and asylum seekers.
The parties noted progress in the repatriation of Rwandan refugees, stating that 8,394 Rwandans have voluntarily returned from the DRC since January 2025, including 2,347 during 2026. They agreed to facilitate the return of an additional 10,000 people before the end of the year.
The meeting also confirmed that more than 3,600 Rwandan refugees living in southern South Kivu have expressed their willingness to return home. They are expected to be repatriated through the Kamanyola border post in the DRC and the Bugarama border crossing in Rwanda.
Officials further acknowledged that more than 400 Rwandan refugees remain in temporary transit sites in North and South Kivu awaiting repatriation. Rwanda reiterated its commitment to receiving all citizens who choose to return voluntarily while applying public health measures, including Ebola prevention protocols.
Regarding Congolese refugees living in Rwanda, the parties agreed that voluntary repatriation has not yet begun. The DRC committed to identifying the return routes by October 2026 to facilitate the start of the process.
The three parties also agreed to hold quarterly coordination meetings, either in person or virtually, to monitor implementation of the repatriation program. The first follow-up meeting is scheduled for September 2026.
UNHCR, which participated as the neutral international agency responsible for refugee protection, was commended for its role in supporting the process. The parties pledged to continue working closely with the agency to ensure the implementation of the commitments reached in Addis Ababa.
*Rwanda, DRC Back Immediate Ceasefire in Minembwe
LONDON, June 25, 2026 - Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) have agreed to support an immediate cessation of hostilities in the Minembwe area of South Kivu Province, as fighting continues to threaten civilians and regional stability.
The commitment was among the resolutions adopted during a meeting of the Joint Oversight Committee (JOC) held in London on June 24. The meeting brought together representatives from Rwanda, the DRC, the United States, Togo and the African Union Commission to review the implementation of the Washington Peace Agreement signed last year.
Participants expressed deep concern over the escalating violence in eastern DRC, particularly in Minembwe, citing the humanitarian impact of drone strikes and other attacks that have resulted in civilian deaths, injuries and displacement.
The armed group MRDP-Twirwaneho, which has controlled Minembwe since 2017, and the AFC/M23 coalition have accused Congolese government forces, supported by Burundian troops, the FDLR and Wazalendo militias, of launching sustained attacks on the commune using heavy weapons and drones.
The Congolese military had previously announced that it had captured Minembwe, but the claim was later disputed after government forces withdrew from the area.
AFC/M23 spokesperson Lawrence Kanyuka alleged that government forces used drones to strike Minembwe's main hospital on the evening of June 24, including a children's ward, and accused Kinshasa of committing war crimes against civilians.
The Joint Oversight Committee emphasized the urgent need for a ceasefire to restore peace in eastern DRC and called for an end to hostilities, including drone attacks.
During the meeting, the United States said the DRC presented updates on efforts to dismantle the FDLR armed group, while Rwanda reported on measures related to the withdrawal of its security arrangements in line with previous agreements.
Both Rwanda and the DRC reaffirmed their commitment to implementing the Washington Peace Agreement, including provisions on dismantling the FDLR and addressing Rwanda's security concerns.
The two countries also agreed to work toward an immediate de-escalation of tensions, particularly in Minembwe, and to use their influence with all parties involved to help secure a ceasefire.
In addition, they pledged support for the implementation of the Enhanced Joint Verification Mechanism (EJVM+) and the Joint Security Coordination Mechanism (JSCM), which will facilitate intelligence sharing and oversee implementation of the peace agreement.
The meeting also reaffirmed support for the ongoing Doha peace talks between the DRC government and the AFC/M23 coalition. Although previous agreements on ceasefires and confidence-building measures have faced implementation challenges, participants agreed to continue supporting the negotiations and to refrain from actions or statements that could undermine the peace process.
*FDLR remains committed to destabilizing Rwanda, report says
June 26, 2026 - The Democratic Forces for the Liberation of Rwanda (FDLR), an armed group based in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, continues to pose a security threat to Rwanda more than three decades after the 1994 Genocide against the Tutsi, according to Rwandan officials and intelligence assessments.
The FDLR, whose leadership includes individuals accused of involvement in the 1994 genocide, has long been cited by Rwanda as a source of regional insecurity. Rwandan authorities accuse the group of promoting genocide ideology and hate speech while operating in eastern DRC.
Rwanda also argues that the FDLR's activities have contributed to violence, displacement and persecution affecting Kinyarwanda-speaking Congolese communities, particularly Congolese Tutsi, in eastern DRC. The Congolese government has denied allegations that it supports the group, although Rwanda has repeatedly accused Kinshasa of collaborating with FDLR elements.
According to security sources cited in the report, the FDLR's military wing, FDLR-FOCA, is led by Pacifique Ntawunguka, also known as "General Omega." He succeeded Maj. Gen. Cyprien Uzabakiriho, known by the alias "Ave Maria."
The group's military intelligence (G2) is reportedly headed by Brig. Gen. Sebastien Uwimbabazi, also known as "Nyembo Abdallah," who is said to coordinate FDLR operations in the Masisi and Walikale territories of North Kivu.
Military operations (G3) are reportedly overseen by Brig. Gen. Lucien Nzabanita, while the Special Operations Command (CRAP) is led by Col. Pierre-Célestin Rurakabije, known as "Simba Guillaume."
Intelligence assessments cited in the report estimate that, as of June 2025, the FDLR had between 7,000 and 10,000 fighters operating in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo.
*Former FDLR commander recounts defection and integration into Rwanda's security forces
June 28, 2026 - Assistant Commissioner of Police (ACP) Augustin Kuradupagase has recounted his journey from serving in the former Rwandan Armed Forces (Ex-FAR) and later joining the FDLR insurgency to becoming an officer in Rwanda's security services.
Speaking about his experience, Kuradupagase said he was born in the former Karago Commune and joined the ESM Military Academy in 1991, graduating with the rank of second lieutenant in 1994.
He said he was wounded during fighting as the Rwanda Patriotic Army (RPA) advanced during the 1994 war and later received treatment at Kanombe Military Hospital.
Reflecting on the conflict, Kuradupagase said soldiers in the former government forces were taught to view Tutsi as the enemy, including civilians inside the country.
Following the collapse of the former government, he fled with other soldiers and civilians to what was then Zaire, where former members of the Rwandan army and militia organized armed groups that later became the Democratic Forces for the Liberation of Rwanda (FDLR).
Kuradupagase said he joined the movement and became part of its special operations structures, including the Commando de Recherche et d'Action en Profondeur (CRAP), a special forces unit. He also said he underwent military training linked to Burundi's CNDD-FDD movement before returning to FDLR ranks.
According to Kuradupagase, plans were being made to launch attacks against Rwanda, but military operations by the RPA disrupted those preparations.
In 1997, he said, he entered Rwanda as part of an infiltration mission through the Virunga mountains before reaching his home area, where he reunited with his family.
Kuradupagase said his parents encouraged him to abandon the armed struggle and instead contribute to rebuilding the country. Although he initially intended to continue fighting, he said changing circumstances on the ground and heavy losses among infiltrators led him to reconsider.
He surrendered to the Rwanda Patriotic Army on May 15, 1997, saying he was received without mistreatment and was encouraged to reintegrate into society.
"I am grateful to the Rwandan authorities because the way I was received after surrendering gave me confidence that nothing would happen to me," he said.
Kuradupagase said he subsequently joined Rwanda's security forces, serving with several military units before transferring to the Gendarmerie in January 1998. Following the creation of the Rwanda National Police, he continued his career in law enforcement.
He later attended the National Police College in Musanze and has held several senior positions within the police service, including coordinating security agency operations. He currently serves as a member of the Rwanda National Police Council and Deputy Director in the United Nations Mission Department.
Kuradupagase also expressed pride that his son now serves in the Rwanda Defence Force with the rank of second lieutenant.
He said Rwanda's progress has been made possible by leadership that prioritizes national unity and merit-based opportunities, noting that his son trained at the Royal Military Academy Sandhurst in the United Kingdom, while his daughter completed her studies at a university in Italy.
Conclusion
The period from June 16 to 29, 2026, highlights complex geopolitical shifts, domestic institutional transitions, and localized security challenges within the Great Lakes region. Diplomatically, regional relations remain fluid; while Rwanda and South Africa show promising signs of political reconciliation through structured frameworks, long-standing friction points persist between Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) regarding regional peace architectures and the active dismantling of armed insurgencies such as the FDLR. Domestically, political decentralization efforts and structured discussions on governance highlight a regional emphasis on national unity and state consolidation.
On the economic front, Rwanda demonstrated structural resilience, marked by strong GDP expansion, rising mineral export revenues driven by high global demand, and forward momentum in massive domestic energy infrastructure projects like the Lake Kivu methane gas initiative. These gains are juxtaposed against acute macroeconomic vulnerabilities in neighboring Burundi, where severe foreign exchange and fuel shortages continue to constrain recovery. Concurrently, security dynamics in eastern DRC remain highly volatile. Despite high-level diplomatic agreements facilitating voluntary refugee repatriation and international frameworks pushing for localized ceasefires, sustained combat and conflicting territorial claims around strategic nodes like Minembwe underscore the fragile balance between diplomatic policy and military reality on the ground.
