*Health Experts Warn of Chikungunya Cases Misdiagnosed as Malaria in Rwanda
Kigali, June 8, 2026 - Health experts in Rwanda are raising concerns over increasing challenges in distinguishing Chikungunya from malaria, warning that the mosquito-borne viral disease is often misdiagnosed due to its similar symptoms.
Researchers say Rwanda’s progress in reducing malaria-related deaths has been significant, but the emergence of Chikungunya cases is creating new diagnostic challenges in primary healthcare facilities.
According to Prof. Léon Mutesa, a researcher and lecturer at the University of Rwanda, Chikungunya is transmitted by the Aedes aegypti mosquito and presents symptoms that closely resemble those of malaria, including high fever and body weakness.
He explained that many patients initially treated for malaria may actually be suffering from Chikungunya, leading to delays in appropriate care.
“The problem is that many people bitten by this mosquito develop symptoms similar to malaria. At the hospital, they start treatment as if it were malaria, but that is not the disease,” Prof. Mutesa said.
He noted that studies conducted in Rwanda, including a 2024 survey in the Eastern Province, identified confirmed cases of Chikungunya among patients who were initially suspected of having malaria. Some patients were asymptomatic, while others sought medical attention due to overlapping clinical signs.
Experts say the severity of the disease in East Africa appears lower compared to regions such as West Africa, but misdiagnosis remains a major concern, particularly where diagnostic tools are limited.
Prof. Mutesa emphasized the need for improved laboratory capacity, including PCR testing, which remains limited in many health facilities.
He added that a key distinguishing factor between Chikungunya and malaria is persistent joint pain, which often continues even after malaria treatment is administered. Lack of improvement after two to three days of malaria treatment may indicate Chikungunya infection.
While the disease can be severe if untreated, experts note that most cases are managed symptomatically, and mortality rates remain relatively low. However, misdiagnosis can prolong recovery and complicate patient care.
Dr. Nkeshimana Menelas, Director of Training and Development of Medical Staff at the Ministry of Health, said Chikungunya is currently managed in Rwanda as part of broader viral disease protocols, similar to Ebola and Marburg infections, for which no specific treatment exists.
He highlighted ongoing research efforts, including the ACT-CHIK project led by the University of Rwanda in collaboration with institutions from seven countries. The initiative, launched on May 8, 2026, aims to advance research toward the development of a vaccine for Chikungunya.
Globally, health authorities estimate that around 35 million people are affected by Chikungunya each year, with Africa accounting for a smaller proportion of cases compared to regions such as South America and Asia.
