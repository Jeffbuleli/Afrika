*Washington Peace Agreement Records Limited Progress After One Year
Kinshasa, June 27, 2026 - One year after the signing of the Washington Peace Agreement between the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Rwanda, implementation has remained limited, with the two countries completing less than one-third of their respective commitments, according to an independent assessment.
The agreement, signed on June 27, 2025, was designed to promote peace and stability in the Great Lakes region through the neutralization of armed groups, the withdrawal of foreign forces, enhanced security cooperation, and regional economic integration.
An annual evaluation found that only 35% of the agreement's overall commitments had been implemented during its first year. Of the 30 identified tasks, 22 showed some level of progress, ranging from initial implementation to full completion.
The Democratic Republic of the Congo completed 31.7% of the commitments assigned to it, while Rwanda reached 30.6%. The international partners involved in supporting the agreement-including the United States, Qatar, the African Union, and other stakeholders-recorded the highest implementation rate at 53.5%.
The assessment noted that implementation progressed rapidly during the first two months after the agreement was signed before slowing considerably toward the end of 2025. Progress remained largely stagnant between December 2025 and February 2026 before modest improvements resumed in the following months.
Several factors were cited for the limited progress, including an imbalance in implementation priorities, with greater attention given to institutional and economic measures than key security commitments. Persistent mistrust between the parties also slowed the fulfillment of interdependent obligations.
Operations aimed at neutralizing the FDLR began only in late March 2026, while continued clashes between the Congolese army and the AFC/M23, along with delays in deploying the Expanded Joint Verification Mechanism Plus (EJVM+), further hindered implementation.
The report also highlighted that the worsening humanitarian and security situation in eastern DRC continues to undermine efforts to implement the agreement, despite repeated diplomatic initiatives.
It added that a lack of trust between the parties and insufficient political commitment from some regional actors remain major obstacles for mediators seeking to advance the peace process.
The assessment also noted that the parallel Doha negotiations between the Congolese government and AFC/M23 have made limited progress, with the parties remaining divided on key issues despite several rounds of talks. Continued insecurity and shifting international priorities have further slowed mediation efforts.

Tshilumbayi said the judicial proceedings are intended to complement, rather than undermine, ongoing peace initiatives, arguing that legal action and diplomacy can move forward simultaneously.
He maintained that Rwanda's involvement in acts of aggression against the Democratic Republic of the Congo is no longer a matter of debate, describing it as an established fact that has resulted in significant human, material and economic losses for the country.
According to Tshilumbayi, referring the dispute to the ICJ reflects the DRC's commitment to resolving interstate conflicts through peaceful legal mechanisms provided under international law.
Addressing concerns that the case could complicate the implementation of the Washington Agreement, he said judicial proceedings before the ICJ are fully compatible with diplomatic negotiations aimed at ending the conflict.
"The International Court of Justice is a peaceful mechanism for settling disputes between states. Pursuing this case does not prevent the implementation of the Washington Agreement," he said.
Tshilumbayi also argued that the current legal case is stronger than the DRC's previous attempt before the ICJ in 2002, which was dismissed on jurisdictional grounds.
He explained that procedural obstacles that affected the earlier case have since changed, noting that Rwanda is now party to international conventions containing jurisdictional clauses that allow the Court to examine the allegations raised by the DRC.
To illustrate the government's legal strategy, Tshilumbayi pointed to the ICJ ruling that held Uganda internationally responsible for damages caused during the Six-Day War in Kisangani.
"Uganda was held liable for the damage caused in Kisangani. That is the same legal outcome we are seeking regarding Rwanda in Kivu," he said.
He said the Congolese government is pursuing a dual-track approach that combines military efforts to defend the country's territorial integrity with diplomatic engagement and judicial action before international institutions.
Tshilumbayi stressed that the ICJ is not a criminal tribunal but an international court mandated to resolve disputes between states that recognize its jurisdiction. He added that judicial proceedings before the Court reinforce, rather than weaken, ongoing peace efforts by providing a lawful avenue for resolving international disputes while seeking accountability for the damage allegedly suffered by the DRC.

Conclusion
The period from June 17 to 29, 2026, highlighted the Democratic Republic of the Congo's simultaneous struggle to address security threats, strengthen state institutions, pursue economic transformation, and preserve political stability amid growing domestic and regional tensions.
Peace efforts remained fragile despite sustained diplomatic engagement. The Washington Agreement and the Doha Process continued to provide a framework for negotiations, but implementation remained slow and mutual distrust persisted between Kinshasa and Kigali. Rwanda maintained that any withdrawal of its forces from eastern DRC depends on the neutralization of the FDLR, while the Congolese government intensified its accusations that Rwanda continues to support the AFC/M23 rebellion and benefit from illicit mineral trafficking networks. The United States reinforced this narrative by imposing new sanctions targeting individuals and companies accused of facilitating the illegal trade in Congolese minerals, further increasing diplomatic pressure on Kigali.
Kinshasa simultaneously expanded its legal, military and economic strategy to defend national sovereignty. The government formally brought a case against Rwanda before the International Court of Justice, seeking international accountability for alleged violations of international law committed during decades of conflict in eastern DRC. At the same time, authorities continued efforts to strengthen national defense while advancing an economic agenda centered on greater control over strategic resources. President Félix Tshisekedi reaffirmed that the era of exporting raw minerals without local processing had come to an end, reflecting a broader objective of promoting domestic industrialization, value addition and economic sovereignty.
Economic reforms nevertheless advanced despite persistent insecurity. The International Monetary Fund approved more than US$348 million in new financing after positive reviews of the country's reform programs, while the government accelerated preparations for the launch of the Kinshasa Stock Exchange through a partnership with the International Finance Corporation. Additional investment initiatives-including renewable energy projects in Muanda, discussions on responsible critical mineral supply chains, and continued expansion of major mining projects-illustrated the country's efforts to attract long-term investment and diversify economic opportunities.
Security and humanitarian conditions, however, remained deeply concerning. Fighting involving armed groups continued across eastern DRC, while illegal mining activities expanded into protected areas such as Maiko National Park, threatening biodiversity and fueling insecurity. The Ebola outbreak prompted authorities to suspend mass public gatherings in several provinces, creating new political sensitivities as opposition groups prepared demonstrations against proposed constitutional reforms. At the same time, allegations of arbitrary arrests, prolonged detention of opposition figures without trial, restrictions on political activities, and limitations on freedom of movement continued to fuel concerns about governance, civil liberties and respect for the rule of law.
Domestic political tensions also intensified. Debates over constitutional reform became increasingly polarized, drawing strong opposition from religious leaders, opposition parties and civil society, who warned against any attempt to amend constitutional term limits. Political rhetoric further hardened as senior government and opposition figures exchanged increasingly confrontational accusations, reflecting a broader deterioration of the country's political climate.
Despite these challenges, several developments offered signs of resilience. The national football team's historic qualification for the knockout stage of the FIFA World Cup generated rare nationwide optimism, while continued international financial support and new investment partnerships demonstrated sustained confidence in the country's long-term economic potential.
Overall, the reporting period illustrated a country at a decisive moment. The Democratic Republic of the Congo continues to pursue greater security, justice and economic transformation, but lasting progress will depend on the effective implementation of regional peace commitments, stronger state institutions, improved governance, responsible management of its natural resources, and sustained respect for constitutional principles, human rights and the rule of law.
