*Mali Recognized for Timely Contributions to CIPRES Social Welfare Budget
Geneva, June 10, 2026 - Mali has been recognized among member states fully compliant with financial contributions to the budget of the Inter-African Conference on Social Welfare (CIPRES), ahead of the organization’s 40th ordinary session of the Council of Ministers in Geneva.
CIPRES, which brings together 18 member states and 28 social welfare institutions, is among the few African organizations financed entirely through member contributions. Its funding system is based on a structure combining a fixed and a variable contribution, in accordance with provisions of the CIPRES Treaty.
The fixed component accounts for 75 percent of each member state's total contribution and remains identical for all participating countries, regardless of size or the scale of their social welfare institutions.
Ahead of the ministerial session, the Committee of Experts reported that Mali’s social insurance institutions - the National Health Insurance Fund (CANAM), the Malian Social Security Fund (CMSS), and the National Institute of Social Security (INPS) - are fully up to date with their financial obligations to the organization.
Officials noted that the three institutions have consistently met both the fixed and variable portions of their contributions, reflecting Mali’s compliance with its commitments within the regional social welfare framework.

*JNIM Issues Statement Announcing Rewards for Information on Malian Military Leaders
Bamako, June 6, 2026 - The Jama’at Nasr al-Islam wal Muslimin (JNIM) has issued a statement calling on members of the Malian armed forces and other individuals to provide information on senior military figures, while announcing financial rewards for intelligence leading to their location or neutralization.
In the statement, the group addressed officers, non-commissioned officers, and enlisted personnel of the Malian armed forces, as well as individuals it described as willing to support what it termed a “struggle” for Mali’s sovereignty and stability.
JNIM declared that it would offer substantial financial rewards to anyone providing actionable information regarding the whereabouts of senior state officials, including transitional leader General Assimi Goïta and two of his close associates.
The announced rewards include €2,000,000 for information related to General Goïta, and €1,000,000 each for Colonel Lassina Diallo and General Malik Dicko. The group stated that payments would be made securely and discreetly upon verification of the information provided.
The statement further claimed that current state leaders were under pressure due to developments on the ground and called for actions aimed at weakening the transitional authorities. It also promised confidentiality and protection for individuals who cooperate.
The announcement forms part of a broader pattern of escalating propaganda and psychological pressure tactics used by armed groups operating in Mali, amid ongoing security tensions across the country.

Conclusion: Mali Situation Assessment (June 1-15, 2026)
Executive Summary
The first half of June 2026 reflects a sharply dual trajectory for Mali. On one side, the Transitional Government has intensified efforts to modernize institutions, expand economic partnerships, and deepen diplomatic engagement across multiple regions of the world. On the other, the country remains constrained by a volatile security environment, persistent conflict dynamics in the north and centre, and growing concerns related to civilian protection, governance under pressure, and information warfare.
Integrated Assessment
During this period, Mali pursued a broadly diversified and pragmatic foreign policy, strengthening relations with Western partners such as Canada and Italy, while simultaneously deepening cooperation with regional organizations like UEMOA and engaging strategic partners including Türkiye and China. This multi-vector diplomacy reflects a deliberate effort to secure technical assistance, investment, training, and sectoral expertise across key areas such as health, education, governance, and cultural cooperation.
In parallel, notable progress was recorded in economic modernization and sustainable development initiatives. Regional and bilateral frameworks-such as the UEMOA agricultural white paper, Japan-supported textile innovation project, and World Bank-backed education cooperation-illustrate a structured push toward productivity, industrial transformation, and human capital development. Complementary environmental and climate-focused programmes, including the IAAT initiative, Great Green Wall financing discussions, and the “Planet Gold Mali” project, further demonstrate an increasing integration of ecological sustainability into national development planning.
On the governance front, the state advanced efforts to strengthen institutional performance and accountability. The introduction of Results-Based Management training at the Presidency, the operationalization of mining sector oversight through the CCAM Technical Committee, and Mali’s compliance with CIPRES financial obligations collectively indicate a gradual consolidation of administrative discipline and regulatory functionality. These reforms point toward an emerging performance-oriented public sector architecture, even as implementation capacity remains uneven.
However, these institutional and diplomatic gains stand in sharp contrast to a deteriorating security environment. The period was marked by sustained insurgent activity, competing territorial claims, reported attacks, and intensified propaganda messaging from armed groups such as JNIM and the Islamic State in the Sahel. Allegations of civilian harm during military operations, combined with reported defections and fragmentation within security forces, underscore the fragility of state authority in several regions, particularly in the north and centre of the country. These dynamics continue to impose a heavy humanitarian and political cost, complicating stabilization efforts.
Final Outlook
Looking forward, the trajectory of Mali’s transition will depend on its ability to close the widening gap between institutional ambition and ground-level security realities. While significant progress is being made in diplomacy, governance reform, economic diversification, and environmental resilience, these achievements remain vulnerable without corresponding stabilization of contested territories.
A sustainable path forward will require strengthened civilian protection, improved coordination among national and allied security actors, and deeper engagement with local governance structures, including traditional authorities and community leaders. Ultimately, the durability of Mali’s reform agenda will hinge on whether diplomatic and institutional advances can be translated into tangible security improvements, restored public trust, and long-term stability across all regions of the country.
