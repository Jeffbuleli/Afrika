*Rwandan Community in France Accuses RFI of Giving Platform to FDLR
The Rwandan Community in France (Communauté Rwandaise de France, CRF) has filed a formal complaint against Radio France Internationale (RFI), accusing the broadcaster of providing a platform to members of the FDLR - a group responsible for the 1994 Genocide against the Tutsi - and presenting them as a legitimate political movement.
The complaint, submitted on November 15, 2025, was addressed to ARCOM, France’s audiovisual and digital communications regulator.
According to the CRF, RFI crossed an ethical line after publishing an article on November 10, 2025, headlined: “We will continue to fight until Kigali accepts talks: FDLR said.” The community argues that the article, written by journalist Patient Ligodi, portrays the FDLR as a lightly armed faction operating in eastern DR Congo while downplaying its genocidal background and decades-long record of atrocities.
The complaint, signed by lawyer Me Richard Gisagara, notes that the FDLR has been widely condemned and sanctioned by the United Nations, the United States, and the European Union for crimes committed in both Rwanda and eastern DR Congo over the past three decades. Several of its leaders have been convicted in German courts for their role in genocide and mass violence.
The CRF argues that RFI’s reporting failed to provide essential contextual information, effectively granting respectability to a group internationally recognized as terrorist.
“CRF regrets that RFI, as a state media outlet expected to uphold democratic values, has not fulfilled its responsibility to provide accurate and balanced information,” the organization stated. “Instead, it repeats the group’s statements without verification, giving them a credibility they should not have.”
In its submission, the CRF cited the French law of September 30, 1986, as well as the editorial guidelines of France Médias Monde and RFI’s internal code of conduct, all of which require journalists to report truthful, balanced, and contextualized information. These rules specifically prohibit journalists from acting as mouthpieces for armed groups.
The CRF also linked the timing of the RFI article to political pressure in the Democratic Republic of Congo, recalling that in January 2025 the DRC government threatened foreign media with severe punishment - including the death penalty - for coverage deemed to oppose the state narrative. According to the CRF, this atmosphere has led some outlets to propagate unfiltered pro-government messaging, increasing the spread of anti-Rwandan rhetoric.
“This pressure has pushed some media to relay DRC propaganda without scrutiny,” the organization warned. “Such bias endangers Rwandan voices, who continue to be targeted by hate speech.”
The CRF is now urging ARCOM to investigate the incident and take appropriate action to ensure that RFI complies with professional standards and legal obligations.
In its concluding remarks, the community reaffirmed its commitment to defending historical truth and combating hatred and impunity: “We will continue to do whatever is necessary to preserve a truthful history and protect our community.”
*Rwanda’s Foreign Minister Rejects ICGLR Resolutions on Eastern DRC Conflict
Rwanda’s Minister of Foreign Affairs and International Cooperation, Ambassador Olivier Nduhungirehe, has dismissed the latest resolutions adopted by the International Conference on the Great Lakes Region (ICGLR) concerning the conflict in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC).
The ICGLR summit, held on 15 November 2025 in Kinshasa, saw member states grant President Félix Tshisekedi the mandate to lead the organization. According to DRC’s Minister for Regional Integration, Floribert Anzuluni, the heads of state condemned the M23 rebel group and urged “the aggressor state to completely withdraw its troops from the DRC.”
However, Minister Nduhungirehe said the resolutions were irrelevant and lacked legitimacy, arguing that Rwanda’s absence from the summit rendered the conclusions one-sided and detached from the realities of the conflict.
“The conclusions of the ICGLR meeting held in Kinshasa last week, in the absence of Rwanda - which elected Tshisekedi as the new President of this organization based in Bujumbura - are completely irrelevant to the issues related to the conflict in eastern DRC,” he said.
Nduhungirehe emphasized that the ongoing diplomatic efforts in Washington and Doha are the only meaningful pathways toward resolving the crisis. Talks between Rwanda and the DRC in the United States, along with negotiations between the DRC government and AFC/M23 in Qatar, he said, remain the most credible initiatives.
“For this reason, only the Washington and Doha peace processes, supported by the African Union, truly seek a lasting solution to the conflict in eastern DRC,” the minister noted.
His remarks came as both dialogue tracks reached promising stages. On November 7, Rwanda and the DRC signed a comprehensive economic cooperation agreement. One week later, on November 15, the DRC government and the AFC/M23 agreed on a set of basic principles aimed at paving the way for sustainable peace.
The progress made in Washington and Doha, Nduhungirehe added, offers renewed hope for stability in a region long plagued by cycles of violence.

*“The World Cannot Thrive Without Women,” Says OIF Secretary-General Louise Mushikiwabo
Kigali, Rwanda - Louise Mushikiwabo, Secretary General of the Organization of French-Speaking Countries (OIF), emphasized the critical role of women in global development, stating that the world cannot progress without them. She made the remarks on November 19, 2025, while opening the 46th Meeting of Foreign Ministers of OIF member states in Kigali.
The meeting focused on the role of women in development over the past 30 years, with Mushikiwabo highlighting persistent gender inequalities in many member countries. She stressed that women, who make up half of the world’s population, are essential for uniting families, fostering social cohesion, and driving national progress.
“Have we ever considered the role that women should play in uniting the family so that people can live well together? Rwanda knows this. […] I wonder how we are unable to see far ahead, don’t we often hear in public that the world cannot thrive while ignoring half of its population?” Mushikiwabo said.
Globally, out of 8.06 billion people, more than 4.05 billion are women, and in Rwanda, women constitute over 51.5% of the population, according to the 2022 census.
Mushikiwabo urged ministers from OIF member countries to ensure women are fully integrated into all aspects of social, economic, and political life. She said, “I urge ministers to create a new system of family functioning that fully understands the capabilities of women, including in preventing and resolving conflicts, and in promoting well-being in the extended family and at home. Make no mistake! For women to succeed, they need the cooperation of men. Without this, achievements will remain incomplete.”
The President of the Supreme Court of Rwanda, Domitilla Mukantaganzwa, praised Rwanda’s progress in advancing women’s rights and inclusion, noting that post-genocide reconstruction prioritized empowering women. He highlighted programs promoting gender equality in government, justice, and other sectors, emphasizing the tangible impact of women in development.
“Thanks to these reforms, we have seen the benefits of women’s participation in every aspect of life. In politics, Rwanda leads the world with 63.7% of MPs being women, and in the justice sector, women make up nearly 50%,” Mukantaganzwa said.
He added that women have played a pivotal role in conflict resolution and reconciliation, particularly in post-genocide Rwanda, citing their contributions as mediators in communities across the country. Mukantaganzwa stressed that in many countries, initiatives for women exist only on paper and urged leaders to translate policies into tangible action.
The OIF Ministerial Meeting brought together participants from 65 countries and marks the first high-level OIF gathering hosted by Rwanda since the organization’s establishment.

*Qatar Warns of Deep Mistrust Hindering DRC-AFC/M23 Reconciliation Efforts
Doha - November 2025. Qatar’s Minister of State for Foreign Affairs, Dr. Mohammed bin Abdulaziz Al-Khulaifi, has highlighted the serious challenges facing the reconciliation process between the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) and the AFC/M23 rebel movement, stressing that the talks remain heavily burdened by deep mistrust between the parties.His remarks follow the November 15, 2025, signing of eight basic principles by representatives of the DRC government and the AFC/M23-an agreement reached after months of talks mediated by Qatar and the United States.
In an exclusive interview with Breitbart, Minister Al-Khulaifi described the signing as “a crucial moment” in efforts to restore peace in the Great Lakes region, but warned that progress remains fragile.
“It is a very difficult task… there is a great deal of mistrust between them,” he said. “The word ‘trust’ is not in the vocabulary of the opposing parties.”
Talks Marked by Tension and Ongoing Fighting
According to the minister, negotiations in Doha, which began in March 2025 after discussions between the Emir of Qatar, President Paul Kagame, and President Félix Tshisekedi, often required mediators to separate the delegations to prevent confrontation.
“Sometimes we abandon direct talks and put them in separate rooms,” he noted, underscoring the volatility of the discussions.
He stressed that the biggest challenge is the continuation of fighting in eastern DRC even as negotiations take place:
“This is not only a political conflict. It is an armed conflict… with civilians being killed.”
The minister described intense last-minute negotiations before the November signing, recalling working “until 8:00 a.m.” to refine key lines of the draft text.
Implementation Expected to Be Difficult
Al-Khulaifi cautioned that implementing the principles will be a “100% difficult” task, given the ongoing hostility and the absence of mutual confidence.
He emphasized the step-by-step approach adopted by Qatar and the U.S., noting that six of the eight principles still require detailed negotiations. Two-prisoner releases and cessation of hostilities-were signed earlier in September and October 2025.
Key issues to be discussed include:
Facilitating humanitarian operations
Restoring government institutions and services
Interim security arrangements
Repatriation of refugees and IDPs
Economic recovery in conflict-affected areas
Reconciliation and justice
Unlike previous mediation attempts, the Doha talks integrate economic measures as a pillar for long-term stability-an approach the minister called “unique and essential.”
Strong Support, But No Guarantees
The process has received strong backing from the United States, the African Union, and other international partners-support that Minister Al-Khulaifi says enhances the credibility of the initiative. Still, he cautioned:
“Will it be difficult to implement? Absolutely. Will we face challenges? 100%. But we remain committed, step by step, toward lasting peace.”
Initially, Qatar expected a comprehensive peace agreement in early August, following the July 19 adoption of the principles framework. The deal did not materialize. Under the new timeline agreed on November 15, a final agreement will be signed once both sides negotiate and validate the implementation details of the remaining six principles.

*USA: Banyamulenge Community Urges Washington to Intervene in Ongoing Attacks in Eastern DRC
The Banyamulenge community in the United States is appealing to American authorities to intervene and protect their relatives who remain under attack in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). The request was published in an open letter by the Mahoro Peace Association, addressed to top U.S. officials including the Secretary of State and members of Congress such as Marco Rubio.
According to the organization’s leader, Douglas Kabunda, the Banyamulenge living in the mountainous areas of Fizi, Uvira, and Mwenga have faced continuous attacks since 2017, reportedly carried out by the DRC national army (FARDC).
Kabunda stated that Banyamulenge-populated areas in South Kivu are encircled by more than 58 military bases, where soldiers allegedly prevent civilians from accessing markets, healthcare, and humanitarian assistance.
He added that houses and crops have been burned, and drone strikes by FARDC and Burundian forces continue to hit civilian zones as of November 2025, despite the Doha ceasefire agreement.
Kabunda also recalled that on November 12, the spokesperson of the Burundian military, Brig. Gen. Gaspard Baratuza, publicly admitted the blockade of Minembwe, accusing its inhabitants of collaborating with armed groups. Kabunda warned that failing to distinguish civilians from combatants constitutes serious violations of international law, including war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide, given that the Banyamulenge are a targeted ethnic group.
The Mahoro Peace Association is urging the U.S. government to pressure for an end to the blockade, the reopening of supply routes, and the withdrawal of Burundian forces from eastern DRC. The organization is also calling for an independent international commission to investigate alleged war crimes, crimes against humanity, and acts of genocide in the region.
Kabunda noted that military officers from Burundi and the DRC involved in the siege and starvation of Banyamulenge communities could face sanctions, including asset freezes and travel bans to the United States.
From November 23 to 28, members of the Banyamulenge diaspora in the United States are expected to hold protests to amplify their appeal to U.S. leaders.
*Rwanda Signs Cross-Border Agreement to Protect Migratory Cranes
The Rwanda Wildlife Conservation Association (RWCA) has signed cooperation agreements with four conservation organizations in Burundi, Tanzania, and Uganda to strengthen the protection of migratory cranes that regularly cross into these countries. The agreements were concluded in Kigali on November 21, 2025, during RWCA’s celebration of its 10th anniversary.
Representatives from partner organizations-including Nature Uganda, the International Crane Foundation (Uganda), Nature Tanzania, and the Association Burundaise pour la Protection de la Nature-joined RWCA in formalizing a regional conservation framework. The collaboration will focus on safeguarding 24,904 square kilometers of shared wetland ecosystems.
A joint survey conducted this year revealed the scale of wetlands requiring protection:
1,030 wetlands in Rwanda (82% of the national total),
787 wetlands in Uganda,
266 in Tanzania,
19 in Burundi,
all forming part of interconnected ecosystems frequented by migratory cranes and other wetland species.
RWCA Director General and founder Dr. Olivier Nsengimana noted that cranes and other species are increasingly crossing borders, particularly from wetlands near Akagera, Akanyaru, and Kagitumba. Many do not return, raising concerns about their safety on the other side, especially in wetlands affected by agriculture.
“Some of the cranes equipped with GPS trackers move into neighboring countries, which is why cooperation with local institutions is essential,”Dr. Nsengimana explained, adding that the agreements will facilitate information sharing, community awareness, and joint monitoring.
He emphasized that tracking wildlife across borders comes with logistical barriers-including permits and travel restrictions-making regional collaboration the most practical solution.
Reflecting on RWCA’s first decade, Dr. Nsengimana highlighted significant progress: Rwanda’s wild crane population has risen from fewer than 300 in 2015 to 1,245 today, supported by a conservation team that has grown from five volunteers to 300 permanent staff.
Fidèle Bingwa, Permanent Secretary in the Ministry of Environment, commended RWCA’s impact, noting that its consistent results have encouraged the government to deepen its commitment to wildlife protection.
*Peace Talks in Doha, Prisons in Kinshasa: AFC/M23 Challenges the DRC’s Security Leadership
The AFC/M23 coalition, which is battling the Government of the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), has questioned what it calls a glaring contradiction in the country’s leadership-pursuing peace talks in Doha while expanding prisons and detaining senior officers in Kinshasa under opaque circumstances.
In recent weeks, several high-ranking FARDC officers - including generals and colonels - have been held for alleged collaboration with the enemy, treason, embezzlement, and attempting to harm the Head of State. The arrests, made without public judicial proceedings, have intensified concerns about due process and political instability.
Among those detained are Gen Christian Tshiwewe, former Chief of Staff of the army and security advisor to President Félix Tshisekedi; Gen Franck Ntumba, former head of military operations at the Presidential Palace; and Brig Gen Benjamin Katende Batubadila, former intelligence chief of the General Staff Guard. Others include John Tshibangu, Commander of the Fourth Military Region, Lt Gen Pacifique Masunzu, and Eric Ruhorimbere Ruhanga.
FARDC spokesperson Gen Maj Sylvain Ekenge Bomusa confirmed on November 22 that several officers have been detained for months, insisting investigations into “serious crimes” were still ongoing.
But AFC/M23 says the explanation is insufficient. In its statement, the group described it as “incomprehensible” that while the government is negotiating peace abroad, it is “building prisons in Kinshasa to detain people illegally.”
The movement accused the government of using these arrests as a political smokescreen, arguing that the generals are victims of injustice and that the official FARDC communication aims to divert public attention.
The detentions have sparked widespread outrage and prompted the Chairman of the AFC/M23 Forum, Corneille Nangaa, to publicly question what he calls the “death of leadership” in Kinshasa.
“How can a civilian detain generals in Kinshasa,” he asked, “while other generals wait their turn for arrest?”
In a message shared via X, Nangaa added:
“In Kinshasa, we are no longer led by leadership. We are led by prison and fear. Twenty FARDC generals are held in Ndolo Military Prison, in inhumane conditions. Two have died, and their bodies have not been recovered. Others have disappeared.”
He accused the government of continuing to add names to an “illegal detention list,” calling it a strategy of intimidation rather than governance.
According to AFC/M23, while the Doha discussions focused on Congolese unity, justice, military cohesion, forgiveness, and transparent governance, the situation in Kinshasa reflects the opposite - marked by arbitrary arrests, military distrust, and a politics of fear.
The group asserts that the Doha process cannot be effective while people “are being arrested without reason” in the DRC.
The movement further warns that the persistent arrests have weakened the army:
“When a regime routinely arrests generals without transparent trials or clear reasons, trust collapses. Generals begin to suspect one another; soldiers begin to doubt. Respect is replaced by fear, and the military becomes fragile.”
A divided and intimidated army, they argue, risks becoming “weak, manipulable, and a source of internal and regional conflict.”
Language-based Discrimination Allegations
AFC/M23 also raised alarms over what it describes as the targeting of Swahili-speaking officers, a claim the government denies. The group argues that such discrimination, if proven, could have dangerous consequences, recalling the DRC’s long history of division driven by language and identity.
They warned that bringing these divisions into the army is “reckless and short-sighted,” adding that “no regime survives long while mistreating its own army.”
AFC/M23 concluded by saying that unless the government becomes transparent, ends secret detentions, and rebuilds trust within the military, the DRC will continue to project a false image - “looking good in front of the cameras while the army weakens behind the scenes.”

Historical Context: Belgium’s Colonial Extraction
European involvement in Congo’s mineral exploitation dates back to the colonial era. Between 1885 and 1960, Belgium extracted enormous quantities of diamonds, gold, copper, ivory, and other valuable resources during its rule over what was then known as the Congo Free State and later the Belgian Congo.
Kagame said this history undermines the credibility of European states when they point fingers at Rwanda today.

Kagame revealed that Rwanda had engaged in extensive verification processes, in collaboration with European partners, to certify the origin of minerals exported from Rwanda.

Kagame criticized what he sees as a pattern of repeated accusations that eventually shape international opinion.
He said: “Some Congolese sell minerals through Rwanda, Burundi, Uganda or other neighbors. But when it comes to precious stones, they always speak about Rwanda because they have heard these accusations many times.”
Rwanda’s Mineral Industry: A Growing Sector
Figures for 2024/2025 show that Rwanda earned $1.7 billion from mineral exports, driven largely by the expansion of professional mining and value-addition industries.Kagame said these reforms aim to shift Rwanda from raw exports toward processing and refining.
Kagame Questions International Silence on FDLR’s Role in Mineral Smuggling
The president also criticized what he described as the international community’s lack of attention to the role of the Democratic Forces for the Liberation of Rwanda (FDLR)-a militia operating in eastern DRC since the 1994 Genocide against the Tutsi.
“Why Is the FDLR Being Acquitted?”
Kagame said that for years, the FDLR has controlled parts of Goma and surrounding areas-regions central to today’s conflict-but has not been scrutinized regarding mineral trafficking.
He asked: “Why is the FDLR being acquitted when we report on it every day? They say it is small. If they think it is small, they should come and speak with those recently repatriated from the DRC. They will give you information.”
FDLR’s Estimated Size and Mineral Activity
It is estimated that between 7,000 and 10,000 FDLR fighters remain active, including those integrated within FARDC units, those working with local militias supported by the government, and independent cells.
Kagame stated that some of these fighters are involved in mining activities in eastern Congo:“Where do they sell the minerals? It seems they end up in Europe, and then Rwanda is blamed.”
Europe’s Double Standards
The president suggested that European narratives often shape global opinion, regardless of facts: “Sometimes they exaggerate. People think what they say is the truth, yet they are the ones who have played a major role in this issue.”

“When I heard about the coup in Guinea-Bissau, I still don’t know what happened,” he said.“When I heard it, I thought someone carried out the coup himself. I didn’t think it was a real coup. I still need more time to confirm it.”
The president added that the increasing number of coups on the continent suggests widespread structural issues that have long been ignored.
Coups as a Symptom of Deeper Problems
Kagame argued that in most cases, coups do not occur spontaneously. “Where coups are happening, there is something wrong,” he said. According to him, at least 90% of coups emerge from grievances that leaders or their international partners have refused to acknowledge.
He criticized what he described as a pattern in which Western countries publicly praise certain leaders for years, only to shift positions when crises erupt. “These coups happen by telling these people that you have been lying to us for some time,” he said.

In one of the more striking parts of his remarks, Kagame distinguished between what he called “good” and “bad” coups.
He defined a bad coup as one driven by opportunism or military arrogance: “Someone who thinks that because he has a gun he can overthrow the government-that is bad.”
However, he said there are situations where a coup may be a response to entrenched corruption or abuse: “If a person or a group says: enough is enough, this person has lied to us, has stolen from us, has exploited us-and they act-then I think I have no problem with it.”

Kagame also commented on recent waves of youth-led protests in Kenya, Madagascar, Tanzania and other regions, where demonstrators have taken to the streets against corruption, mismanagement, or social inequality.
While acknowledging that some methods used by protesters may be misguided, he said leaders must not ignore the underlying grievances.“Before they do that, it is our responsibility to do something so that they have no reason to do so,” he emphasized.
Kagame stressed the importance of opening channels of dialogue with young people and ensuring that governance is transparent and responsive. “Every country has problems, and citizens may not get what they want when they want it,” he said, adding that it is the responsibility of leaders to explain decisions clearly and build trust.

*FDLR Ex-Leader Reveals Group’s Ties to Minerals and DRC Military Support
Kigali, November 28, 2025 - Lt Col Mpakaniye Emilien, alias Che Guevara Jacob, a former leader in the Democratic Forces for the Liberation of Rwanda (FDLR), has confirmed the role of both the FDLR and the Congolese army in mineral exploitation and the ongoing conflict in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). Collaboration with the DRC Army
Mpakaniye asserted that the FDLR’s continued existence depends on support from the DRC government. He claimed that the FARDC (DRC army) provides weapons and ammunition to the group, both for operations in eastern DRC and for attacks against Rwanda.
“If the DRC government did not work with the FDLR, the FDLR would cease to exist,” he said. “The FARDC says the FDLR is helping fight the enemy, but they are also providing resources to attack Rwanda.”
He stressed that this information is not based solely on his personal experience but is corroborated by multiple reports, including investigations by United Nations experts.

After voluntarily returning to Rwanda, Mpakaniye described a dramatic change in perspective. Upon arriving at the Mutobo rehabilitation camp, he realized that the FDLR’s justification for attacking Rwanda was based on false narratives.
“I thought the FDLR had a legitimate reason to fight Rwanda, but after returning home, I saw that the group lied to us. Rwanda welcomed us, gave us resources, and helped us start a new life,” he said.

Mpakaniye’s testimony underscores the complex interplay between rebel groups, state actors, and the mineral economy in the eastern DRC, highlighting the challenges of regional security and the efforts Rwanda has undertaken to rehabilitate former combatants peacefully.

Conclusion
The second half of November 2025 highlights a stark contrast between Rwanda’s internal trajectory of economic modernization and diplomatic expansion, and the volatile security crisis escalating on its western border.
1. Regional Instability and Security Dilemmas The security situation in the Great Lakes region remains the primary challenge. Despite ongoing mediation efforts in Doha and Washington, distrust persists between the DRC and Rwanda. The conflict in Eastern DRC has intensified, with the AFC/M23 coalition making significant territorial gains in North and South Kivu, drawing in Burundian troops and engaging FARDC and Wazalendo forces.
Diplomatic Stance: Rwanda has adopted a firm posture, with Foreign Minister Nduhungirehe rejecting recent ICGLR resolutions as "irrelevant" and President Kagame issuing strong warnings regarding the security of Goma.
Mineral Dispute: The President also strongly rebuked European nations, accusing them of historical and ongoing looting of DRC minerals while scapegoating Rwanda.
Repatriation: On a positive note, efforts to demobilize FDLR fighters have yielded results, with over 500 Rwandans, including former combatants, returning home from the DRC during this period.
2. Diplomatic Leadership and Global Influence Kigali cemented its status as a diplomatic hub by hosting high-level summits.
Francophonie: The hosting of the 46th OIF Ministerial Meeting underscored Rwanda's influence within the Francophone world, with a specific focus on women's role in development.
Pan-Africanism: The African Renaissance Retreat, led by President Kagame and Aliko Dangote, brought together global leaders to discuss self-driven African solutions.
New Partnerships: Diplomatic ties were strengthened through new ambassadorships in Singapore and Lithuania , and the opening of a Swiss Embassy in Kigali.
3. Economic Resilience and Infrastructure Despite regional trade disruptions caused by post-election unrest in Tanzania, Rwanda’s economy shows resilience.
Trade Growth: The UAE and DRC have emerged as the top export destinations, with total exports reaching Rwf 3,955.5 billion.
Development: Key infrastructure projects, such as the Rubengera-Nyange road, were completed , and significant investments were made in public health, including a new Rwf 30 billion laboratory for zoonotic diseases.
4. Governance and Social Regulation Internally, the government maintained strict regulatory oversight.
Accountability: The Rwanda Governance Board closed over 9,000 non-compliant places of worship to ensure safety and proper governance.
Anti-Corruption: A crackdown on corruption saw arrests within the FDA and brewery sectors , aligning with AU recognition of Rwanda’s strong anti-corruption standing.
In summary, while Rwanda continues to advance its "Vision 2050" through aggressive economic diversification and strategic diplomacy, the deteriorating war in the Eastern DRC and the associated geopolitical fallout pose a critical threat that dominated the national agenda in late November 2025.
